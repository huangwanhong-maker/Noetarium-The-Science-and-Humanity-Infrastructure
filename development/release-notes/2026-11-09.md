# Release Notes: 9 November 2026

## 1. What People Pay For

In this update the Urban Science Workbench measures what people value in the places they live, in what they pay and in what they say ([ADR 0088](../decisions/0088-what-people-pay-for.md)).

- **What a home's price pays for.** Choose a city whose home sales the data library holds: Brooklyn's in 2025 to begin.
  - Each home is coloured by how much more or less it sold for than its rooms, land, age and place predict.
  - The card gives what the subway, a park, a protected lane and the centre add to a price, three ways: plain least squares, and two models that allow for neighbouring prices moving together. The tests choose between them.
- **What it found.** Neighbouring prices move together strongly.
  - **Protected lanes.** A plain regression credits a lane within 300 m with +21% to +25% of a home's price; the spatial models with +4% to +11%. Much of what looks like the lane's premium belongs to the neighbourhoods the lanes were built in.
  - **The subway.** Its effect shrinks the same way.
- **A survey about a street.** Set what a lane, trees and a few minutes might be worth against a monthly levy. The workbench designs the question cards and says how many people must answer for each value to be known within a few dollars: about seven for a lane within ±$5 a month at plausible values. The cards can be downloaded to field the survey.
- **Built to be reused.** The models are in the foundations, the city's records in the data library: another city is another record. Five pictures are in the gallery.

## 2. What a Change to the Streets Did, There and Around It

In this update the Urban Science Workbench can show what a change to a city's streets did where it was made and in the streets about it, for any city whose record of changes and counts the data library holds ([ADR 0087](../decisions/0087-what-a-change-did-and-around-it.md)).

- **Choose a city.** Melbourne's major buildings (2010–2019) with the walkers its counters saw, or New York's protected cycle lanes (2013–2026) with the riders its counters saw.
- **What one sees.** The changes appear with the clock. Each counter is coloured by how its counts changed when a change first came near it, along the streets, against the counters not yet reached. The card gives the effect at the change and around it, by rings of distance, two ways, period by period. It also says what the record can tell: how many counters were counting before their change came.
- **What it found.**
  - In Melbourne, no effect of a major building on the walkers near it can be seen.
  - In New York, the riders on a new protected lane may double, but the record cannot establish it: most of its counters began counting after their lane was built.
  - A record that wants to tell what a change did must count before the change, beside it and a little away from it.
- **Built to be reused.** The same code serves both cities and any other a case adds, and any other kind of count: the estimators in peira, the streets' distances in urban science, the records in the data library, the marks in the visualization layer. Seven pictures are in the gallery.
- **Works better.** Streets read from the world's tiles are joined where the tiles had cut them apart, so distances along them are right.

## 3. Who Can Reach Care, Schools and Shops, and Who Is Left Without

In this update the Urban Science Workbench answers, anywhere on Earth, who can reach the services they need and who cannot ([ADR 0086](../decisions/0086-access-to-services-and-unmet-need.md)).

- **Pick a place, choose a service and a way of travelling.** Choose care, schools or shops, on foot or by road, within 10, 15 or 30 minutes. The people about the place come from the global population grid, about 1 km a cell. The services are the buildings the map calls hospitals, clinics, schools or shops, each offering its floor space. The roads and paths are those drawn there.
- **What one sees.** Each cell is coloured by its share of what lies within reach, a service's space divided among everyone who can reach it. The cells lie on the ground and follow the hills, and those with nothing within reach are hatched. A card gives the share of people within reach, the people with nothing, those far below the average, how unequal access is, and the share of rural people within 2 km of a main road.
- **What it found.**
  - In Melbourne's centre, 55% of people reach mapped care within 15 minutes on foot, and everyone does by road.
  - In Freetown, 37% reach it on foot, with a thirty-fifth of Melbourne's mapped floor space of care per person.
  - In the hills about Dhulikhel, 99% reach care within half an hour by road.
  - What these numbers show first is how much of each city's care is on the map. Six pictures are in the gallery.
- **Works better.** A place is no longer stranded on a stub of footpath when travelling by road, and people beyond the area loaded are counted as left out, not as without.

## 4. Quasi-Experiments, Causal Graphs and Cohorts in peira; Demography on Them

In this update peira takes its second stage and begins its third, and demography becomes the first domain built on it ([ADR 0085](../decisions/0085-demography-on-peira.md)).

- **Methods moved where they belong.** Panels went from nomos to tyche, and instruments and differences in differences to peira, so that a clinical trial or an urban study reaches them without the social sciences' library. The economics toolbox in the Economy Atlas and the Urban Science Workbench reads them from their new places.
- **Effects when assignment was not controlled.**
  - Treatments adopted at different times, estimated group by group and period by period, where the usual two-way regression misleads (shown: 1.94 against a true 2.43).
  - The effect on compliers with an instrument.
  - Regression discontinuity, sharp and fuzzy.
  - Synthetic control, with placebos.
- **Causal graphs.** Which independences a graph implies and which sets identify an effect, held to linear models in 4,032 cases.
- **Cohorts.** Person-time on the Lexis diagram, exact rates and standardisation.
- **Demography.** Life tables, reproduction rates, projection by the Leslie matrix and the stable population it tends to, each checked against what theory says exactly. For example, a population all in one age group takes the stable structure and grows by its dominant eigenvalue.
