# Part E. Philosophy of Inquiry: Structure of Theories, Evolution of Science, Understanding and Dependence

## 1. Scope and Method

This part supplements the survey of inquiry with AI by opening the classical sources on theory structure, the evolutionary and social description of science, questions and personal knowledge, and understanding, testimony and epistemic dependence. The search was made on 20 November 2026 (the sources were opened on that date) with web search and fetch.

Sources used:

- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP) entries, opened as secondary sources: *Imre Lakatos*, *Thomas Kuhn* (A. Bird, rev. 2025), *Evolutionary Epistemology* (M. Bradie and W. Harms, rev. 2020), *Scientific Progress* (I. Niiniluoto, rev. 2024), *Karl Popper*, *Charles Sanders Peirce*, *John Dewey*, *R. G. Collingwood*, *Hans-Georg Gadamer* (J. Malpas and N. Keane, rev. 2026), *The Social Dimensions of Scientific Knowledge*, *Epistemological Problems of Testimony* (N. Leonard). Where the fetched text did not show an author, none is asserted here.
- Crossref records for articles (DOI, venue, volume, pages) and Open Library records for books (publisher, first year).
- arXiv and PubMed Central pages for recent papers.
- Search-result summaries for some positions, named as such.

Marks:

- **[V]** the record was opened and authors, title, year and venue or publisher match what is written. A [V] mark certifies the bibliographic record only; the source of each statement of position is named in the text.
- **[P]** found only through a secondary source, or a field unconfirmed (the field is stated).
- **[U]** not opened. No work is marked [U].

Caveats stated once. Pages and chapter ranges are given only where a record showed them. Page numbers and quotations from the primary texts are not given, since no primary text was read. Several publishers' pages refused automated access (the University of Chicago Press page for Hills 2009 returned 403; Nature's page redirected to a login), so abstracts were not read in those cases. The searches contained no personal or project names.

## 2. Annotated Works

### 2.1 Structural Accounts of Theories

**Lakatos, 1970 essay.** [P]
Lakatos, I. (1970). "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes." In I. Lakatos and A. Musgrave (eds.), *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. (Open Library records the volume, Cambridge University Press, 1970; the essay's page range and the title wording were taken from the SEP bibliography and are not confirmed against the volume.)
Position, per the SEP entry *Imre Lakatos*: a research programme has a hard core of theses that the negative heuristic forbids testing by modus tollens, and a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses adjusted to deflect refutation. The positive heuristic is a partly articulated plan for developing refutable variants of the belt. A programme is theoretically progressive if each theory has excess content, empirically progressive if some of it is corroborated, and degenerating otherwise.
Bearing: supports idea 2 (a compact core with heuristics for extension, kept distinct from a developed body) in structure, and gives a usable criterion for retaining or dropping a line of work. Limit: the core is protected by methodological decision, which in the design is a human act (idea 1); Lakatos's criterion judges a programme over time and in retrospect, and gives no rule for the moment of acceptance. The SEP entry also reports the standing objection that the progressive/degenerating line can be drawn only after the fact.

**Lakatos, 1978 volume.** [V]
Lakatos, I. (1978). *The Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes: Philosophical Papers, Volume 1*, ed. J. Worrall and G. Currie. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press. (Open Library record confirms author, title, 1978, Cambridge University Press; editors and subtitle are from the SEP's reference to "Philosophical Papers" and my recollection, and are not confirmed by the record I opened.)
Position: the collected statement of the same methodology, with the case studies. Same position as above.
Bearing: as above; useful as the citable book for the hard core and heuristics.

**Kuhn.** [P]
Kuhn, T. S. (1970). *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, 2nd ed., enlarged, with the Postscript. Chicago: University of Chicago Press. (Open Library confirms the work, first published 1962, University of Chicago Press; the second edition's year and the Postscript are from the SEP entry *Thomas Kuhn*, by A. Bird, which cites it as 1962/1970a.)
Position, per the SEP: normal science is puzzle-solving within a disciplinary matrix of shared beliefs, values, instruments, techniques and metaphysical commitments. Exemplars, the concrete achievements that suggest puzzles and serve as the standard for solutions, are one component; Kuhn called them in the Postscript the "most novel and least understood" aspect of the book (SEP's quotation of his remark). Scientists judge new work by its similarity to exemplars, not by explicit rules.
Bearing: supports idea 2 directly, since the generative core as commitments, primitives and exemplars corresponds to the matrix and its exemplars, and the point that exemplars are learned by use and not by rule bears on what an agent can be given. Limit: Kuhn's matrix is a communal, largely tacit possession acquired through training; a written "core" document does not by itself constitute one. Kuhn also holds that anomalies are normally set aside, which is a reason not to treat an agent's counterexample search as self-sufficient.

**Laudan.** [V]
Laudan, L. (1977). *Progress and Its Problems: Toward a Theory of Scientific Growth*. London: Routledge and Kegan Paul (Berkeley: University of California Press). (Open Library lists both imprints for 1977; the subtitle is from the SEP bibliography.)
Position, per the SEP entry *Scientific Progress*: the unit of appraisal is the research tradition; progress is measured by problem-solving effectiveness, counting solved empirical problems and subtracting anomalies and conceptual problems. The same entry notes the difficulty of individuating and counting problems.
Bearing: supports idea 3 by giving a non-truth-directed measure of how well a lineage is doing, and bears on idea 2 through conceptual problems (internal tensions in the core), which agents could detect. Limit: counting problems is contested, so a score computed by agents would not be neutral; the choice of what counts as a problem is a form of asking the question (idea 1).

### 2.2 Evolutionary Accounts of Science and the Social Organisation of Inquiry

**Campbell, 1960.** [V]
Campbell, D. T. (1960). "Blind variation and selective retentions in creative thought as in other knowledge processes." *Psychological Review*, 67(6), 380–400. DOI 10.1037/h0040373. (The record gives "retentions"; the brief's "retention" is a slip in the title.)
Position, per the SEP *Evolutionary Epistemology*: knowledge-gaining processes, creative thought included, work by variation that is blind to its outcomes and by selective retention. (The SEP page gives the 1960 citation and the theme; the paper itself was not read.)
Bearing: supports idea 3 as a historical source for the variation-and-retention frame. Limit: "blind" is the claim at issue. Campbell's later 1974b title (below) shows he moved to "unjustified" variation, so the design should not cite him for the claim that useful variation is random.

**Campbell, 1974a and 1974b.** [P]
Campbell, D. T. (1974a). "Evolutionary Epistemology." In P. A. Schilpp (ed.), *The Philosophy of Karl R. Popper*, pp. 412–463. La Salle, IL: Open Court.
Campbell, D. T. (1974b). "Unjustified Variation and Selective Retention in Scientific Discovery." In F. J. Ayala and T. Dobzhansky (eds.), *Studies in the Philosophy of Biology*, pp. 139–161. London: Macmillan.
Both entries are from the SEP bibliography only; the books were not opened. The SEP credits the 1974a paper with coining "evolutionary epistemology". Bearing: as for 1960; the shift in wording is the useful point.

**Toulmin.** [V]
Toulmin, S. (1972). *Human Understanding: The Collective Use and Evolution of Concepts*. Princeton: Princeton University Press. (Open Library confirms author, title, 1972, Princeton University Press and Clarendon Press; the subtitle is from the SEP.)
Position, per the SEP *Evolutionary Epistemology*: concepts evolve within disciplines, and standards of rationality are local and themselves evolve. The SEP remarks that this seems to require giving up goal-directedness in inquiry, a consequence few accept.
Bearing: supports idea 3 and, through "disciplinary populations of concepts", the idea that lineages are the units. Limit: Toulmin's view puts the standard of acceptance inside the community's evolving practice; it cannot be cited for an agent-run selection with no accountable community.

**Hull.** [V]
Hull, D. L. (1988). *Science as a Process: An Evolutionary Account of the Social and Conceptual Development of Science*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press. (Open Library confirms author, title, year, publisher; the subtitle is from the SEP.)
Position: conceptual lineages are selected through the use and credit scientists give one another. The SEP *Social Dimensions of Scientific Knowledge* reports Hull's claim that reputational stakes give scientists strong reason not to cheat, and answers that this works only if detection is reliable, citing cold fusion and the Burt and Wakefield cases.
Bearing: supports idea 3 most directly (credit and use as the currency). Limit: Hull's mechanism assumes that the people being credited are the ones who bear the risks of error, and that detection of fraud is dependable; with agent-generated output both assumptions need new support. Credit that flows to authors is why authorship (idea 1) matters to the mechanism.

**Kitcher, 1990.** [V]
Kitcher, P. (1990). "The Division of Cognitive Labor." *The Journal of Philosophy*, 87(1), 5–. DOI 10.2307/2026796. (The Crossref record gives the first page only; the last page is not confirmed.)
Position: not read; the SEP *Social Dimensions* entry did not discuss the 1990 paper. The related account of the 1993 book is given below.
Bearing: see Kitcher 1993.

**Kitcher, 1993.** [V]
Kitcher, P. (1993). *The Advancement of Science: Science without Legend, Objectivity without Illusions*. New York: Oxford University Press. (Open Library confirms author, title and Oxford University Press, 1993; the subtitle is from my recollection.)
Position, per the SEP: a researcher's decision to pursue a maverick strategy is modelled as a bet depending on its chance of success, the numbers pursuing each approach and the expected reward; a community can balance orthodox and maverick researchers even if individual motives are impure.
Bearing: supports idea 3's claim that a community gains from a division of labour among rival approaches. Limit: it is a model of rational individuals choosing strategies; it is silent on whether a proposing machine should be treated as a strategy-chooser, and its allocation depends on parameters (chances of success) that are assumed.

**Strevens, 2003.** [V]
Strevens, M. (2003). "The Role of the Priority Rule in Science." *The Journal of Philosophy*, 100(2), 55–79. DOI 10.5840/jphil2003100224.
Position, per the SEP: credit goes to the first to discover, so extending another's work earns little; this pushes researchers toward novel problems and strategies and produces a division of labour.
Bearing: supports idea 3 and bears on authorship (idea 1): what is distributed is credit, and a design in which agents propose and the person accepts must say who holds priority. Limit: it is an account of incentives among scientists, and does not show that the resulting division is optimal.

**Zollman, 2007.** [V]
Zollman, K. J. S. (2007). "The Communication Structure of Epistemic Communities." *Philosophy of Science*, 74(5), 574–587. DOI 10.1086/525605.
Position, per the SEP *Social Dimensions* (following Bala and Goyal 1998): in network models, sparse communication reaches the correct hypothesis more often though more slowly, while dense communication has a non-negligible chance of locking in on a wrong one. The SEP notes that the models do not explain how a community would select or enforce a structure.
Bearing: supports idea 3 (a measure of restraint on information flow among rival approaches keeps diversity). Limit: see the critique below.

**Zollman, 2010.** [V]
Zollman, K. J. S. "The Epistemic Benefit of Transient Diversity." *Erkenntnis*, 72(1), 17–35. DOI 10.1007/s10670-009-9194-6. (The Crossref record carries 2009 as its date, which is the online date; the print year 2010 given in the brief is not confirmed by the record I opened.)
Position, per the SEP: applies the same models to the division of labour, with the premature acceptance of the acid hypothesis of ulcers as illustration; the diversity that helps is transient.
Bearing: supports idea 3, and its word "transient" qualifies it, since a population that never converges is not an advantage.

**Rosenstock, Bruner and O'Connor, 2017.** [V]
Rosenstock, S., Bruner, J., and O'Connor, C. (2017). "In Epistemic Networks, Is Less Really More?" *Philosophy of Science*, 84(2), 234–252. DOI 10.1086/690717.
Position, per the SEP: Zollman's result is sensitive to parameters; changing the number of nodes or the disparity between the alternatives can remove it, and with more nodes or greater disparity the chance of consensus on the wrong hypothesis in dense networks falls close to zero.
Bearing: limits idea 3: the benefit of restricted communication is not robust, so the design should not state it as a general finding.

**Popper.** [V]
Popper, K. R. (1963). *Conjectures and Refutations: The Growth of Scientific Knowledge*. London: Routledge and Kegan Paul. (Open Library confirms author, title, 1963, Routledge and Kegan Paul; the subtitle is from the SEP, which lists the book as Routledge.)
Position, per the SEP *Karl Popper*: science grows by bold conjecture and attempted refutation; a theory that survives severe tests is corroborated, not verified; testing ends in a decision of the research community to accept certain basic statements, compared to a jury verdict that can be revised. The SEP notes the criticism that auxiliary hypotheses can protect high-level theories, and that Popper later conceded that falsifiability alone does not demarcate science.
Bearing: supports the agents' roles in idea 1 (proposal, counterexample search) and the person's acceptance as a decision. Limit: Popper gives no account of where problems come from; his method does not make the question a product of the method.

### 2.3 Inquiry, Questions and Personal Knowledge

**Peirce.** [P]
Peirce, C. S. *Collected Papers of Charles Sanders Peirce*, vols. 1–6 ed. C. Hartshorne and P. Weiss, vols. 7–8 ed. A. W. Burks. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1931–1958. (Open Library records the Collected Papers, first published 1931, Harvard University Press and Belknap Press; volume editors and the end date are from my recollection and are not confirmed.)
Position, per the SEP *Charles Sanders Peirce* (the author is not shown in the fetched text): abduction is the first phase of method, the making of an explanatory conjecture because of a surprising phenomenon, never a necessary inference and tested afterwards by deduction and induction; the method is public and self-correcting, so inquirers beginning from different points converge, and the point of convergence is what Peirce means by truth; at every stage inquirers are fallible. The excerpt I read did not use the phrase "community of inquiry". The Collected Papers numbers cited in that excerpt (CP 2.408, 2.3, 2.32, 6.25) do not concern abduction or the community, and I could not confirm the standard passages from an opened source; none is given here.
Bearing: supports idea 1 (agents may propose abductive conjectures; testing and acceptance belong to the process) and idea 3 (inquiry as community convergence). Limit: Peirce's community is open-ended and impersonal; it does not reserve a role to a single inquirer.

**Dewey.** [V]
Dewey, J. (1938). *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry*. New York: Henry Holt and Company. (Open Library confirms author, title, 1938, Henry Holt; later in *The Later Works*, vol. 12, per the SEP.)
Position, per the SEP *John Dewey*: inquiry begins in a felt unsettlement, formulates a problem, constructs and reasons out a hypothesis and tests it by action, and ends in turning an indeterminate situation into a determinate one; the pattern loops back and is not linear.
Bearing: supports idea 1: the problem is constituted by the inquirer's situation, so asking the question cannot be delegated without moving the situation. Limit: Dewey describes inquiry in general; he does not say who must be the inquirer.

**Collingwood.** [V]
Collingwood, R. G. (1939). *An Autobiography*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. (Open Library confirms author, title, 1939, Oxford University Press.)
Position, per the SEP *R. G. Collingwood* (which I read only in part and which does not show the author): propositions are answers to questions; presuppositions give rise to questions and are not themselves true or false; a statement's role, not its content, makes it one or the other. The SEP's page describes the *Autobiography* as an account of his work; I did not confirm from it the chapter on question and answer.
Bearing: supports idea 1 and idea 2: the person's commitments are presuppositions that fix which questions arise, which is the case for keeping the asking of questions and the setting of commitments with the person. Limit: it concerns the logic of questions, not who may ask them.

**Gadamer.** [P]
Gadamer, H.-G. (1989). *Truth and Method*, 2nd rev. ed., trans. J. Weinsheimer and D. G. Marshall. New York: Crossroad. (From the SEP bibliography; German original *Wahrheit und Methode*. The book record was not opened.)
Position, per the SEP *Hans-Georg Gadamer* (J. Malpas and N. Keane): the priority of the question in the structure of understanding comes from Platonic dialectic and from Collingwood; prejudgments open up what is to be understood and can themselves be revised. The entry does not set out the logic of question and answer in detail.
Bearing: supports idea 4 loosely: understanding is an event of the one who understands, and begins in the question. Limit: brief; the SEP gives little detail and no claim about artificial agents.

**Polanyi, Personal Knowledge.** [V]
Polanyi, M. (1958). *Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy*. Chicago: University of Chicago Press; London: Routledge and Kegan Paul. (Open Library confirms author, title, 1958 and these publishers; the subtitle is from my recollection.)
Position, per search-result summaries (no SEP entry was found for Polanyi): all knowing involves personal participation (indwelling), and knowledge is rooted in tacitly held elements. These summaries, not the book, are the source.
Bearing: supports idea 4 and idea 1: commitment and judgment are the knower's. Limit: the claim that a person's tacit skill cannot be fully written down also qualifies idea 2, since a written core is never the whole of what a scholar holds.

**Polanyi, The Tacit Dimension.** [V]
Polanyi, M. (1966). *The Tacit Dimension*. Garden City, NY: Doubleday; London: Routledge and Kegan Paul. (Open Library confirms 1966, with a 1967 Anchor paperback.)
Position, per the same search summaries: "we can know more than we can tell" is the starting point.
Bearing: as above; it warns that a record of an inquiry, however complete, cannot carry over the whole of the understanding.

### 2.4 Understanding, Testimony and Epistemic Dependence

**Hills, 2009.** [V]
Hills, A. (2009). "Moral Testimony and Moral Epistemology." *Ethics*, 120(1), 94–127. DOI 10.1086/648610.
Position: record opened; the abstract was not read (publisher page returned 403). From my knowledge of the paper, and so unconfirmed here: moral testimony is a poor way to acquire moral understanding, and moral understanding, not mere moral knowledge, is what one should aim at. The 2016 paper below, in a secondary summary, extends the point to understanding generally.
Bearing: supports idea 4 for the moral case. Limit: the paper is on moral testimony, and extends to science only by analogy.

**Hills, 2016.** [V]
Hills, A. (2016). "Understanding Why." *Noûs*, 50(4), 661–688. DOI 10.1111/nous.12092. (The Crossref record gives 2015 as the online date; 2016 is the issue year.)
Position, per search-result secondary sources (the paper itself not read): understanding why p is a kind of intellectual know-how, distinct from knowledge that p and knowledge why p; it tolerates epistemic luck in a way that knowledge does not; one secondary source reports Hills, with Pritchard, as holding that understanding cannot be transmitted by testimony, while Sliwa and Boyd are reported as denying it.
Bearing: supports idea 4 and shows it is contested: whether testimony can transmit understanding is disputed in the literature. A designer who claims that understanding cannot be handed over should say which side of this dispute is taken.

**Zagzebski.** [V]
Zagzebski, L. T. (2012). *Epistemic Authority: A Theory of Trust, Authority, and Autonomy in Belief*. New York: Oxford University Press. (Open Library confirms author, title, 2012, Oxford University Press; the subtitle is from my recollection.)
Position, per search-result secondary sources: a preemption thesis, that an authority's belief that p gives me a reason to believe p that replaces my other reasons instead of adding to them; critics (McMyler, Keren, others) argue that the analogy with Raz's practical authority fails and that preemption may unhinge beliefs from good reasons already held.
Bearing: mixed. It supports the legitimacy of deference to a reliable authority (a counter-position to idea 4's emphasis on self-achievement), while the critics' point that preemption undercuts understanding supports it. The book itself was not read.

**Pritchard.** [P]
Pritchard, D., Millar, A., and Haddock, A. (2010). *The Nature and Value of Knowledge: Three Investigations*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. (Pritchard's part is "Knowledge and Understanding"; publisher and year from university repository records surfaced by search, 2010, ISBN 978-0-19-958626-4; the book itself was not opened.)
Position, per those records and an NDPR review noted in them: Pritchard argues that the finally valuable cognitive state is understanding, not knowledge, understanding being a cognitive achievement. A secondary source quoted above reports him as holding that understanding cannot be transmitted by testimony.
Bearing: supports idea 4 directly. Limit: the record I read is a summary; the exact claim about testimony should be checked in the text before it is cited.

**Elgin.** [V]
Elgin, C. Z. (2017). *True Enough*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. (Open Library confirms author, title, 2017, MIT Press.)
Position, per search-result summaries (publisher listing, an NDPR review and others): models and idealisations known to be inaccurate can convey understanding ("felicitous falsehoods"); the standard is "true enough" for the purpose at hand; understanding extends to art and fiction. Reviewers dispute the vagueness of the standard.
Bearing: qualifies ideas 2 and 4 in a useful way: understanding is not a stock of true propositions, so a core of commitments and exemplars is not a store of truths; it is also a reason to ask agents for models, as long as the person judges fitness for purpose.

**Lackey.** [P]
Lackey, J. (2008). *Learning from Words: Testimony as a Source of Knowledge*. Oxford: Oxford University Press. (From the SEP bibliography, *Epistemological Problems of Testimony*, by N. Leonard; the book was not opened.)
Position, per the SEP: against the transmission view, testimony can generate knowledge and justification (the Creationist Teacher and Persistent Believer cases). The SEP does not state Goldberg's position on generation directly; it cites his 2010 *Relying on Others* and his 2005 unsafe-testimony paper, which support the generation side.
Bearing: limits idea 4: that testimony can generate knowledge in the hearer does not show that it can generate understanding; this literature concerns knowledge, and a designer should not draw on it for understanding without argument.

**Hardwig, 1985.** [V]
Hardwig, J. (1985). "Epistemic Dependence." *The Journal of Philosophy*, 82(7), 335–349. DOI 10.2307/2026523. (The Crossref record gives the first page only; the page range is from my recollection.)
Position, per search-result summaries of the paper and its critics: much of what we know rests on trust in experts, and insisting on autonomy would leave one with uninformed, untested and so irrational beliefs; the dependence holds among experts in different specialties as well as between expert and layperson; it poses a dilemma, either only the community knows such propositions, or individuals know them vicariously without justification of their own. Critics (Almassi; Goldman) dispute the claim that the believer has no evidence of her own.
Bearing: the main counter-position to idea 4 and a limit on idea 1. It shows that rational inquiry may rest on others and that scholars rationally accept results they cannot check. A fair design should grant this for results taken from agents or the literature; the claim that remains available is narrower, namely that the dependence does not extend to the interpretation and acceptance of what enters one's own theory, a distinction Hardwig's paper does not draw.

**Hardwig, 1991.** [V]
Hardwig, J. (1991). "The Role of Trust in Knowledge." *The Journal of Philosophy*, 88(12), 693–708. DOI 10.2307/2027007. (Crossref gives the first page only; the page range is from my recollection.)
Position: the record was opened; the paper was not. I do not state its argument beyond its title and its place as the companion to the 1985 paper.
Bearing: the same counter-position; to be read before it is characterised in the paper.

**Wimsatt.** [P]
Wimsatt, W. C. (1981). "Robustness, Reliability, and Overdetermination." In M. B. Brewer and B. E. Collins (eds.), *Scientific Inquiry and the Social Sciences*. San Francisco: Jossey-Bass. Reprinted in *Re-Engineering Philosophy for Limited Beings* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2007), pp. 43–74; and in *Characterizing the Robustness of Science*, Boston Studies in the Philosophy of Science (Dordrecht: Springer, 2012), pp. 61–87, DOI 10.1007/978-94-007-2759-5_2. (Crossref records the two reprints; the 1981 original's venue and publisher are from my recollection and were not opened. The paper was not read.)
Position: from my knowledge, unconfirmed here: a result is robust when it is invariant across independent means of determination, and robustness is a criterion for treating a result as real.
Bearing: supports a design practice, not a claim: agents that reach the same result by independent routes give more reason to trust it than one agent's repeated output. Limit: agents built on one model family are not independent routes.

### 2.5 Recent Work on AI and Understanding

**Krenn et al., 2022.** [V]
Krenn, M., Pollice, R., Guo, S. Y., Aldeghi, M., Cervera-Lierta, A., Friederich, P., dos Passos Gomes, G., Häse, F., Jinich, A., Nigam, A., Yao, Z., and Aspuru-Guzik, A. (2022). "On scientific understanding with artificial intelligence." *Nature Reviews Physics*, 4(12), 761–769. DOI 10.1038/s42254-022-00518-3. (Opened on PubMed Central.)
Position: three roles for AI in understanding: a computational microscope, a source of inspiration that humans develop, and a possible "agent of understanding" that itself gains insight; in the first two the human turns AI output into understanding, and the third has not been demonstrated. Following de Regt and Dieks, understanding is recognising a theory's qualitative consequences without exact calculation, and the authors propose a "scientific understanding test" that asks whether an AI can transfer its understanding to human experts.
Bearing: supports idea 1 (agents as microscope and inspiration, the person as the one who understands) and gives a test relevant to idea 4. Limit: it is a perspective by scientists, not a philosophical argument, and it leaves open that an agent may one day understand.

**Beckmann and Queloz, 2025.** [V]
Beckmann, P., and Queloz, M. (2025). "Mechanistic Indicators of Understanding in Large Language Models." arXiv:2507.08017 (v1 July 2025, revised February 2026). Preprint; no journal reference was listed.
Position: mechanistic interpretability findings make the view of models as mere pattern imitators increasingly untenable; the authors give a three-tier account (conceptual, state-of-the-world, principled understanding) tied to levels of computational organisation, and say machine understanding differs from human cognition, since models use heterogeneous mechanisms in parallel.
Bearing: qualifies idea 4: it is a serious position that models may have some understanding, so the design should not rest on the claim that agents have none. The design's claim can be that the person's understanding must be the person's, whatever the agents have.

**Messeri and Crockett, 2024.** [P]
Messeri, L., and Crockett, M. J. (2024). "Artificial intelligence and illusions of understanding in scientific research." *Nature*, 627(8002), 49–58. DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0. (The Crossref record was opened; the article page redirected to a login, so the argument was not read and is not summarised.)
Bearing: the title bears directly on idea 4; to be read before any claim is attributed to it.

**Ting, Curtis-Trudel and Yao, 2026.** [P]
Ting, Y.-S., Curtis-Trudel, A., and Yao, S. (2026). "What Understanding Means in AI-Laden Astronomy." arXiv:2601.10038 (v1 January 2026, v2 April 2026). The arXiv page lists *Nature Astronomy* (2026), DOI 10.1038/s41550-026-02809-6, as the journal reference; I did not open the journal's page.
Position, per the arXiv abstract: AI integration in astronomy is an epistemological question; the authors identify five tensions (understanding requires more than prediction, problem-finding is distinct from pattern recognition, research priorities may drift toward what AI makes easy, among others) and propose "pragmatic understanding", treating AI as a tool that extends human cognition while needing new norms of validation.
Bearing: supports ideas 1 and 4 for the working scientist; the point that priorities drift toward what AI makes easy is a risk for idea 3's selection by use. Limit: a case study of one field, by philosophers and an astronomer.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Four Acts Reserved to the Person (Idea 1)

Supported by: Dewey and Collingwood, for whom the question and the situation belong to the inquirer; Popper and Peirce, for whom conjecture and test are distinct steps and acceptance is a decision of the inquirer or community; Krenn et al. and Ting et al., who place the human in the role of turning machine output into understanding.

Qualified or denied by: Hardwig, who shows that rational inquiry already accepts results from others without checking them; Zagzebski's preemption thesis, on which deference to a reliable authority can be rational; Beckmann and Queloz, who leave open that models understand something.

Not to be claimed on this authority: that no inquiry may rely on others (Hardwig denies it), that the reservation is a theorem of philosophy (it is a design choice, whose reasons are partly Hills, Pritchard and Polanyi on understanding), or that the four acts are the right four (no source here argues for exactly this list).

### 3.2 A Compact Generative Core Distinct from the Developed Body (Idea 2)

Supported by: Lakatos's hard core, protective belt and heuristics; Kuhn's exemplars and disciplinary matrix; Laudan's research tradition with its conceptual problems.

Qualified by: Kuhn and Polanyi, for whom the core is largely tacit, learned through training and use, and so is not fully written down; Elgin, for whom what carries understanding need not be true propositions; Lakatos's critics, for whom the core/belt line can be drawn only in retrospect.

Not to be claimed: that these authors endorse a written core as a working document. They describe what a community or a scholar is found to hold. The design's core is an instrument that a person may keep, and its fit to Lakatos and Kuhn is an analogy.

### 3.3 Evolutionary Description and Division of Labour (Idea 3)

Supported by: Campbell (variation and retention), Toulmin (evolving concepts), Hull (lineages, credit and use), Kitcher (rational allocation among strategies), Strevens (priority gives incentives to differ), Zollman (transient diversity).

Qualified or denied by: Rosenstock, Bruner and O'Connor, who show that Zollman's benefit of restricted communication is not robust; Campbell's own move from "blind" to "unjustified" variation; Toulmin's view that this seems to give up goal-directedness; Hull's reliance on dependable detection of cheating, which the SEP entry questions.

Not to be claimed: that selection by credit and use tracks truth (Laudan deliberately avoids truth as an aim; Hull's mechanism assumes reliable detection), that variation among agents is blind or independent (agents on one model are correlated; Wimsatt's robustness needs independent routes), that sparse communication among agents is better (the result is parameter-sensitive), or that a community "benefits" from rival approaches in general (Kitcher and Zollman state conditions). The analogies are descriptive of science; none is evidence about a population of agents.

### 3.4 Understanding Must Be Achieved by the One Who Understands (Idea 4)

Supported by: Pritchard (understanding as a cognitive achievement, with a secondary report that it is not transmitted by testimony), Hills (understanding why as know-how; a secondary report that it is not transmitted; Hills 2009 on moral testimony, unconfirmed here), Polanyi (personal participation), Gadamer (understanding begins in the question), Krenn et al. (the human turns output into understanding).

Qualified or denied by: Hardwig and Zagzebski, who defend rational dependence on others and on authority; Lackey, whose case that testimony can generate knowledge keeps open what testimony can do; Sliwa and Boyd, as reported by a secondary source, who deny that testimony cannot transmit understanding; Beckmann and Queloz, who make machine understanding a live question; Elgin, for whom understanding does not require truth, so that it is not a thing that is simply possessed.

Not to be claimed: that the literature agrees that understanding cannot be given by testimony (it is disputed), that Hardwig's and Lackey's work on knowledge settles anything about understanding, or that agents cannot understand. What is available is a narrower claim: a person who has not worked through a result has not understood it, whoever supplied it, and a design may require the work to be done by the person.
