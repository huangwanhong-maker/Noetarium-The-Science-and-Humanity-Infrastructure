# Learning Programmes, Municipal Finance, Ordinal Scorecards and Land Value Capture: Their Design, Measurement and Evaluation, and the Records and Computations They Ask of an Infrastructure. A Survey

*A survey of four practices of city government and of what each asks of a computational infrastructure. Knowledge-exchange and learning programmes for city officials are designed as a declared results chain (goal, institutional challenge, change objective, agents of change, intermediate outcomes, instruments), yet their results are mostly captured by self-report at fixed windows, and the best-documented evaluation finds that satisfaction is near universal while workplace change follows only about half the time. Municipal and subnational finances are assessed by a handful of ratio families whose names hide incompatible definitions, by multi-year projections from which debt capacity is solved, by agency scorecards that combine computed ratios with declared judgements, and by statutory schemes that turn two to eight ratios into a classification with legal consequences. Scorecards of cities and governments score on ordinal scales, and some of their aggregations (PEFA's averaging table, percentage scores) carry an assumption of equal spacing that their weakest-link and threshold rules do not. Land value capture and land readjustment rest on accounting identities that can be checked exactly, but the "capture rates" reported for them are three different quantities, and uplift is rarely measured against a counterfactual. In every field the scheme is declared and versioned, the record is evidence of uneven standing, and the result is credible only when the counterfactual and the judge are named.*

Status: `draft` (a survey for design, 2026-11-22). Activity: research, in service of use. It serves the design of modules that record and compute knowledge-exchange programmes, municipal finances, ordinal scorecards and land value capture on the infrastructure's foundations (peira, tyche, nomos, tekmerion, kybernesis, archeion, kategoria) and domains (economics, policy, urban science). No decision record has yet been written for those modules.

## 0. Scope, Method and Markers

### 0.1 The Question

The survey answers four questions, and for each asks what it means for an infrastructure that records and computes the practice:

1. how knowledge-exchange and learning programmes for cities are designed, and how their results are captured (Part I);
2. how municipal and subnational finances are assessed: ratios, multi-year cash-flow projection, debt capacity, shadow credit ratings, statutory early-warning schemes, debt sustainability, and fiscal capacity and effort (Part II);
3. how scorecards of cities and governments are scored and aggregated on ordinal scales (Part III);
4. how land value capture and land readjustment are designed, estimated and evaluated (Part IV).

*Remark.* Three distinctions run through all four Parts and organise the synthesis (§5).

- **A declared scheme against a computation.** A results framework, a ratio's definition and threshold, a scorecard's levels and aggregation rule, or a statute's share and consent threshold is someone's declaration, with an author, a version and a period of validity. What is computed from it is only as good as the declaration is current.
- **A nominal quantity against an effective one.** A statutory share of uplift, a scorecard's average or a programme's "approval rate" is not the quantity it is often read as: the effective capture of uplift, a meaningful comparison of performance, or a change that the programme caused.
- **A claim against its substantiation.** Each field reports results whose standing depends on who made the claim, against what counterfactual, and who else checked it.

### 0.4 Corrections and Discrepancies Found While Assembling

**Part I, learning programmes.**

1. **Mayne's conditions are second hand.** The four conditions and six steps of Mayne (2008) are [S], quoted by TASO, because ILAC Brief 16 returned HTTP 403. The later seven-step list (Mayne 2019c) and the five criteria of Mayne (2011), which add "disproving alternative explanations", are [V] through Nielsen and Lemire's review. The "reasonable person" wording of Mayne (2001) is [M] (Part I §2).
2. **Three outcome typologies, not one.** CDRF 2009 has six learning outcomes, WBI 2011 six intermediate capacity outcomes and AoKE 2015 five intermediate outcomes [V]; the crosswalk between them is lossy [M]. The CDRF also numbers its figure inconsistently (Figure 2.4 in the text, Figure 2.1 in the list of figures) and words the list differently in its Annex 5 [V] (Part I §1).
3. **URBACT's approval rate rests on respondents.** Plans approved rose from 21% at closure to 88% three years later "of the cities that answered" [V]. The evidence is before-and-after self-report with no comparison group, and the evaluation concedes that content and quality were not studied [V]; its "almost nine-fold increase" is its own attribution (Part I §3).
4. **AoKE's table of instrument strengths is expert judgement.** The guide cites no empirical basis for it [V], and the reading that each row partitions all ten instruments comes from an interleaved layout (Part I §3).

**Part II, municipal finance.**

5. **Brazil's caps conflict between sources.** The World Bank handbook says Brazilian municipalities may borrow below 60% of operating revenue in debt stock or 15% in debt service [V as what the handbook says]. Senate Resolution 40/2001 caps net consolidated debt at 1.2 times net current revenue for municipalities [S], and the researcher recalls Resolution 43/2001's debt-service limit as 11.5% [M]. The handbook's 60% matches CAPAG's grade-A debt band rather than the legal cap; its figures are treated as unreliable (Part II §5).
6. **Mexico's ranges differ by source and year.** Indicator III's lower limit for states is 7.5% in one source and 8 in the IMF note; the municipal ranges differ by year [S]; the official municipal methodology could not be fetched (Part II §5).
7. **Colombia's earlier threshold is secondary.** The current stock threshold of 100% of current revenue is [V] (Ley 358 art. 6 as amended by Ley 2155 de 2021). The earlier 80%, the three-zone *semáforo* and the abolition of its yellow zone in 2003 are [S]. Part II's closing section wrote the change as "80% → 100% in 2021 [V]"; strictly, only the new value is verified, and §6.2 says so (Part II §5).
8. **The MFSA dashboard was read from extracted text.** The pairing of the fiscal-autonomy benchmarks (> 80%, < 70%) with their ratios is the researcher's reading of a rotated table [M], and several MFSA benchmarks have no stated source [M] (Part II §1).
9. **The agencies' methods are known in part.** Moody's 2018 republication may have changed the printed weights [M]; its sub-factor thresholds and its BCA matrix are not printed in the credit opinion; S&P's numeric cut-offs are in a Guidance not retrieved; Fitch's outcome matrix was garbled in extraction; Fitch's synthetic coverage is defined from a snippet [S], and its notional tenor of 15 years is [M] (Part II §4). New South Wales's benchmarks came through text the IMF note's extraction garbled [S] (Part II §2).
10. **The closed forms are derived.** The debt-capacity formula L_max, the lender's coverage floor of 1.2–1.5, debt dynamics on a revenue denominator, the stochastic analysis of local debt and the formulas of the representative tax system are the researcher's statements of standard method [M]; no primary source was found that states them for local governments (Part II §3, §6, §7).

**Part III, scorecards.**

11. **The UNDRR Scorecard's counts and text contain errors.** The preliminary level's reference codes give 46 questions against the 47 the document states [V, with a caveat]; one detailed criterion's level 0 is printed without its "not" [V]. An encoding must be checked against the Scorecard's Excel tool (Part III §2).
12. **The averaging table's interval assumption is computed, not stated.** That PEFA's M2 table is a step function of the mean of equally spaced codes is the researcher's computation on the transcribed table [V, computed]; the 2016 Framework says only "approximate average" [V]. PEFA's own numeric conversion is known from a snippet [S] (Part III §1, §3).
13. **The theorems of ordinal aggregation are from memory.** Stevens (1946), Roberts (1979), Aczél and Roberts (1989), Orlov (1981), Ovchinnikov (1996), Marichal (2002) and Ebert and Welsch's result for ordinal, non-comparable variables are [M] and must be read before they are cited in a theory note; Krippendorff's α and its conventional thresholds come from an encyclopaedia [S] (Part III §3, §4).

**Part IV, value capture.**

14. **The Republic of Korea's consent threshold is given twice.** The Compendium's general section gives a simple majority (51%) for Colombia and Korea; its Korea profile gives half of owners and two thirds of land area for privately led projects [V both]; they probably concern different types of project [M] (Part IV §1).
15. **A Korean table does not add up.** In the Myeonmok project the public-land decrease is given as 203,758 m², while the listed areas differ by 201,559 m², a gap of 2,199 m² the source does not explain [V] (Part IV §2).
16. **Gujarat's durations disagree with their own tables.** The text says 86% of schemes in Gujarat and 97% in Ahmedabad took more than ten years; the tables give 79.9% (115 of 144) and 94.1% (32 of 34) among schemes with data [V for counts; arithmetic the researcher's] (Part IV §2).
17. **A definition contradicts its arithmetic.** De Souza's prose defines the value ratio y as the previous price over the posterior, while the arithmetic uses y = e/a, the posterior over the previous [V] (Part IV §2).
18. **The FHWA states a TIF district's life three ways:** 15–20, 15–30 and 25–30 years [V] (Part IV §3).
19. **The rail meta-analysis exists in two versions.** The working paper gives a premium within a quarter mile of 6.2% residential and 15.2% commercial [V]; the published abstract gives about 4.2% and 16.4% [S]; only the working paper was read (Part IV §5).
20. **Unverified figures are fenced off.** Mohammad et al.'s comparisons came from an AI-written wiki and are not to be used; Chinese Taipei's 45% cap is [M]; the England figure of about £7bn is [S]; the incidence of Dade County's impact fees is from an abstract [S]; and "only 23% of countries" base levy totals on value increments allowed several responses, so its complement is not exactly "cost" (Part IV §1, §2, §4, §6).

### 1. The Stages of Such Programmes and the Exact Lists of the CDRF, the Art of Knowledge Exchange, Kirkpatrick and Phillips
#### Takeaway
The World Bank's two guides give a stage model that can be encoded directly. CDRF has 4 stages and 10 steps; AoKE has 5 steps and 15 sub-steps. Both rest on one chain: development goal → institutional challenge (three capacity factors or areas) → change objective → change agents → intermediate or learning outcomes → instruments and activities → follow-up and measurement. The three capacity factors are stable across versions, but their names and indicator lists differ. The outcome typology exists in **three versions** that a schema must keep apart:
- CDRF 2009: six learning outcomes;
- WBI 2011: six "intermediate capacity outcomes";
- AoKE 2015: five intermediate outcomes.

Kirkpatrick's four levels and Phillips's fifth level (ROI) are defined on their owners' sites. CDRF itself warns that Kirkpatrick Level 2 (tested learning) does not apply to consensus, networks, policy formulation or implementation.

#### Cited Findings

**1.1 CDRF (Otoo, Agapitova and Behrens, WBI, June 2009): definitions**
- **[V] Definition of capacity development:** "Capacity development is a locally driven process of learning by leaders, coalitions and other agents of change that brings about changes in sociopolitical, policy-related, and organizational factors to enhance local ownership for and the effectiveness and efficiency of efforts to achieve a development goal." (p. 3) — [CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf) (OKR handle 10986/23037, CC BY 3.0 IGO)
- **[V] The three capacity factors**, exactly as the source names them (p. 11):
  - "Conduciveness of the sociopolitical environment, made up of the political and social forces that determine the priority given to the development goal by the government, the private sector, and civil society."
  - "Efficiency of policy instruments, or the formal mechanisms to be used to guide stakeholder actions toward achievement of the development goal. Those formal mechanisms include administrative rules, laws, regulations, and standards."
  - "Effectiveness of organizational arrangements, or the systems, rules of action, processes, personnel, and other resources that government and non-government stakeholders bring together to achieve development goals."
  - [CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf)
- **[V] Indicators must be monotone and measurable.** "The indicators are defined so that the greater the amount or extent of the indicator, the more favorable the capacity factor will be to achieving the development goal." Indicators "may be customized to particular situations but should always remain measurable." (pp. 4, 12) — [CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf)
- **[V] Two levels of measurement.** "Learning outcomes measure change at the level of the agent (whether individual or group of individuals)." "Capacity indicators measure whether the actions taken by the agent of change after learning have a favorable impact on the larger system that conditions the achievement of the development goal." (p. 10) — [CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf)

  2. Enhanced skills
  3. Improved consensus/teamwork

- **[V] Generic learning objectives per outcome** (Table 2.4, p. 18; the PDF layout is interleaved, and this is my reading of it):
  1. **Raised awareness:** participant understanding of an issue or situation improved; participant attitude improved; participant confidence improved; participant motivation improved.
  2. **Enhanced skills:** new skills/knowledge acquired; new skills/knowledge applied.
  3. **Improved consensus/teamwork:** discussion initiated/resumed/activated; participatory process initiated/expanded; consensus reached; action steps/plan formulated/improved; collaboration increased/improved.

**1.4 CDRF 2009: the programme cycle, 4 stages and 10 steps (table of contents and pp. 22–40) [V]**
- **Stage 1, Identification and needs assessment**
  - Step 1: Validate the development goal.
  - Step 2: Assess capacity factors relevant to the development goal.
  - Step 3: Decide which changes in capacity factors can be facilitated by learning.
- **Stage 2, Program design**
  - Step 4: Specify objective(s) of capacity development program in the form of capacity indicators targeted for change.
  - Step 5: Identify agents of change and envision change process.
  - Step 6: Set intended learning outcomes and their indicators.
  - Step 7: Design activities.
- **Stage 3, Implementation and monitoring**
  - Step 8: Monitor learning outcomes; adjust program as necessary.
  - Step 9: Monitor targeted capacity indicators and the progress toward the development goal, and adjust program as necessary.
- **Stage 4, Completion and assessment**

**1.11 AoKE 2015: instruments (Table 5, p. 43) [V]**

**1.13 Kirkpatrick's four levels (Kirkpatrick Partners' current statement)**
- **[V] Level 1 Reaction:** "measures the degree to which the target audience finds an experience or initiative favorable, engaging, relevant", "and supportive of the work needed to achieve the targeted outcomes"; "Most importantly at this level, is the measurement of relevancy."
- **[V] Level 2 Learning:** "measures the degree to which the target audience acquires the intended knowledge, skills, attitude, confidence", with components "knowledge, skills, attitude, confidence, and commitment".
- **[V] Level 3 Behavior:** "measures the degree to which the target audience performs the critical behaviors in its environment". "Components of Level 3 include critical behaviors, performance support, and the performance environment."
- **[V] Level 4 Results:** "measures the degree to which targeted organizational outcomes occur as a result of an experience or initiative", by "identifying indicators (both leading and lagging)".
- **[V] Planning runs in reverse:** "it is critical to start with Level 4: Results", "planning from Level 4 to Level 1".
- Source: [Kirkpatrick Partners](https://www.kirkpatrickpartners.com/the-kirkpatrick-model/)
- **[M]** The "required drivers" and "New World Kirkpatrick Model" terms come from J.D. and W.K. Kirkpatrick (2016), *Kirkpatrick's Four Levels of Training Evaluation*. The page I read does not use them. The original four levels are Kirkpatrick (1959; book 1994).
- **[V] CDRF on Kirkpatrick** (Annex 5 table, p. 94):
  - Level 1 is "Not expensive"; it "can provide little information on actual learning or behavioral change."
  - Level 2 computes "the learning gain by deducting the average class pre-test score from the average class post-test score", costs "Moderate", and "requires about one week of course content experts' time and one week of assistants' time".
  - Level 2 is "not relevant to facilitating consensus and teamwork, formulating policies and strategies, implementing strategies/plans, or fostering networks."
  - Source: [CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf)
- **[M]** Meta-analytic critique: Alliger, Tannenbaum, Bennett, Traver and Shotland (1997, *Personnel Psychology*) found that affective reactions correlate weakly with learning and transfer, and that "utility" reactions correlate somewhat better. The levels are therefore not a causal hierarchy. I recall these figures and did not check them here.

**1.14 Phillips's ROI Methodology (ROI Institute)**
- **[V] Levels:**
  - Level 1 Reaction and Planned Action: "Measures participant satisfaction with the program and captures planned action".
  - Level 2 Learning: "Measures changes in knowledge and skills".
  - Level 3 Application and Implementation: "Measures changes in behavior and specific actions on the job to make the program successful".
  - Level 4 Business Impact: "Measures changes in business impact measures".
  - Level 5 ROI: "Compares the monetary benefits to the costs".
- **[V] Formulas:** BCR = Program Benefits ÷ Program Costs; ROI (%) = Net Program Benefits ÷ Program Costs (× 100 implied).
- **[V] Six process steps:**
  1. "Identify the improvements in impact measures."
  2. "Isolate the amount of improvement to the program."
  3. "Convert the improvements to monetary value."
  4. "Tabulate the fully loaded costs of the program."
  5. "Identify the intangible benefits linked to the program."
  6. "Compare the monetary benefits to the cost in an ROI calculation."
- **[V]** The method keeps "a conservative approach" and "a fully loaded cost profile".
- Source: [ROI Institute](https://roiinstitute.net/roi-methodology/)
- **[M]** Phillips's published guiding principles are not on that page. They include:
  - a Level 0 (inputs);
  - participants' estimates of the share of improvement due to the programme, adjusted downward by their stated confidence;
  - first-year benefits only for short programmes;
  - missing data counted as no improvement.

- **[M] One results chain underlies both guides:** goal → capacity factor/area (closed set of 3) → indicator or characteristic (closed sets of 6/8/6 in 2009, 5/8/6 in 2011) → change objective (free text, typed by the challenge category) → change agent → intermediate outcome (closed set, 5 or 6 by version) → type of progress (closed sub-list) → indicator (threshold, time window, evidence method) → instrument (closed set of 10) → activity (closed set in 4 classes).
- **[M] Three outcome typologies must stay distinct.** CDRF 2009 "learning outcomes", WBI 2011 "intermediate capacity outcomes" and AoKE 2015 "intermediate outcomes" overlap without being identical. Crosswalk:
  - 2009 "fostered coalitions/networks" splits into 2011 "strengthened coalitions" and "enhanced networks";
  - 2009 "formulated policy/strategy" and "implemented strategy/plan" merge into 2011 "new implementation know-how";
  - AoKE folds coalitions into "improved consensus" and networks into "enhanced connectivity", and makes "new and improved actions" the terminal outcome.
- **[M] The crosswalk is lossy.** It needs to be a declared mapping between codebook versions, not a silent merge.
- **[M] Kirkpatrick fits individuals in organisations.** For city officials whose results are collective, such as consensus, coalitions and a council's approval of a plan, the CDRF/AoKE outcome classes are the better primary codebook. Kirkpatrick levels can be a secondary tag on individual-level evidence.

### 2. The Credibility of Contribution Claims and the Substantiation of Outcome Statements
#### Takeaway
Mayne makes a contribution claim credible through four conditions:
1. a plausible, reasoned theory of change;
2. activities implemented as planned;
3. evidence that the expected chain of results occurred;
4. other influencing factors assessed, and shown either to be insignificant or to have a recognised share.

Later work adds explicitly disproving alternative explanations, causal packages (INUS conditions) and process-tracing tests with Bayesian updating. Outcome harvesting runs the other way. It first records "who changed what, when and where" and the change agent's contribution, then has the record checked by independent, knowledgeable substantiators, whose agreement is graded (fully, partially, disagree). MSC verifies selected stories by visits and records the reasons each story was selected.

#### Cited Findings

**2.1 Mayne's contribution analysis**
- **[S] The four conditions** (TASO quoting Mayne 2008 and Befani and Mayne 2014):
  - "Plausibility: The programme is based on a reasoned theory of change."
  - "Fidelity: The activities of the programme were implemented."
  - "Verified theory of change: The theory of change is verified by evidence such that the evaluator is confident that the chain of expected results occurred."
  - "Accounting for other influencing factors: Other factors influencing the programme were assessed; either they were shown not to have made a significant contribution or, their relative contribution was recognised."
  - Source: [TASO, Contribution analysis briefing](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/TASO_Contribution-analysis-briefing.pdf)
- **[S] The six steps of Mayne (2008)** (ILAC Brief 16, as quoted by TASO):
  1. Set out the attribution problem to be addressed. This includes determining "the level of confidence required" from how findings will be used, and assessing "the plausibility of the expected contribution in relation to the size of the programme".
  2. Develop the theory of change and the risks to it.
  3. Gather existing evidence on the theory of change.
  4. Assemble and assess the contribution story, and challenges to it. This examines "links in the results chain and assessing which are strong and which are weak", and whether stakeholders agree.
  5. Seek out additional evidence.
  6. Revise and strengthen the contribution story.
  - Source: [TASO](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/TASO_Contribution-analysis-briefing.pdf); original at [ILAC Brief 16 (AgEcon)](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/52525/files/ILAC_Brief16_Contribution_Analysis.pdf) (returned 403 to me)
- **[S] Three "circles of influence"** (Mayne 2008, after Montague et al. 2002):
  - "direct control" (typically outputs);
  - "direct influence" (immediate and perhaps some intermediate outcomes);
  - "indirect influence" (less influence "due to its lack of direct contact with those involved and/or the significant influence of other factors").
  - Source: [TASO](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/TASO_Contribution-analysis-briefing.pdf)
- **[V] The later step list (Mayne 2019c)**, as given in the CJPE review:
  1. "Set out the specific cause–effect questions to be addressed";
  2. "Develop robust theories of change for the intervention and its pathways";
  3. "Gather the existing evidence on the components of the theory of change model of causality, including the results achieved and the causal link assumptions realized";
  4. "Assemble and assess the resulting contribution claim and the challenges to it";
  5. "Seek out additional evidence to strengthen the contribution claim";
  6. "Revise and strengthen the contribution claim";
  7. "Return to Step 4 if necessary" (the seventh step is for complex interventions).
  - Source: [Nielsen and Lemire 2026, CJPE 40(1):1–35, doi:10.3138/cjpe-2024-0044](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/download/6/1148/2561)
- **[V] Five criteria in Mayne (2011)**, as reported by the review: "(a) plausibility, (b) implementation according to plan, (c) evidentiary confirmation of key elements, (d) accounting for other influencing factors, and (e) disproving alternative explanations." The review also says Mayne moved from a probabilistic account to a generative and configurational one: "causal packages", Mackie's INUS conditions, and "Insufficient but Necessary part of a condition, which is itself Unnecessary but Sufficient". — [Nielsen and Lemire 2026](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/download/6/1148/2561)
- **[V] Robust theories of change and quality standards.** The review reports that Mayne (2017; 2019c, pp. 6–7) "developed 12 criteria for a structurally sound and plausible (robust) theory of change, including that the theory of change is plausible, explicit, logically coherent, and measurable." It proposes quality standards for applications of contribution analysis, among them:
  - "The most salient influencing factors and alternative explanations are identified and explicated";
  - "Data collection involves multiple methods and sources";
  - "Data analyses systematically and transparently test hypotheses for key causal links, influencing factors, and alternative explanations."
  - Source: [Nielsen and Lemire 2026](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/download/6/1148/2561)
- **[S] Process tracing linked to contribution analysis** (Befani and Mayne 2014, via TASO): the two can be combined using Van Evera's "smoking gun", "hoop" and "doubly decisive" tests. Hoop tests are "designed to disconfirm" and smoking-gun tests "to confirm". Combining them "maximises certainty in the case of the hoop test and uniqueness in the case of the smoking gun test". — [TASO](https://cdn.taso.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/TASO_Contribution-analysis-briefing.pdf); see also [Nielsen and Lemire 2026](https://ces.journals.uvic.ca/index.php/cjpe/article/download/6/1148/2561) on Bayesian updating (Befani et al. 2016)
- **[V] A practitioner's version of steps 4–6** (Itad): "investigate any credible alternative explanations"; the aim is "credible plausibility". — [Itad, Contribution Analysis report](https://www.itad.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Contribution-Analysis-Report.pdf)
- **[M] Mayne (2001)**, "Addressing attribution through contribution analysis", *Canadian Journal of Program Evaluation* 16(1):1–24, introduced the idea of a "plausible association" judged by whether "a reasonable person, knowing what has occurred in the programme and that the intended outcomes actually occurred, agrees that the programme contributed to those outcomes". I recall this formulation and did not check it against the text here.

**2.2 Outcome harvesting (Wilson-Grau and Britt, Ford Foundation MENA Office, May 2012, revised November 2013)**
- **[V] Definition of outcome:** the method rests on "the definition of outcome as a change in the behavior, relationships, actions, activities, policies, or practices of an individual, group, community, organization, or institution." Outcomes "can be positive or negative, intended or unintended, but the connection between the initiative and the outcomes should be verifiable." — [Outcome Harvesting brief](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)
- **[V] The six iterative steps** (p. 4):
  1. "Design the Outcome Harvest";
  2. "Gather data and draft outcome descriptions";
  3. "Engage change agents in formulating outcome descriptions";
  4. "Substantiate: Harvesters obtain the views of independent individuals knowledgeable about the outcome(s) and how they were achieved; this validates and enhances the credibility of the findings";
  5. "Analyze and interpret", organising "outcome descriptions through a database";
  6. "Support use of findings".
  - Source: [Outcome Harvesting brief](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)
- **[V] Outcome description:** "The formulation of who changed what, when and where it took place, and how the change agent contributed to that outcome are combined in sufficient specificity and measurability to enable the harvest user to take action." Optional dimensions: significance, "history, context, contribution of other social actors, and emerging evidence of impact". **Contribution** is defined as "Verifiable explanation of how the change agent contributed to the outcome." (pp. 5–6) — [Outcome Harvesting brief](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)
- **[V] Establishing contribution.** To establish contribution, "indirect or direct, partial or whole, intended or not – beyond a reasonable doubt", the harvester uses three mechanisms:
  1. "Reported (and validated) observations";
  2. "Direct critical observation";
  3. "Direct or simple inductive inference from items 1 or 2".
  - Source: [Outcome Harvesting brief](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)
- **[V] Substantiation (pp. 18–20):**
  - Substantiators are "positioned outside the change agent organization, but are well-informed about the outcome and the change agent's contribution".
  - They may be recommended by the change agents, chosen by donors or allies, or form "an external panel of experts".
  - "a sample of the total number of outcome descriptions might be selected for substantiation".
  - "the criteria for selecting whether and how to substantiate depend on the degree of credibility required by the uses for the findings."
  - The record of opinion asks separately about the outcome and about the contribution, each on "Fully agree / Partially agree / Disagree", with comments. Comments let the harvester "decide whether or not to discount the outcome".
  - Unreconciled differences are recorded: "harvesters note the varying perspectives".
  - The process is "one of approximation to identify the essential facts of the matter and not one of negotiating differences of opinion".
  - Source: [Outcome Harvesting brief](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)

**2.3 Most Significant Change (Davies and Dart, v1.00, April 2005)**
- **[V] The ten steps:**
  1. How to start and raise interest;
  2. Defining the domains of change;
  3. Defining the reporting period;
  4. Collecting SC stories;
  5. Selecting the most significant of the stories;
  6. Feeding back the results of the selection process;
  7. Verification of stories;
  8. Quantification;
  9. Secondary analysis and meta-monitoring;
  10. Revising the system.
  - Source: [MSC Guide](https://www.mande.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2005/MSCGuide.pdf)
- **[V] How selection works** (p. 9):
  - Stories are filtered up the hierarchy. Each level "selects the single most significant account of change within each of the domains."
  - "Every time stories are selected, the criteria used to select them are recorded and fed back to all interested stakeholders."
  - The core question asks about "the most significant change" in a period. Respondents state "why they consider a particular change to be the most significant one".
  - Source: [MSC Guide](https://www.mande.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2005/MSCGuide.pdf)
- **[V] Verification (Step 7):**
  - It guards against stories that "be deliberate fictional accounts", "describe real events that have been misunderstood" or "exaggerate the significance of events".
  - It can be skipped where selectors know the events.
  - The guide does "not recommend making random checks"; verification targets the selected stories.
  - Quantification can count how far a selected change "has taken place in other locations".
  - Source: [MSC Guide](https://www.mande.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2005/MSCGuide.pdf)

**2.4 Attribution in the World Bank guides**
- **[V] AoKE (pp. 71–72):**
  - "It generally takes a fair amount of time for institution-level changes to happen. When they do, multiple variables are usually involved, not just a knowledge exchange initiative. This makes attribution tenuous."
  - Template 2 asks "Was it influenced? Yes / Somewhat / No" and "How do you know?" for each challenge category.
  - The guide notes "Strong intermediate outcomes can often indicate success or progress towards a change objective."
  - Source: [AoKE 2015](https://www.apccolombia.gov.co/sites/default/files/2022-06/WB%20%282015%29%20The%20art%20of%20knowledge%20exchange.pdf)
- **[V] CDRF:** stronger evidence means "memos … making specific causal connections to the capacity development activities", verifiable examples, and observation before and after (Table A5.1). This is an evidence grading, not a test of rival explanations. — [CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf)

#### Inferences
- **[M] The three methods record the same structure.** Contribution analysis, outcome harvesting and MSC each attach to an outcome:
  - a claimed causal link;
  - evidence items with their sources and their independence from the programme;
  - a judgement by someone other than the claimant;
  - a stated level of confidence required by the use.
- **[M] Credibility can be scored per link, not per programme.** For each link in the theory of change, record:
  1. the link is stated, plausible and grounded in research;
  2. the activity happened (fidelity);
  3. the outcome is observed;
  4. rival factors are listed, each judged negligible, partial or dominant;
  5. the result of any discriminating test (hoop or smoking gun);
  6. independent substantiation (fully / partially / disagree).
  - The claim's standing is then the weakest link along the path.
- **[M] Ground the evidence grade in the sources.** "Stronger" versus "weaker" evidence (CDRF Table A5.1) and "independent and knowledgeable substantiator" (outcome harvesting) can serve as the grading scheme of evidence items. The grade should come from source type and independence, not from the evaluator's impression.

#### Gaps
- Mayne (2001) and Mayne (2012, "Contribution analysis: Coming of age?", *Evaluation* 18(3):270–280) were not read in the original. The "reasonable person" wording is [M].
- I did not read Wilson-Grau's 2018 book (*Outcome Harvesting: Principles, Steps, and Evaluation Applications*). Its principles, such as "credible-enough outcomes", are therefore not covered.

### 3. The Evaluation Evidence on Learning Formats That Lead to Action, on Follow-up and on Action Plans
#### Takeaway
The best-documented finding comes from IEG's 2008 evaluation: most training produces learning, but only about half produces substantial change in the workplace. The drivers are targeting (anchored in diagnosis), follow-up support, and workplace resources and incentives, not satisfaction with the event.

For city networks the evidence is mostly self-reported, commissioned or qualitative:
- Peer exchanges are valued chiefly for personal networking, and most do not amount to learning.
- C40 membership shows no detectable effect on cities' CO2 emissions, with influence mainly on ambition.
- URBACT's action-planning networks show high approval of plans and partial implementation, measured by survey without a counterfactual.

The policy-transfer literature (Rose; Dolowitz and Marsh) explains failure as uninformed, incomplete or inappropriate transfer. It frames lesson drawing as a prospective judgement of whether the conditions that made a programme work can be recreated at home.

No study found compares peer exchange, workshops, study visits and twinning head to head with a common outcome measure. AoKE's table of instrument strengths is expert judgement.

**3.5 Policy transfer and lesson drawing, and why transfers fail**
- **[V] Rose's lesson drawing versus policy transfer** (Stone summarising Rose 1993 and Page 2000): lesson drawing emphasises "the conditions under which policies or practices operate in exporter jurisdictions and whether and how the conditions which might make them work in a similar way can be created in importer jurisdictions". — [Stone 2001, CSGR WP 69/01](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/pais/research/csgr/research/workingpapers/2001/wp6901.pdf)
- **[S] Dolowitz and Marsh, via Stone:**
  - Objects of transfer: "(i) policies, (ii) institutions, (iii) ideologies or justifications, (iv) attitudes and ideas, and (v) negative lessons".
  - Degrees: "straight-forward copying … as well as various forms of emulation, synthesis and hybridisation, and inspiration" (Dolowitz and Marsh 1996:351).
  - A "policy transfer continuum" from voluntary to coercive (2000:13–17).
  - Failure: they distinguish "successful transfers and inappropriate, uninformed or incomplete transfer", with "constraints and forms of resistance … [that] can lead to policy failure".
  - Source: [Stone 2001](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/pais/research/csgr/research/workingpapers/2001/wp6901.pdf)
- **[M] The three failure types** (Dolowitz and Marsh 2000, *Governance* 13(1):5–24, at p. 17):
  - **uninformed transfer:** the borrower has insufficient information about the policy and how it operates where it came from;
  - **incomplete transfer:** crucial elements of what made it work were not transferred;
  - **inappropriate transfer:** insufficient attention to differences in economic, social, political and ideological context.
  - I give these from memory. The search did not return the article's own wording.
- **[S] Rose's prospective question and ten steps.** Rose (1991, "What is lesson-drawing?", *J. Public Policy* 11(1):3–30) asks under what conditions a programme that works elsewhere would work at home. Rose (2005, *Learning from Comparative Public Policy: A Practical Guide*) sets out ten steps, from understanding programmes and lessons through searching abroad, modelling how a programme really works, deciding whether a lesson should and can be applied, to increasing the chances of success. "A model of a foreign programme is the starting, not the end, point of lesson-drawing." — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/Learning-From-Comparative-Public-Policy-A-Practical-Guide/Rose/p/book/9780415317429); [O'Reilly chapter 6 excerpt](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/learning-from-comparative/9780415317429/18_Chapter_06.xhtml)
- **[M] Rose's (1993) lesson types:** copying, adaptation, hybrid, synthesis, inspiration. Unchecked.
- **[S] Critique of the framework.** Lodge argues that Dolowitz and Marsh redescribe aspects of failure as kinds of transfer rather than giving independent explanations. — [search abstract, Lodge](https://es.scribd.com/document/162430786/Lodge-Limitations-of-Policy-Transfer)

#### Inferences
- **[M] The evidence agrees on what matters and does not test formats.** IEG's evidence, Haupt et al., Andrews and Manning, the OECD survey and URBACT point the same way: diagnosis and targeting before the event, follow-up after it, resources and authority to act, and continuity of people. None of them compares formats head to head. A recording system should therefore capture these conditions as covariates of every exchange, so that a programme's own records can later answer the format question.
- **[M] The C40 null result needs careful reading.** It concerns emissions, which are far downstream of learning. Network influence on "ambition" is an intermediate outcome in AoKE's terms. A results chain that skips the intermediate links will tend to find nothing.
- **[M] Rose's conditions are support factors.** The question of whether the exporter's conditions can be recreated at home is the same as Cartwright and Hardie's "it will work here" argument, which the infrastructure already has (tekmerion's warrant; policy's `transfer`). A transfer claim from a study visit can be stored as a claim of external validity with its support factors.

- No IEG evaluation specific to knowledge exchange or South–South cooperation was read. Such evaluations may exist; I did not find or open them.
- No published academic evaluation of CityNet or ICLEI learning outcomes was found in this search.
- I could not obtain the full OECD/Bloomberg toolkit (403).

### 4. The Measurement of Time to Action, of the Implementation of Action Plans and of Sustained Change, and the Comparison of the Costs of Formats
#### Takeaway
Measurement in practice relies on fixed follow-up windows: end of event, 6 weeks, 1–3, 3–6–9 months, 6–9 months, and 3 years for URBACT. It uses ordinal self-ratings (small–large; yes–somewhat–no), approval of the plan, funding secured, and the share of planned actions started or implemented. "Time to action" is never measured as a duration with censoring. It appears only as threshold indicators ("within three months…") or as a snapshot of whether actions had started at a given date.

Sustained change is a named type of progress ("maintenance of change") but is rarely followed. Cost comparison is anecdotal: AoKE gives orders of magnitude from a few thousand dollars to over $100,000. The only formal cost-to-benefit device is Phillips's ROI, which needs monetised, isolated benefits that knowledge exchanges seldom have.

- **[V] Phillips's cost discipline:** ROI requires "fully loaded costs" and isolating "the amount of improvement to the program" before converting it to money. — [ROI Institute](https://roiinstitute.net/roi-methodology/)

#### Inferences
- **[M] Time to action needs survival analysis.** Programmes measure it as snapshots. The natural statistic is a time-to-event: from the exchange's end date to the first documented action, such as an approved plan, an allocated budget or a started process. Right-censoring applies at the last follow-up, and abandonment is a competing risk. Kaplan–Meier, Aalen–Johansen and Cox methods are already in peira's `survival`. "75% started within 3 months" and "80% before closure" are then points on a survival curve, comparable across programmes only when each has a declared follow-up schedule.
- **[M] Implementation of a plan can be a count over its actions.** Each action has status (planned, started, completed, abandoned), date, owner, cost estimate, secured funding and evidence. URBACT's IAP quality checklist (actions, timing, responsibility, cost, funding, monitoring, risk) is then a completeness score for the plan, computed at its baseline.
- **[M] Cost-effectiveness should be per intermediate outcome, not ROI.** Monetised benefits will rarely be defensible, so cost per participant-day or per documented intermediate outcome is more usable. Examples: cost per substantiated action, or per plan approved. Fully loaded costs include participants' time and the hosts' in-kind support. Comparisons are honest only within the same outcome class and follow-up window.

#### Gaps
- No source found reports distributions of time to action, sustained implementation beyond 3 years, or decay of change after programmes.
- No source found compares the cost per outcome of formats. AoKE's figures are illustrative.
- I did not find whether IEG's 2008 evaluation gives costs per format. The text mentions training as a share of project cost, but I did not extract an exact figure.

### 1. The Municipal Finances Self-Assessment and Urban Audits: Ratios, Definitions and Thresholds
#### Takeaway
The MFSA is a four-module, eight-step template (city profile, database, generic financial framework, historical analysis, ratio analysis, five-year projections, financial-management assessment, improvement plan) whose ratio "dashboard" holds about fifteen ratios with stated benchmarks: gross operating savings before interest over current revenue > 0.3, net operating surplus after full debt service over current revenue > 0.2, cash ≥ 90 days of current liabilities, debt outstanding ≤ 10 years of operating surplus, debt service < 10% of current revenue, capital investment > 40% of current revenue, plus per-capita comparisons. The Urban Audit is its physical counterpart: an inventory of infrastructure and service gaps that yields a priority investment programme and a priority maintenance programme, which the MFSA then tests for financial feasibility.

#### Cited Findings

**Structure of the MFSA**
- [V] Four modules: (1) collect and organise information on city finances and urban management issues (city profile) (steps 1–2); (2) historical analysis and summary tables of revenue, expenditure and financial situation (steps 3–5); (3) financial projections (step 6); (4) evaluation of financial management tools and a Municipal Finances Improvement Action Plan (steps 7–8). An Excel template was published at `siteresources.worldbank.org/EXTURBANDEVELOPMENT/Resources/MFSA-Template.xlsx`. — [World Bank Municipal Finances handbook 2014, pp. 405–407](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] The eight steps and their objectives (Figure 8.7): 1 city profile; 2 basic database; 3 generic financial and accounting framework ("ability to generate growth savings and operating surplus; capital investment effort; creditworthiness"); 4 historical analysis ("revenue and expenditure structure and trends; dependency on grants and external financing; level of service provided"); 5 ratio analysis ("review of economic and financial performance through main indicators; comparison with other LGU"); 6 financial projections ("impacts of political choices on future accounts; financial needs (loans)"); 7 financial management assessment ("level of municipal autonomy; budget credibility; reporting: comprehensiveness, transparency, predictability"); 8 financial improvement action plan. — [handbook p. 407](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Its three findings: "financial sustainability ... based on operating surplus, capacity to borrow money, and ability to increase its capital investment"; "financial governance and management quality, based on credibility of the budget and its comprehensiveness and transparency"; "efficiency of service delivery, based on cost and tariff analysis". — [handbook pp. 406–407](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] The MFSA "relies on municipal staff themselves" (a self-assessment, not an outsourced audit), "puts city officials in the driver's seat", and was customised for South East Europe (Box 8.6), where it showed that creditworthiness data are "not reported in any of the typical mandatory accounting reports". — [handbook pp. 403–405](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [S] The 2019 successor *Better Cities, Better World: A Handbook on Local Governments Self-Assessments* (World Bank, OKR handle 10986/32120) lays the MFSA out as six steps (core databases, historical analysis, ratio analysis, financial projections, financial management assessment, action plan) with "Appendix B: Detailed Methodology and Procedures to Help Calculate Specific Results in MFSA", and pairs it with the Urban Audit Self-Assessment. — [PEFA note on the handbook](https://pefa.org/news/better-cities-better-world-handbook-local-government-self-assessment); [OKR record](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/32120) (not accessible, HTTP 403)

**The generic financial framework (Step 3) and its account structure**
- [V] Figure 8.8 splits the budget: *current budget* revenue = 1 taxation revenue, 2 grants, 3 others (income from services and assets), 4 year N−1 margin; expenditure = 1 payroll, 2 operation and maintenance, 3 debt service, 4 year N−1 loss; the difference is the operating surplus. *Capital investment budget* revenue = self-financing (the operating surplus), property sales, subsidies, loans; expenditure = civil works, land and equipment purchase, loan repayment. — [handbook p. 417](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Box 8.8 definitions: debt service "should be split between the current budget — for loan interest, and the capital budget — for loan repayment. In a more prudential approach, all the debt service (including loan principal repayment) should be covered by the operating surplus, as a proof of debt servicing ability." Most local public accounting systems are cash-based and "do not include depreciation". Unpaid expenditures and billed-but-unrecovered revenues must be annexed because they "affect apparent surplus at the end of the year". — [handbook pp. 417–418](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

**The Step 5 ratio dashboard (benchmarks "based on international standards used in Western European countries and in the U.S.")**
- [V] Stock ratios — creditworthiness: *operating savings before interest / current actual revenues*, benchmark > 0.3; *net operating surplus (after debt service including capital repayment) / current actual revenues*, > 0.2; *cash (end of year) / (current liabilities ÷ 365)*, 90 days. Indebtedness: *debt outstanding / operating surplus* ("capacity to clear its debt"), < 10 years; *debt service / total current revenues*, < 10%. Fiscal autonomy: *(own tax receipts + unconditional grants) / current actual revenues*; *tax pressure = tax receipts / tax potential* (benchmarks printed beside these two are "> 80%" and "< 70%"). Capital investment effort: *capital investment expenditures / current actual revenues*, > 40%; *capital investment expenditures delegated by state / total investment expenditures*, > 50% means "the LG functions are still weak". Level of service: *maintenance works expenditures / operating expenditures*, > 30%. Others: *municipal employees per 1,000 inhabitants*, > 25 flags limited room; *salaries and wages / operating actual expenses*, > 40% flags limited room for maintenance and investment; *actual revenues / estimated revenues*, > 95% means a reliable budget; *arrears / net cash (end of year)*, > 1 means the LG "accumulates short-term debt and reduces its credibility toward contractors". Flow ratio: *margin ratio = total financial resources (cash) / total financial obligations (payments + arrears)*, benchmark 1.02 ("living within its financial means"). Comparison ratios: total revenues, total expenditures, current revenues, debt outstanding and capital investment, each per capita, against LGs of the same size. — [handbook pp. 427–430](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
  - *Caveat.* The dashboard is a rotated, multi-column table; I read it from extracted text. The pairing of each benchmark with its ratio is clear for most rows; for the fiscal-autonomy pair (> 80%, < 70%) the alignment is my reading of the layout. [M]
- [V] The handbook recommends referring to ratios already used by the ministry of finance or interior, publishing them annually for all municipalities, and adding local-GDP ratios only when local GDP exists. — [handbook p. 427](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

**The Step 6 five-year projection table**
- [V] Rows: A total current revenues (own tax: property, business, other; state transfers: shared tax, unconditional grants, conditional grants; other revenues: asset rent, interest); B total operating expenditures (payroll by staff group; operating costs: supplies, electricity, communication, fuel, maintenance, other); **C gross operating savings = A − B**; D debt service (existing debt: interest, repayment; new debt (loans after YN−1): interest, repayment; totals); **E net savings = C − D**; F capital expenditures; **G investment financing = F − E** (investment grants; own capital revenues excluding operating surplus; loans); **H overall closing balance (cash flow) = (A + G) − (B + D + F)**. Columns: trend of previous three years, main assumptions, index, specific calculation, N−1 actual, N estimated, N+1 … N+5 projected, "in current currency". — [handbook pp. 431–434](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Purpose: "demonstrate the impact of policy decisions (expenses, borrowing, tax pressure, and so on) and their underlying assumptions on the financial position", testing "several sets of assumptions and scenarios". — [handbook p. 431](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Step 7 draws on PEFA and checks six areas: credibility of the budget; comprehensiveness and transparency; policy-based budgeting; predictability and control in budget execution; accounting, recording and reporting; external scrutiny and audit. — [handbook pp. 434–435](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

**Supervisory ratios reported alongside the MFSA in the handbook**
- [V] France's Finance Law 1999 obliges municipalities to compute 11 key ratios a year and send them to the central government (Table 8.2): actual operating expenditure per capita; local tax receipts per capita; actual current revenue per capita; capital investment expenditure per capita; debt outstanding per capita; intergovernmental operating transfer (DGF) per capita; salaries / total operating expenditure; tax pressure (actual or potential); (operating expenditure + debt repayment) / actual current revenue; capital investment expenditure / actual operating revenue; debt outstanding / actual operating revenue — each against the average of its size stratum. — [handbook pp. 394–395](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Indicators used in west and central Africa: date of budget approval; "Is the Y−1 budget deficit less than 5 percent of operating revenue?"; "Are salaries and wages less than 20 percent of current revenue?"; "Is the capital investment budget greater than 40 percent of total expenditures?"; "Is debt service less than 12 percent of current revenue?". — [handbook pp. 393–395](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Senegal's Municipal Development Agency (ADM) lent against a cash contribution of "at least 20 percent of project cost" and published a Financial Ratios Guide (Figure 8.5). — [handbook p. 392](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

**Urban Audits**
- [V] The urban audit's "main objective [is] the gathering of baseline information on the existing condition of infrastructure and services, the identification of patterns of urbanization and pockets of poverty, and the spatial location and quantification of the gaps, leading to the identification of a priority investments program and a priority maintenance program." Combined with the financial audit it yields a Municipal Adjustment Program and a signed municipal contract (Senegal's UDDP/PAC, cloned in over 200 municipalities in 10 African countries; Tunisia's Third Municipal Development Project restructured 132 of 260 municipalities). — [handbook pp. 403–405, Box 8.7](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

#### Inferences
- [M] The MFSA's account model is French-style "épargne" accounting: gross savings (épargne brute, before interest) → net savings (after full debt service) → financing of capital spending. A module should compute C, E, G, H exactly as printed and treat H as the residual cash, which is zero when the financing sources in G exactly equal the need F − E; a negative H is unfinanced investment.
- [M] Several MFSA benchmarks are directional rules of thumb without a stated source (e.g., > 0.3 for gross savings, > 25 employees per 1,000); they should be held as a declared scheme, not as truths.

- The 2019 *Better Cities, Better World* Appendix B (the "detailed methodology") and the MFSA Excel template could not be retrieved (HTTP 403; the siteresources URL is retired). Whether its ratio set or benchmarks changed after 2014 is unverified.
- The Urban Audit's own indicator list (service coverage per sector) was not read.

### 2. The Ratios Analysts Use, Defined from the Accounts
#### Takeaway
The same seven families recur across the World Bank, the IMF, the rating agencies and statutory schemes, but each source defines numerator and denominator slightly differently (gross vs net operating balance, interest-only vs full debt service, direct vs tax-supported debt, cash vs accrual), and "DSCR" in particular means three different things. A module must name every ratio by its exact definition and source, not by its family name.

#### Cited Findings

**Own-source revenue share / fiscal autonomy**
- [V] *Own-source revenues / total revenues* "measures a local government's own-source revenues compared to its total revenues"; a high share means reliance on "recurrent, predictable revenues". Also *recurrent revenues / total revenues* and *recurrent revenues per capita* (burden of taxes and charges). — [handbook Table 8.3, p. 398, after Josifov, Pamfil and Comsa 2008](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] IMF: *own-source revenues / total revenues* ("degree of reliance on external funding sources"); *discretionary expenses / total expenses*, where discretionary = total minus essential spending (wages, interest, mandatory services, important ongoing capital projects); revenue and expenditure per capita. — [IMF How-To Note 2022, Annex 1](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)
- [V] MFSA variant counts unconditional grants as autonomous: *(own tax + unconditional grants) / current revenues*. — [handbook p. 428](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

**Operating surplus / operating balance**
- [V] S&P: operating balance = adjusted operating revenues − adjusted operating expenditures (operating expenditures *include interest*; adjustments remove noncash items such as depreciation and provisions and pass-through items, to a "modified cash base"); key ratio *operating balance / adjusted operating revenues*. Operating revenues "exclude capital revenues, such as capital subsidies and proceeds from asset sales, and any revenues from borrowed funds". — [S&P LRG methodology 2019, ¶40–43, ¶68–73](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [V] Moody's scorecard: *gross operating balance / operating revenues* (12.5% of the financial factor). — [Moody's credit opinion, Cape Town 2015](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)
- [V] IMF: *operating balance / revenues* ("whether operating expenses are contained within operating revenues"). — [IMF How-To Note 2022](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)
- [V] Colombia: *ahorro operacional* = ingresos corrientes − gastos de funcionamiento − transferencias pagadas; current revenue includes tax, non-tax, royalties actually received, national transfers, shares in national revenue, "recursos del balance" and financial returns; salaries, fees, benefits and social-security contributions count as operating spending "even if budgeted as investment". — [Ley 358 de 1997, art. 2 parágrafo](https://normas.cra.gov.co/gestor/docs/ley_0358_1997.htm)
- [S] Spain: *ahorro neto* starts from recognised rights of revenue chapters 1–5 minus recognised obligations of expenditure chapters 1, 2 and 4, then deducts the annuity of debt. — [Ministerio de Hacienda, note on local borrowing 2024](https://www.hacienda.gob.es/SGFAL/DGCFEL/Endeudamiento/Nota-Informativa-Endeudamiento-2024.pdf); [FAMP presentation](https://www.famp.es/export/sites/famp/.galleries/documentos-general/Endeudamiento-local-y-planes-de-ajuste.-Sostenibilidad-financiera-haciendas-locales.-Luis-Temes-Castrillon.-Ministerio-Hacienda.pdf)

**Debt service coverage (three incompatible definitions)**
- [V] Cash-flow DSCR (IMF): *operating balance before interest and depreciation / debt service*; where loans have bullets or grace periods, use "maximum future debt service". — [IMF How-To Note 2022, Annex 1](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)
- [V] Liquidity DSCR (S&P): *total free cash / debt service over the next 12 months*, where total free cash = adjusted cash + liquid assets + balance after capital accounts (with interest added back) + onlending + already contracted funding; score 1 when free cash *minus* contracted funding covers > 100% of debt service. — [S&P 2019, ¶47–51, ¶78–80](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [S] Synthetic DSCR (Fitch): *operating balance / mortgage-style annuity on net adjusted debt*; Fitch also reports an "actual coverage ratio". — [Fitch, Canton of Zurich rating report 2026](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf) (definition from search snippet; the actual-coverage row is [V] in the report's table)
- [V] Inverse form (MFSA): *debt service / total current revenues* < 10%. — [handbook p. 428](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [S] New South Wales's Financial Sustainability Rating framework uses a DSCR benchmark of 2.0x, an operating ratio of 4.0% and a cash expense ratio of 3.0 months (values described as garbled in the extracted IMF text). — [IMF How-To Note 2022, Box 3.1](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)

**Debt to revenue / leverage**
- [V] S&P: *tax-supported debt / consolidated operating revenues*, where tax-supported debt = direct debt + debt of non-financial GREs the LRG would very likely support and that need its payments + guaranteed debt; consolidated operating revenues add the commercial revenues of those GREs net of transfers from the LRG. Second ratio *interest / adjusted operating revenues*. Direct debt includes PPP and securitisation debt when "the primary motivation ... is to achieve off-balance-sheet treatment". — [S&P 2019, ¶57–60, ¶81–86](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [V] Moody's: *net direct and indirect debt / operating revenues* (25% of the financial factor) and *short-term direct debt / total direct debt* (25%). — [Moody's, Cape Town 2015](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)
- [V] Fitch: *payback = net adjusted debt / operating balance* (years); *fiscal debt burden = net adjusted debt / operating revenue*; for large "Type A" LRGs, *economic liability burden* (debt and liabilities relative to the economy) is primary. Net adjusted debt = direct debt + leases − unrestricted cash. — [Fitch, Zurich 2026](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf)
- [V] MFSA: *debt outstanding / operating surplus* < 10 years. — [handbook p. 428](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] IMF: *debt / free own-source revenues*, or debt / subnational GDP if reliable. — [IMF How-To Note 2022](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)

**Capital investment ratio and asset maintenance**
- [V] MFSA: *capital investment / current revenues* > 40%; *maintenance / operating expenditure* > 30%. West/central Africa supervision: capital budget > 40% of total expenditure. — [handbook pp. 394, 429](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] IMF: *maintenance expenditure / stock of infrastructure assets* against maintenance norms; *asset renewals / depreciation* ("less than 1 would indicate a depleting stock"); *capital expenditure / depreciation*. — [IMF How-To Note 2022](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)
- [V] S&P: *balance after capital accounts / total adjusted revenues*, where balance after capital accounts = operating balance + capital revenues − capital expenditures, "a proxy of the overall funding needs ... [that] would generally correspond to changes in net debt". — [S&P 2019, ¶40, ¶44, ¶76–77](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)

**Collection efficiency**
- [V] Italy's statutory parameters use *cash collections of own revenues / final forecasts of current revenues* (P2, deficit if < 22% for municipalities) and an indicator of "effective collection capacity (referred to total revenues)" (P8, deficit if < 47%). — [Decreto 28 dicembre 2018, Tab. A1](https://www.moltocomuni.it/wp-content/uploads/decreto-del-28-dicembre-2018.pdf)
- [V] MFSA's budget-credibility proxy: *actual revenues / estimated revenues* > 95%. — [handbook p. 429](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [M] The usual property-tax definitions are *current collection rate = collections against the current year's levy / current year's levy* and *arrears collection rate = collections of prior-year arrears / opening arrears*; a combined rate divides all collections by levy plus opening arrears. Which is used changes the number a great deal.

**Liquidity**
- [V] MFSA: cash ≥ 90 days of current liabilities; margin ratio cash / (payments + arrears) ≈ 1.02; arrears / net cash > 1 a warning. — [handbook pp. 428–430](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] IMF: *free cash and liquid assets / current liabilities*; *short-term debt / total debt* (refinancing risk). — [IMF How-To Note 2022](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)
- [S] South Africa's MFMA Circular 71 template lists a *cash/cost coverage ratio (excluding unspent conditional grants)* norm of 1–3 months and *capital cost (interest paid and redemption) as a % of total operating expenditure* of 6%–8%, and a 45% norm in the debt-to-revenue row. — [uMhlathuze annexure reproducing the Circular 71 template](https://www.umhlathuze.gov.za/images/Copy_of_x13679-1.pdf)

#### Inferences
- [M] Gross vs net matters by an amount equal to interest: the MFSA's "operating savings before interest", Moody's "gross operating balance" and the IMF's "operating balance before interest and depreciation" are before interest; S&P's and Fitch's operating balance are after interest (S&P's operating expenditure "includes interest expenses"). Fitch's payback (net debt / operating balance) and the MFSA's 10-year rule (debt / operating surplus) are the same concept and directly comparable only after fixing which surplus.

#### Gaps
- S&P's numerical cut-offs for each factor (e.g., which operating-balance percentages score 1–5) are in its companion *Guidance* article, not the criteria; not retrieved.
- MFMA Circular 71 itself was not read; the 95% collection-rate norm often cited for it was not confirmed.

### 3. Multi-Year Cash-Flow Projection and Debt Capacity
#### Takeaway
Borrowing capacity is the largest new debt whose service, added to existing service, keeps a projected coverage ratio above a floor in every year of the loan's life, and that respects every statutory cap. With annuity repayment it has a closed form: the minimum over years of spare cash flow (surplus divided by the floor, less existing service) capitalised at the loan's rate and tenor. The handbook insists the test cover the whole repayment period, because historical-ratio tests hide balloon payments.

#### Cited Findings
- [V] "Borrowing capacity is the maximum amount of new debt that a local government can issue without hurting its capacity to deliver services and serve existing and new debt. It depends on how much money it has to repay and service the new debt today, reduced by its commitments payable in the future, but increased by its likely future revenues." It depends on four factors: economic and financial prospects (including the risk in the central government's transfer policy); the terms of new loans (rates, maturities); the structure and size of the existing debt stock (clustered maturities); and the institutional limits. — [handbook pp. 355–356](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Balloon-payment risk: simplified mandatory indicators ("debt stock or new debt as a percentage of net operating revenues") suffice for small borrowing, "but for larger borrowing, a more detailed cash-flow analysis is required that covers the entire repayment period"; projections of historical trends "may ignore or hide potential balloon payments" when grace periods or bullet maturities coincide. Creditors mitigate with a debt service reserve fund of "6 to 12 months of debt service" or an escrow of project revenue. — [handbook p. 356](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Example of a self-imposed cap: San Francisco's 10-year capital plan limits general-fund debt service to 3.5% of the city's own discretionary revenues and forbids raising property taxes for new debt, so "new debt can be raised only after the old debt is repaid". — [handbook Box 7.2, p. 331](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Pay-as-you-go (investment only from net operating surplus) yields "a large number of small, short-lived projects"; long-term debt spreads the cost over the generations that use the asset. — [handbook pp. 326–327](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [V] Colombia defines "capacidad de pago" as "el flujo mínimo de ahorro operacional que permite efectuar cumplidamente el servicio de la deuda en todos los años, dejando un remanente para financiar inversiones" (art. 1) and computes current revenue and operating savings from the prior year's executed budget, adjusted by the central bank's inflation target (art. 7). — [Ley 358 de 1997](https://normas.cra.gov.co/gestor/docs/ley_0358_1997.htm)
- [V] Mexico's ceiling is on net financing in a year: *financiamiento neto = disposiciones − amortizaciones* of short- and long-term financing within the fiscal year (Reglamento art. 25), capped by the Sistema de Alertas level (art. 23–24). — [Reglamento del Sistema de Alertas](https://www.nl.gob.mx/sites/default/files/migrate-files/12.-_reglamento_del_sistema_de_alertas.pdf)

**Formulas (my statement of the standard method)**
- [M] Projection: for t = 1…T, project current revenue R_t by component (own tax by base × rate × collection rate; shared taxes and grants by the transfer rule; user charges), operating expenditure E_t by component (payroll by headcount × wage index; O&M by asset stock and price index), giving gross operating savings before interest S_t = R_t − E_t. Existing debt service DS^0_t comes from the loan schedules (interest on outstanding balance + scheduled principal, including bullets).
- [M] DSCR in year t with a new loan: κ_t = S_t / (DS^0_t + A_t), where A_t is the new loan's service.
- [M] Annuity loan of principal L, rate r, tenor n, grace 0: A = L · r / (1 − (1 + r)^(−n)). Coverage floor κ_min (lenders commonly use 1.2–1.5; NSW uses 2.0 as a benchmark): require S_t ≥ κ_min (DS^0_t + A) for t = 1…n, so A ≤ min_t (S_t/κ_min − DS^0_t) and
  **L_max^DSCR = [min_{1≤t≤n} (S_t/κ_min − DS^0_t)]⁺ · (1 − (1 + r)^(−n)) / r**.
- [M] With a grace period of m years (interest only, then annuity over n − m): the binding years are split; compute A_grace = rL for t ≤ m and A = L r/(1 − (1+r)^(−(n−m))) after, and solve L_max = min over the two regimes. With equal-principal repayment, service is highest in year 1: A_1 = L(1/n + r).
- [M] Statutory caps convert to further bounds and the capacity is the minimum: stock caps L ≤ c·R − D_0 (e.g., Spain c = 1.10 of current revenue; Brazil 1.2 × net current revenue for municipalities; Colombia 1.00 of current revenue); interest caps (Colombia: interest including the new loan ≤ 0.40 × ahorro operacional for presumed capacity, ≤ 0.60 absolute); flow caps (Mexico: net new financing ≤ 15% / 5% / 0 of unrestricted revenue by alert level). L_max = min(L_max^DSCR, L_max^stock, L_max^interest, L_max^flow).
- [M] Report the binding constraint and the binding year: that is the explanation a person needs, and a different constraint binds for different cities.

#### Inferences
- [M] The capacity is a functional of the projection; it inherits every projection assumption (revenue buoyancy, transfer rules, wage growth). A responsible module should return L_max as a distribution or as a set of scenario values, not a single number, and should show the year-by-year coverage path.

#### Gaps
- No primary World Bank or agency document stating the closed-form L_max was found; the handbook describes the method in words. The 1.2–1.5 lender floor range is [M].

### 4. Shadow Credit Ratings: The Agencies' Methodologies for Regional and Local Governments
#### Takeaway
All three agencies separate a standalone assessment (Moody's BCA, S&P's SACP, Fitch's SCP) from support and sovereign constraints, and all combine a mostly qualitative country-level institutional assessment with entity-level financial metrics in a two-way matrix. Moody's uses a weighted 1–9 scorecard (20/20/30/30) crossed with the sovereign rating; S&P uses an institutional framework score (1–6) crossed with the equal-weighted average of five individual factors (1–5); Fitch crosses a six-factor risk profile with a financial profile driven by the payback ratio (net debt / operating balance) or, for large regions, the economic liability burden. Only the financial factors are computed from accounts; institutional framework, governance and support are judgements that a shadow rating must take as declared inputs.

#### Cited Findings

**Moody's (RLG BCA scorecard, as printed in a 2015 credit opinion)**
- [V] Scorecard factors, sub-factors and weights: **Factor 1 Economic Fundamentals 20%** — economic strength 70% (value 122.04 for Cape Town, i.e., regional GDP per capita as % of the national average [M for the definition]), economic volatility 30%. **Factor 2 Institutional Framework 20%** — legislative background 50%, financial flexibility 50%. **Factor 3 Financial Performance and Debt Profile 30%** — gross operating balance / operating revenues 12.5%; interest payments / operating revenues 12.5%; liquidity 25%; net direct and indirect debt / operating revenues 25%; short-term direct debt / total direct debt 25%. **Factor 4 Governance and Management 30%**, scored as the **MAX** (weakest) of risk controls and financial management, investment and debt management, transparency and disclosure. Scores run 1 (strongest) to 9. — [Moody's credit opinion, Cape Town, 17 Feb 2015](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)
- [V] Worked example from that page: factor 1 = 1 → 0.20; factor 2 = 5 → 1.00; factor 3 = 3(12.5%) + 5(12.5%) + 1(25%) + 1(25%) + 1(25%) = 1.75 → 0.53; factor 4 = 1 → 0.30; idiosyncratic risk assessment 2.03, rounded to 2; systemic risk assessment Baa2 (the sovereign bond rating); the "BCA matrix" gives a suggested BCA of baa3, against baa2 assigned by committee. The matrix "generates estimated baseline credit assessments from a set of qualitative and quantitative credit metrics" but is "not ... a matrix for automatically assigning or changing these assessments"; results are "backward-looking". — [same](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)
- [V] Extraordinary support bands: low 0–30%, moderate 31–50%, strong 51–70%, high 71–90%, very high 91–100%. The BCA includes ongoing contractual subsidies; extraordinary support is action "to prevent a default". — [same](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)
- [V] Joint default analysis has four inputs: the BCA of the local government, the supporting government's rating, an estimate of default dependence between the two, and an estimate of the likelihood of extraordinary support. — [handbook Box 8.4, after Rubinoff, Bellefleur and Crisafelli 2008](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)
- [M] JDA's arithmetic: with default probabilities p_L (from the BCA) and p_S (sovereign), correlation ρ, joint default p_LS = p_L p_S + ρ √(p_L(1−p_L) p_S(1−p_S)); supported default probability ≈ p_LS + (1 − w)(p_L − p_LS) where w is the support probability; map back to a rating by Moody's idealised default table.
- [M] Moody's republished its RLG methodology in January 2018; whether its scorecard weights or sub-factors changed from those printed above was not verified (the methodology PDF needs registration). Moody's US cities and counties use a separate scorecard (economy, finances, management/institutional, leverage), not this one. — partial support: [search result on Moody's US local GO scorecard, 30/30/20/20](https://www.treasurer.ca.gov/Cdiac/seminars/2019/20190212/day2/6.pdf) [S]

**S&P (Methodology For Rating LRGs Outside the U.S., 15 July 2019)**
- [V] Steps: assess the institutional framework; "establish the individual credit profile based on the **equally weighted average** of five other factors (economy, financial management, budgetary performance, liquidity, and debt burden)"; combine both into the anchor by Table 1; adjust for caps, overriding factors and a holistic view to get the SACP; apply the rating-above-the-sovereign methodology. — [S&P 2019, ¶6](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [V] Institutional framework = predictability 25% + revenue and expenditure balance 50% + transparency and accountability 25% on a five-point scale, converted to six categories: 1 extremely predictable and supportive; 2 very predictable and well-balanced; 3 evolving but balanced; 4 evolving and unbalanced; 5 volatile and unbalanced; 6 very volatile and underfunded. "Mostly qualitative", assessed by country and level of government. — [S&P 2019, ¶13–16](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [V] Table 1 (anchor; rows IF 1–6, columns ICP 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5): IF1: aaa, aaa, aa+, aa, aa−, a, bbb+, bb+, bb− and below. IF2: aaa, aa+, aa, aa−, a+, a−, bbb, bb, b+ and below. IF3: aa+, aa, aa−, a+, a−, bbb, bb+, bb−, b and below. IF4: aa−, a+, a, a−, bbb, bb+, bb−, b, b−. IF5: a, a−, bbb+, bbb, bb+, bb−, b, b−, b−. IF6: bbb+, bbb, bbb−, bb+, bb−, b+, b−, b−, b−. A non-integer ICP falls in a range (e.g., IF3 with ICP 2.2 → 'aa−'/'a+'), resolved by position in range, expected trends, under-reflected characteristics and peers. — [S&P 2019, Table 1, ¶8](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF) (table read from extracted text)
- [V] Table 2 caps and overrides: very weak financial management caps the SACP at 'bb+'; very weak liquidity caps at 'bb+'; both very weak caps at 'b−'; −1 notch each for an excessive debt burden not reflected in the debt score, excessive deficits after capital accounts, and large contingent liabilities (−1 or more); never below 'b−'. A holistic adjustment of ±1 notch follows. The LRG is generally not rated above the sovereign. — [S&P 2019, Table 2, ¶9–11](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [V] Quantitative inputs: budgetary performance from *operating balance / adjusted operating revenues* and *balance after capital accounts / total adjusted revenues*; liquidity from the free-cash DSCR over 12 months plus a qualitative assessment of access to external liquidity; debt burden from *tax-supported debt / consolidated operating revenues* and *interest / adjusted operating revenues*, improved one category when direct debt is less than five years of operating margin, worsened up to two for contingent liabilities. Economy starts from GDP per capita on the sovereign table merged to a 1–5 scale, adjusted for divergence from the national average. Qualitative adjustments are generally one category and at most two. — [S&P 2019, ¶24–26, ¶38–65](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)
- [S] S&P's 2024 US governments criteria "align closely" with this framework and weight economy at 20% within the ICP. — [S&P credit FAQ on US governments methodology, 2024](https://www.spglobal.com/ratings/en/research/articles/240909-credit-faq-a-closer-look-at-the-new-methodology-for-rating-u-s-governments-13240044)

**Fitch (International LRG Rating Criteria, version of March 2026 as applied)**
- [V] "The six key risk factors, combined according to their relative importance, collectively represent the risk profile of the LRG. Risk profile and financial profile assessments, which measure the LRG's debt burden and debt service requirements amid a reasonable economic or financial downturn over the rating horizon, are combined in an SCP. The SCP, together with ... extraordinary support or the rating cap, produces the IDR." The six factors: revenue robustness, revenue adjustability, expenditure sustainability, expenditure adjustability, liabilities and liquidity robustness, liabilities and liquidity flexibility (each Weaker / Midrange / Stronger), with an implied operating-environment score. Risk profile categories: Stronger, High Midrange, Midrange, Low Midrange, Weaker, Vulnerable. — [Fitch, Canton of Zurich, 28 Oct 2026](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf)
- [V] Financial profile thresholds (Fitch's table as reproduced in the report): **economic liability burden** (primary for Type A): aaa ≤ 40%; aa 40–70%; a 70–100%; bbb 100–140%; bb 140–180%; b > 180%. **Payback ratio** (net adjusted debt / operating balance; primary for Type B): aaa ≤ 5x; aa 5–9x; a 9–13x; bbb 13–18x; bb 18–25x; b > 25x. **Synthetic coverage** (secondary): aaa ≥ 4x; aa 2–4x; a 1.5–2x; bbb 1.2–1.5x; bb 1–1.2x; b < 1x. **Fiscal debt burden** (secondary): aaa ≤ 50%; aa 50–100%; a 100–150%; bbb 150–200%; bb 200–250%; b > 250%. Metrics are taken in the "rating case" (a stressed five-year forecast, here 2026–2030). — [same](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf)
- [V] Type A LRGs are those whose "main spending responsibilities cover health, education and social spending, with a material share of general government expenditure and debt, and fiscal imbalances"; their primary metric is the ELB, "strongly related to central government debt". Worked example: ELB 13.3% (aaa), payback 19.8x (bb), synthetic coverage 0.7x (b), fiscal debt burden 34.9% (aaa) → financial profile 'aa'; with a Stronger risk profile the SCP is 'aaa' after peer comparison. An "Analytical Outcome Guidance" matrix maps risk profile × financial profile to a suggested SCP. — [same](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf)
- [M] Type B LRGs (most municipalities) cover debt service from annual cash flow and are assessed primarily on payback; the synthetic annuity is computed on net adjusted debt at the LRG's apparent cost of debt over a fixed notional tenor (15 years, unverified).

#### Inferences
- [M] For a shadow rating, the computable core is: Moody's factor 3 (five ratios → 1–9 scores via thresholds that the scorecard does not print here), S&P's budgetary performance, liquidity and debt burden, and Fitch's financial profile (whose thresholds are verified above). Institutional framework, governance and support must be declared inputs with their source, and the output should say which part was declared.

### 5. Subnational Fiscal Rules and Statutory Early-Warning Schemes
#### Takeaway
Statutory schemes turn two to eight ratios into a classification that changes what a local government may do: Mexico (three ratios → green/yellow/red → a ceiling on net new borrowing of 15%/5%/0% of unrestricted revenue), Colombia (interest / operating savings 40%/60% and debt / current revenue 100% → free, conditioned or prohibited borrowing), Brazil (Senate debt caps plus the Treasury's CAPAG grades A–D, which gate federal guarantees), Spain (net savings sign and debt / current revenue 75%/110% → permitted, authorised, prohibited), Italy (eight parameters, half breached → structurally deficit), Japan (four ratios → early-warning or rebuilding plans), India (net borrowing ceilings in % of GSDP). The design pattern is: declared ratio definitions on the accounts, declared thresholds, a combination rule, and a declared consequence.

#### Cited Findings

**Mexico — Ley de Disciplina Financiera (2016) and the Sistema de Alertas**
- [V] Three indicators (Reglamento art. 6): I *deuda pública y obligaciones / ingresos de libre disposición (ILD)*; II *servicio de la deuda y de obligaciones / ILD*; III *obligaciones a corto plazo y proveedores y contratistas / ingresos totales*. Debt (art. 7) includes simple loans, bonds, the uncovered principal of zero-coupon-backed loans, financial leases, drawn contingent and revolving lines, debt of its entities backed by its ILD (weighted by a published factor), drawn guarantees, contingent debt, the investment-service balance of PPPs, and any other scheduled financial payment. Debt service (art. 8) = principal + interest + fees + financial costs, excluding refinanced or prepaid principal and short-term obligations, plus PPP investment-service payments. ### 6. The Debt Sustainability of Local Governments
#### Takeaway
The sovereign debt-dynamics identity carries over to a local government with the denominator changed: since local GDP is rarely reliable, revenue (or operating revenue) is the natural scale, so the debt ratio moves with the interest rate against revenue growth, less the primary balance as a share of revenue. The debt-stabilising primary balance is d(i − γ)/(1 + γ). Stochastic analysis simulates joint shocks to revenue growth, interest and the primary balance and reports fan charts and the probability of crossing a declared threshold (a statutory cap or an agency band).

#### Cited Findings
- [S] The probabilistic ("fan chart") approach to debt sustainability was proposed by Celasun, Debrun and Ostry (IMF WP 06/67) for sovereigns, deriving fan charts "from the pattern of shocks and the endogenous response of fiscal policy". — [IMF WP catalogue](https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/cat/longres.aspx?sk=18930)
- [S] The IMF's stochastic DSA treats the real interest rate, growth, the real exchange rate and the primary balance as drivers, with shocks drawn from past deviations. — [ESM note on IMF stochastic DSAs, 2023](https://www.esm.europa.eu/system/files/document/2024-01/ESM-20231130%20%28IMF%2C%20Stochastic%20DSAs%29%20v2.pdf)
- [S] The OECD examines subnational vulnerabilities through debt levels and sensitivity to interest-rate changes. — [OECD, *Riding the rollercoaster: Subnational debt in turbulent times*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/riding-the-rollercoaster_c0a7fdc3-en.html)
- [S] Subnational debt in the OECD-UCLG database (2016) ranged from near zero to 67% of GDP (Canada). — [IMF How-To Note 2022](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/howtonotes/2022/english/htnea2022003.pdf.md)
- [V] Fitch measures debt sustainability under a "rating case" that assumes a reasonable economic or financial downturn over a five-year horizon. — [Fitch, Zurich 2026](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf)

**Formulas (standard algebra, my statement)**
- [M] Let D_t be debt at end of year t, R_t revenue (the scale), PB_t the primary balance (revenue − non-interest expenditure, including capital spending), i_t the effective interest rate on D_{t−1}, SFA_t stock-flow adjustments (arrears accumulated, guarantees called, asset sales used for debt). Then D_t = (1 + i_t) D_{t−1} − PB_t + SFA_t. With d_t = D_t/R_t, pb_t = PB_t/R_t and γ_t = R_t/R_{t−1} − 1:
  **d_t = d_{t−1} (1 + i_t)/(1 + γ_t) − pb_t + sfa_t**,
  Δd_t = d_{t−1} (i_t − γ_t)/(1 + γ_t) − pb_t + sfa_t.
- [M] Debt-stabilising primary balance (Δd = 0, sfa = 0): **pb* = d (i − γ)/(1 + γ)**. Payback form: years to repay = d / ob, where ob = operating balance / revenue; Fitch's payback is exactly D/OB.
- [M] Stochastic version: model (γ_t, i_t, pb_t) as a VAR or bootstrap of historical joint deviations, with a fiscal reaction pb_t = α + β d_{t−1} + ε_t; simulate N paths over the horizon; report percentiles of d_t, the probability that d_t exceeds a declared cap (e.g., 1.10 in Spain, 1.2 × RCL in Brazil, 1.00 in Colombia) and the probability that coverage falls below κ_min. Local revenue's dependence on transfers means γ should be decomposed (own revenue elasticity to local activity; transfer rule's dependence on national revenue).

#### Inferences
- [M] For a local government whose revenue is mostly formula transfers, the relevant shock is a change in the transfer rule or the national tax pool, not local GDP; the stochastic model should allow regime shifts and contingent-liability calls (SOE debt, PPP guarantees, pension shortfalls), which S&P and Mexico both fold into debt.

### 7. Fiscal Capacity and Effort
#### Takeaway
Fiscal capacity is what a jurisdiction would raise if it applied a standard (usually national-average) set of effective rates to its own bases (the representative tax system, RTS); effort is actual own revenue over that capacity. Where bases are not measured, capacity is predicted by regressing revenue on income and structural variables. Equalisation transfers fill a share of the gap between standard capacity and each jurisdiction's capacity, often net of expenditure need measured by a representative expenditure system.

#### Cited Findings
- [S] Bahl's approach "appl[ies] average effective rates to a standard set of tax bases"; "taxable capacity is defined ... as the total tax amount that would be collected if each [unit] applied an identical set of effective rates to the selected tax bases"; data limits lead to "subjectively defined tax base proxies", and rates can be set a priori or from observed data. — [Bahl, on measuring tax effort (GSU ScholarWorks)](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e26349ad-a251-43da-b823-ac8006d9a68a/content)
- [S] Equalisation "often entail[s] the use of a representative tax system or standardised costs to compute equalisation entitlements". — [OECD, *Fiscal Federalism 2022*](https://oecd.org/en/publications/fiscal-federalism-2022_201c75b6-en/full-report/component-5.html)
- [S] The World Bank's 2024 Indonesia work uses a Representative Tax System and a Representative Revenue System. — [World Bank, Indonesia insight paper 2024](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099012524034514173/pdf/P1741581e7b9550501a7cf16d8a83aff345.pdf)
- [V] The handbook's France Table 8.2 compares each municipality's "tax potential" and "tax pressure" (actual or potential) with its stratum average; the MFSA uses *tax pressure = tax receipts / tax potential*. — [handbook pp. 394, 428](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)

**Formulas (standard, my statement)**
- [M] RTS: for bases k with measured base B_ik in jurisdiction i and actual collections T_ik, the representative rate t̄_k = Σ_i T_ik / Σ_i B_ik; capacity C_i = Σ_k t̄_k B_ik; per capita c_i = C_i/P_i; **effort e_i = Σ_k T_ik / C_i** (and by base, T_ik / (t̄_k B_ik)).
- [M] Regression-based capacity: estimate own revenue per capita y_i = f(x_i) + u_i, with x_i = income per capita, urbanisation, sectoral composition, property values; capacity ĉ_i = f̂(x_i), effort e_i = y_i / ĉ_i (a stochastic-frontier variant takes the frontier, not the mean, as capacity).
- [M] Gap-filling equalisation: G_i = θ · max(0, c̄ − c_i) · P_i with standard c̄ (national average, or the average of a reference group, as in Canada's ten-province standard), θ the share equalised; with needs, G_i = θ · max(0, (n_i − c_i)) · P_i where n_i is a representative expenditure need (Australia's Commonwealth Grants Commission assesses both sides). The incentive problem: a jurisdiction raising its own rate on a base it dominates lowers its entitlement little if t̄ uses all jurisdictions' rates, but a lot if it is large.

#### Inferences
- [M] Capacity and effort belong in the assessment of a single city only as comparisons with a declared peer group (its stratum, its state's municipalities); a module should compute them from a panel, never from one city's accounts.

#### Gaps
- No primary methodological text (ACIR 1962/1986; Martinez-Vazquez and Boex's World Bank module) was read.

### 1. The Aggregation of Dimension Scores into Indicator Scores in PEFA 2016 (M1, M2 and "+")
#### Takeaway
PEFA 2016 scores each of its 94 dimensions on an ordinal A–D scale. A dimension earns a level only when every requirement of that level is met. Each multidimensional indicator is aggregated within itself by a declared method:
- **M1, weakest link:** the minimum, with "+" added if any other dimension scores higher.
- **M2, averaging:** a fixed lookup table over the multiset of dimension scores.

Nothing is aggregated across indicators. I found that the M2 table is exactly a step function of the arithmetic mean of the codes D=1, C=2, B=3, A=4, with rounding that is not uniform. It therefore assumes the levels are equally spaced, while M1 needs only their order.

#### Cited Findings
**Structure**
- PEFA 2016 has 31 performance indicators in 94 dimensions, grouped into seven pillars [V] ([PEFA 2016 brochure](https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/PEFA_2016_Framework_Final_WEB_0.pdf)):
  1. budget reliability;
  2. transparency of public finances;
  3. management of assets and liabilities;
  4. policy-based fiscal strategy and budgeting;
  5. predictability and control in budget execution;
  6. accounting and reporting;
  7. external scrutiny and audit.
- Scale: "Each dimension is scored separately on a four-point ordinal scale: A, B, C, or D, according to precise criteria established for each dimension." [V] ([PEFA Framework 2016, Part 2 §2.1](https://web.archive.org/web/20210423214147/https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/PEFA%202016_latest%20version_with%20links%20%282%29.pdf))

**Level semantics: all requirements must be met**
- "In order to justify a particular score for a dimension, every aspect specified in the scoring requirements must be fulfilled. If the requirements are only partly met, the criteria are not satisfied and a lower score should be given that coincides with achievement of all requirements for the lower performance rating." [V] (same, §2.1)
- "A score of C reflects the basic level of performance for each indicator and dimension, consistent with good international practices. A score of D means that the feature being measured is present at less than the basic level of performance or is absent altogether, or that there is insufficient information to score the dimension." [V] (same)
- A is "warranted if evidence clearly demonstrates that an internationally-recognized level of good performance is achieved." [V] ([brochure](https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/PEFA_2016_Framework_Final_WEB_0.pdf))

**Special values**
- **D\*:** "A score of D due to insufficient information is distinguished from D scores for low-level performance by the use of an asterisk--that is, D\*. The aggregation of multidimensional indicators containing D\* scores is no different from aggregation with other D scores. Aggregate indicator scores will not include an asterisk, and thus the insufficiency of information is only noted at the dimension level." [V] (§2.1.1)
- **NA:** "In cases where one or more dimensions of a multidimensional indicator are not applicable, the assessor proceeds as if the 'not applicable' dimensions did not exist." A D on one dimension can force NA on others; the example is PI-26.1, no internal audit function, making PI-26.2–26.4 NA. [V] (§2.1.2)
- **NU:** "it may be decided for certain reasons that a particular indicator will not be used … 'NU' is entered instead of a score." [V] (§2.1.2)
- "The use of NA and NU must be justified in the PEFA report. Assessments that score less than two-thirds (21) of the PEFA indicators should be referred to as 'partial PEFA assessments'." [V] (§2.1.2)

**Standard quantifiers.** PEFA fixes these words throughout [V] (§2.1.4):

| Term | Share by value |
|---|---|
| All | 90 percent or more |
| Most | 75 percent or more |
| Majority | 50 percent or more |
| Some | 25 percent or more |
| A Few | less than 25 percent and more than 10 percent |

**M1, weakest link** [V] (§2.2):
- Use: "used for multidimensional indicators where poor performance on one dimension is likely to undermine the impact of good performance on other dimensions of the same indicator."
- Steps:
  1. "Each dimension is initially assessed separately and given a score on the four-point calibration scale."
  2. "The aggregate score for the indicator is the lowest score given for any dimension."
  3. "Where any of the other dimensions score higher, a '+' is added to the indicator score. Note: It is NOT acceptable to choose the score for one of the higher-scoring dimensions and add a '-' for any lower scoring dimensions."

**M2, averaging** [V] (§2.2):
- Use: "The aggregate indicator score awarded using this method is based on an approximate average of the scores for the individual dimensions of an indicator, as specified in a conversion table (table 1). Use of this method is prescribed for selected multidimensional indicators where a low score on one dimension of the indicator does not necessarily undermine the impact of a high score on another dimension of the same indicator."
- Steps: find the section for two, three or four dimensions; "Identify the row in the table that matches the scores for each dimension of the indicator; the ordering of the dimension scores does not matter"; "Enter the corresponding overall score for the indicator."
- Limits: "The conversion table applies to indicators using M2 (AV) scoring methodology only. Using it for indicators designated for M1 (WL) will result in an incorrect score. The conversion table is intended for use on individual indicators only and is not suitable for aggregating scores across the full set, or subsets, of indicators. No standard methodology has been developed for aggregation across indicators because each indicator measures a different subject and has no standard, quantitative relationship with other indicators." [V]

**What I found by computing on Table 1** [V, computed]:
- **M2 is a function of the number of dimensions and the sum of the codes D=1, C=2, B=3, A=4, and of nothing else.** Every pair of multisets with the same n and the same mean receives the same score.
- **The step maps** from mean to score are:
  - n=2: the exact mean in half-steps (1.5→D+, 2.5→C+, 3.5→B+).
  - n=3: 1.33→D+, 1.67→D+, 2.33→C+, **2.67→B**, 3.33→B+, **3.67→A**.
  - n=4: **1.25→D**, 1.75→D+, **2.25→C+**, 2.75→B, 3.25→B+, 3.75→A.
- **The rounding is not uniform.** Below C, means round down to the half-step (1.67→D+, 1.25→D, 1.75→D+), except DDC at 1.33→D+. From C upward they round up (2.67→B, 3.67→A, 2.25→C+).
- **Two properties hold.** The table never lowers the result when one dimension improves (it is monotone), and it always lies between the minimum and maximum dimension score.

**Quality assurance and evidence in the report** [V] (Part 3):
- Box 1.1 records the assessment's oversight team, assessment manager and team, and the reviewers of the concept note and draft report, "in particular the PEFA Secretariat".
- For each dimension the report "indicates the factual evidence, including quantitative data, which has been used to substantiate the assessment", notes any "issues in relation to the timeliness or reliability of data", and states explicitly where information was insufficient.
- Performance change since a previous assessment is reported with "evidence for each dimension and indicator score compared with the previous score" and with comparability issues disclosed.

#### Inferences
- **M1 is ordinally meaningful [M, by Roberts's definition, §3].** min(x₁,…,xₙ) commutes with every strictly increasing recoding f, since min f(xᵢ) = f(min xᵢ), and the "+" flag ("some xᵢ > min") is invariant as well. M1 therefore needs only the order of A > B > C > D.
- **M2 needs equal spacing [M, from the computation above].** It is a step function of the arithmetic mean of equally spaced codes. Statements such as "indicator X scores higher than indicator Y under M2" are not invariant under strictly increasing recodings.
  - Example: {D, A} → C+ beats {C, C} → C, since the mean is 2.5 against 2.0.
  - Recode D = −10 with C = 2, B = 3, A = 4. The order is preserved, yet {D, A} now averages −3 and falls below {C, C}.
  - PEFA limits the damage by confining M2 to dimensions it judges independent within one indicator, and by forbidding aggregation across indicators.
- **D\* is a value plus a flag.** It aggregates exactly like D, and the flag is dropped at indicator level. Software should keep the flag in the trace even though the rule discards it.
- **NA works as deletion before lookup.** An indicator with one NA dimension of four uses the three-dimension rows. A deletion rule should be declared per rule.
- **Each PEFA dimension is a cumulative (Guttman-type) scale.** Requirement sets are nested by level and all must hold, so "level = the highest level whose requirements are all met". That is itself a conjunctive, non-compensatory rule inside the dimension.

### 2. The Definition and Summary of Scores in the UNDRR Disaster Resilience Scorecard for Cities, with the City Resilience Index, the LGAF and Local Indices
#### Takeaway
The UNDRR Scorecard (2017 version) has two levels:
- **Preliminary:** 47 questions on the Ten Essentials, each scored 0–3 with anchored descriptors.
- **Detailed:** 117 criteria, each scored 0–5 with descriptors specific to the criterion, often cumulative counts ("at least 8 of the 10 listed groups").

Results are summarised by averaging or consensus at the level of each Essential and by a "final percentage score" that excludes criteria judged not relevant. Cities may apply their own weights across the Essentials. The Scorecard itself warns that scores are subjective and "not comparable across different cities".

I found no published critique aimed specifically at the averaging; this is a gap. The CRI scores 156 questions 1–5, qualitatively and quantitatively, and declines to produce a single overall score. The LGAF copies PEFA's A–D pre-coded statements, which panels of experts decide collectively.

#### Cited Findings
**UNDRR Disaster Resilience Scorecard for Cities (May 2017)**

#### Inferences
- **The UNDRR averages and percentages assume equal spacing [M].** The "average … at the level of each of the Ten Essentials" and the "final percentage score" both treat 0–5 (or 0–3) as equally spaced and compensatory across criteria. They are therefore not meaningful in Roberts's sense when the anchors are only ordered, which is how they are written (for example "framed around clear presentation" against "robust risk assessment methodology").
- **The percentage's denominator varies with relevance [M].** Excluding "not relevant" criteria means two cities' percentages rest on different criterion sets, compounding the Scorecard's own warning against comparing cities.
- **Some anchors are counts and some are bands [M].** Count anchors ("at least 8 of 10 groups") and frequency bands are ordinal codings of an underlying count or rate. Software could keep the count itself and derive the level by the declared thresholds, so that no information is lost.
- **The CRI is two scores per question [M].** A judged 1–5 score anchored at worst and best cases, and a 1–5 score derived automatically from a metric by a standardised scale (a declared threshold function). Declining a single overall score is the CRI's own non-compensatory choice at the top.
- **The LGAF reaches its scores by deliberated consensus [M].** It does not record independent ratings that could later be measured for agreement.

### 3. Meaningful Aggregations of Ordinal Scores in Roberts's Sense, and the Non-Compensatory Rules Used in Practice
#### Takeaway
A statement about scores is meaningful when its truth survives every admissible transformation of the scales. For ordinal scales the admissible transformations are all strictly increasing maps.

- **Meaningful on one common ordinal scale:** comparisons of medians, order statistics, minima and maxima, counts at or above each level, threshold, lexicographic and Pareto rules, and lookups defined on the levels themselves.
- **Not meaningful:** comparisons of arithmetic means and sums, and so of averages, percentages of the maximum, and PEFA's M2.
- **When each dimension has its own independent ordinal scale,** essentially only dictatorial (projection) rules survive.

The OECD/JRC Handbook states that weights in linear and geometric aggregation are trade-offs, not importance. It recommends non-compensatory multi-criteria rules (Condorcet–Kemeny–Young outranking) when weights are meant as importance.

#### Cited Findings
- **The scale types.** The OECD/JRC Handbook (2008) reproduces the measurement-theoretic hierarchy [V] ([OECD/JRC Handbook, pp. 52–53](https://www.oecd.org/sdd/42495745.pdf)):
  - "A fundamental point in measurement theory is that of the uniqueness of scale, i.e. which admissable transformations of scale allow for the truth or falsity of the statement involving numerical scales to remain unchanged (problem of meaningfulness). In the case of an ordinal scale, it is unique up to a strictly monotone increasing transformation".
  - An interval scale is "unique up to a positive affine transformation".
  - For ordinal numbers, interpreting arithmetic "as a quantitative statement about the property … is not possible for ordinal-scale numbers but can be done for differences between interval-scale numbers"; "the fewer the admissible transformations of a scale, the more meaningful the statements involving that scale."
- **Stevens (1946)** introduced the nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio scales with their "permissible statistics", the median and percentiles for ordinal data [M] ([Stevens, Science 103:677–680](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.103.2684.677), not read).
- **Roberts (1979)** defines a statement as meaningful if its truth value is unchanged when every scale in it is replaced by an admissible transformation [M] (*Measurement Theory with Applications to Decisionmaking, Utility, and the Social Sciences*, Addison-Wesley, not read). The Handbook cites Roberts (1979) for the claim that weights with ordinal criterion scores mean importance coefficients [V] ([Handbook p. 105](https://www.oecd.org/sdd/42495745.pdf)).
- **Marcus-Roberts and Roberts (1987), "Meaningless Statistics"** [S] ([J. Educational Statistics 12(4):383–394](https://ideas.repec.org/a/sae/jedbes/v12y1987i4p383-394.html); secondary summaries):
  - Computing a mean on an ordinal scale is not itself forbidden, but conclusions drawn from it may not be meaningful.
  - The mean of Group I can exceed that of Group II on the original scale and fall below it after an admissible transformation.
  - Comparing medians is meaningful, since the order is preserved under every admissible transformation.
- **Ebert and Welsch (2004), "Meaningful environmental indices: a social choice approach"** [S] ([JEEM 47(2):270–283](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jeeman/v47y2004i2p270-283.html), abstract):
  - A meaningful index is one whose underlying ordering is independent of admissible transformations of the variables.
  - Arithmetic-mean indices are mostly not meaningful.
  - For ratio-scale variables the meaningful index is a geometric mean.
  - The result for ordinal, non-comparable variables (only dictatorial rules) is from memory [M].
- **Aggregation theory beyond Ebert and Welsch** [M] (not read):
  - Aczél and Roberts (1989), "On the possible merging functions" (*Mathematical Social Sciences* 17), characterise the merging functions that are meaningful by scale type.
  - Orlov (1981), Ovchinnikov (1996, "Means on ordered sets", *Math. Soc. Sci.* 32) and Marichal (2002, "On order invariant synthesizing functions", *J. Math. Psych.* 46) show the following. The continuous, idempotent functions whose comparisons are invariant when one strictly increasing map is applied to all arguments are the lattice polynomials. The symmetric ones are exactly the order statistics, which include the median, min and max. Weighted medians and the Sugeno integral are their weighted analogues.
- **Compensability** [V] ([OECD/JRC Handbook, pp. 32–33](https://www.oecd.org/sdd/42495745.pdf)):
  - "In both linear and geometric aggregations, weights express trade-offs between indicators. A deficit in one dimension can thus be offset (compensated) by a surplus in another. This implies an inconsistency between how weights are conceived (usually measuring the importance of the associated variable) and the actual meaning when geometric or linear aggregations are used."
  - "To ensure that weights remain a measure of importance, other aggregation methods should be used, in particular methods that do not allow compensability. Moreover, if different goals are equally legitimate and important, a non-compensatory logic might be necessary."
  - "A non-compensatory multi-criteria approach (MCA) could assure non-compensability by finding a compromise between two or more legitimate goals. In its basic form this approach does not reward outliers, as it retains only ordinal information".
  - It "could be computationally costly when the number of countries is high" (Munda and Nardo 2007).
- **The Handbook's definitions** [V] ([OECD/JRC Handbook, pp. 104–105](https://www.oecd.org/sdd/42495745.pdf)):
  - "Compensability refers to the existence of trade-offs, i.e. the possibility of offsetting a disadvantage on some criteria by a sufficiently large advantage on another criterion … a preference relation is non-compensatory if no trade-off occurs".
  - "the use of weights with ordinal criterion scores originates in non-compensatory aggregation procedures and gives the weights the meaning of importance coefficients (Keeney & Raiffa, 1976; Podinovskii, 1994; Roberts, 1979)".
  - Following Vansnick (1990), the compensatory and non-compensatory approaches derive from Borda (1784) and Condorcet (1785).
  - Arrow's theorem means "no perfect aggregation rule may exist".
- **The non-compensatory rule (NCMC)** [V] ([Handbook §6.13, pp. 112–113](https://www.oecd.org/sdd/42495745.pdf)):
  - "weights in additive aggregations necessarily take the meaning of substitution rates (trade-offs) and do not indicate the importance of the associated indicator"; "For the weights to be interpreted as 'importance coefficients' … non-compensatory aggregation procedures must be used".
  - NCMC (Munda 1995; Roy 1996; Vincke 1992) computes an outranking matrix of pairwise comparisons, eⱼₖ = Σ_q [w_q(Pr_jk) + ½ w_q(In_jk)], summing the weights of indicators on which j beats k plus half of those tied. It then ranks by the Condorcet–Kemeny–Young–Levenglick procedure.
  - The worked example on the Technology Achievement Index notes that a compensatory equal-weight rule "would imply that it is acceptable to renounce … 2% of patents … in exchange for a 2% increase in electricity consumption."
- **Munda and Nardo, and Greco et al.** Munda and Nardo (2009), "Noncompensatory/nonlinear composite indicators for ranking countries: a defensible setting" (*Applied Economics* 41) is the journal version of the method [M]. Greco, Ishizaka, Tasiou and Torrisi (2019) review weighting, aggregation and robustness and warn that composite indices "could sweep methodological issues in their underlying framework under the carpet" [S] ([Social Indicators Research 141:61–94](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11205-017-1832-9), abstract).
- **Non-compensatory rules in practice** [V] for each instance (sources above):
  - **PEFA M1:** the minimum, with a "+" flag.
  - **PEFA dimensions:** all requirements must be met for a level, a conjunctive and cumulative rule.
  - **PEFA across indicators:** a refusal to aggregate.
  - **UNDRR detailed criteria:** cumulative threshold anchors.
  - **LGAF:** pre-coded statements, a holistic choice of one anchored description.
  - **CRI:** a refusal to give a single overall score.
  - **OECD/JRC:** NCMC by outranking.

#### Inferences
- **Two conditions decide meaningfulness [M].**
  - Whether the dimensions share one ordinal scale whose levels are comparable across dimensions. PEFA's A–D is calibrated to a common meaning (C = the "basic level … consistent with good international practices"), so a case can be made for comparable ordinal scales within a scheme.
  - Whether the statement compares objects or reports a value. Reporting "the median dimension score is B" is meaningful; reporting "the mean is 2.6" is not.
- **Rules admissible on comparable ordinal levels [M]:**
  - min (weakest link) and max;
  - the k-th order statistic, including the median, with a declared tie rule for even n (lower or upper median);
  - the profile vector of counts at each level, and cumulative counts ("number of dimensions at B or above"), invariant under any relabelling that keeps the order;
  - threshold rules ("at least k of n at level ≥ B", "no dimension below C");
  - lexicographic rules (first by the minimum, then by the count at the minimum, and so on, as a leximin on levels);
  - Pareto dominance between profiles, reported as a partial order with incomparability kept;
  - weighted median and Sugeno integral, whose weights are importance and not trade-offs;
  - lookup tables declared directly on multisets of levels.
- **A meaningful lookup table need not be meaningful in its comparisons [M].** A table is defined on symbols, but conclusions such as "this indicator improved more than that one" may still rest on an implicit numeric spacing. PEFA's M2 is a function of the code sum, so it inherits the interval assumption. A tool can detect this: test whether a declared table is a function of the sum under some equally spaced coding, and if it is, label the rule interval-dependent.
- **Rules that need interval scales [M]:** sums, means, percentages of the maximum score (UNDRR's), weighted linear indices, and PEFA M2. With mere ratio scales (positive quantities with a true zero, such as the CRI's quantitative metrics before banding), the geometric mean is meaningful for comparisons (Ebert and Welsch).

#### Gaps
- Roberts (1979), Stevens (1946), Aczél and Roberts (1989), Marichal (2002), Ovchinnikov (1996) and the full texts of Munda and Nardo and of Greco et al. were not read; their theorems are stated from memory [M] and should be checked before they are cited in a theory note.
- I did not verify whether PEFA's documentation states the equal-spacing assumption behind M2 explicitly. "Approximate average" is as far as the 2016 text goes [V].

### 4. The Recording of the Evidence behind Each Score, and the Measurement of Agreement among Assessors
#### Takeaway
All four schemes ask for evidence per score: PEFA most strictly, then LGAF and UNDRR.
- **PEFA:** factual evidence, data-quality issues and insufficiency are stated per dimension; D\* marks insufficient information; draft reports are peer-reviewed with the reviewers named.
- **LGAF:** "collective and motivated" panel rankings, optionally checked by sampling.
- **UNDRR:** recorded justification "will enable validation".

None of the schemes I read measures agreement among assessors statistically; disagreement is resolved by consensus, review and moderation. Krippendorff's α with the ordinal difference function suits independent ordinal ratings with missing values.

#### Cited Findings
- **PEFA** [V] ([PEFA Framework 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/20210423214147/https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/PEFA%202016_latest%20version_with%20links%20%282%29.pdf)):
  - The report must give for each dimension "the rationale for its scoring", indicate "the factual evidence, including quantitative data, which has been used to substantiate the assessment … specific wherever possible, for example, in terms of quantities, dates, and time spans", note "issues in relation to the timeliness or reliability of data and evidence", and state where "insufficient information has been obtained either for a whole indicator or one of its dimension".
  - D\* marks a D from insufficient information at the dimension level (§2.1.1).
  - NA and NU must be justified (§2.1.2).
  - Sampling "should explain the reason for the use of sampling and justify the sampling approach they adopt" (§2.1.5).
  - Box 1.1 records the oversight team, the assessment team, and the invited and actual reviewers of the concept note and draft report, including the PEFA Secretariat, with dates.
- **PEFA performance change** [V] (same): "Presentation of evidence for each dimension and indicator score compared with the previous score", "Highlighting comparability issues … so that the robustness of the evidence of change is fully disclosed", and "Explanation of changes in performance that may not be captured by a change of the score, but is nevertheless evidenced".
- **UNDRR** [V] ([Preliminary](https://preparecenter.org/wp-content/sites/default/files/unisdr_disaster_resilience_scorecard_for_cities_preliminary.pdf)):
  - "Recording your justification for each evaluation score will enable validation, as well as future revisions and tracking of progress".
  - Each Essential lists "Data you will need".
  - Cities "decide on their own 'evidence'" from UNISDR's suggested types.
  - Disagreement is resolved in workshops ("an average or consensus score is applied at the level of each of the 'Ten Essentials'"; "Enable dialogue and concensus between key city stakeholders").
- **LGAF** [V] ([Burns et al. 2010](https://www.fig.net/resources/proceedings/fig_proceedings/fig2010/papers/ts03a/ts03a_burns_deininger_et_al_4640.pdf)):
  - Expert investigators assemble the legal and statistical evidence as "briefing material for the Panels of Expert".
  - Panels give "a collective and motivated ranking".
  - Sampling may "confirm panel opinions with evidence".
  - Panels have three to eight members, with consensus "in a participatory and deliberative process" [S] ([World Bank LGAF page](https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/land-governance-assessment-framework)).
- **CRI.** Questions are answered "from a team composed of experts from various fields" [S] (search summary of [Arup CRI](https://www.arup.com/projects/city-resilience-index)). The qualitative 1–5 score is anchored by written best and worst cases, and the quantitative score is automated from a metric [V] ([CRI booklet](https://mercociudades.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/CRI-total-Booklet.pdf)).
- **Krippendorff's α** [S] ([Wikipedia, "Krippendorff's alpha"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha), a secondary source):
  - α = 1 − D_o/D_e, the observed disagreement over that expected by chance.
  - It applies to any number of coders and to incomplete data, where unpairable values are dropped.
  - Its ordinal difference function is δ²(v, v′) = (Σ_{g=v}^{v′} n_g − (n_v + n_{v′})/2)², which uses only rank frequencies and so is invariant under strictly increasing recodings.
  - Common practice is to "rely on data with reliabilities α ≥ 0.800", to "consider data with 0.800 > α ≥ 0.667 only to draw tentative conclusions", and to "discard data whose agreement measures α < 0.667". The minimum should depend on the cost of wrong conclusions.
- **Other agreement measures.** Weighted κ (Cohen 1968) and Gwet's AC2 are alternatives for ordinal ratings [M].

#### Inferences
- **The schemes end in one consensus score [M].** None of the schemes keeps the independent ratings that agreement statistics need. Moderated scores cannot be audited for assessor reliability afterwards.
- **A design that measures reliability [M]** has assessors score independently first and keeps those ratings sealed. It then computes ordinal α per criterion, and over the scheme, before moderation. The moderated score is kept as a separate act with its reasons, as the LGAF's "motivated ranking" and the UNDRR's justification already do.
- **Calibration [M].** Assessors score shared anchor cases before scoring; a calibration set gives an α of its own. The PEFA Secretariat's review of draft reports (Box 1.1) acts as moderation by a third party. The PEFA CHECK endorsement criteria are a formalised version [M; not read].

#### Gaps
- The PEFA CHECK quality-endorsement criteria, PEFA training and certification of assessors, and any published inter-rater study of PEFA, UNDRR, CRI or LGAF scores were not found or read.
- I found no source on how the Scorecard workshops reach a consensus (voting, moderation rules, recorded dissent).

### 1. The OECD–Lincoln Taxonomy of Instruments and the Data the Global Compendium Reports on Their Use
#### Takeaway
The Compendium's "OECD-Lincoln taxonomy" has five instruments: infrastructure levy, developer obligations, charges for development rights, land readjustment and strategic land management. It deliberately excludes property and land taxes, tax increment finance, joint ventures and PPPs. Its data come from an expert questionnaire in 60 countries, not from fiscal accounts. It reports counts of countries using each instrument, legal bases, appeals, who administers, valuation methods and obstacles. It reports almost no comparable revenue figures. Only 23% of countries base the total of an infrastructure levy on actual land value increments; the rest base it on cost.

#### Cited Findings
**Scope and taxonomy**
- **Coverage.** 60 countries, data collected 2020–2021 by an online questionnaire of over 350 queries, completed by academic experts. Experts were asked to describe "typical scenarios of LVC use in the country within the last 10 years". [V] — [OECD Compendium, Box 1.2](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)
- **Regions and income.** 27 countries (45%) in Europe, 12 (20%) Asia-Pacific, 11 (18%) Americas, 10 (17%) Middle East and Africa. 35 (58%) high-income, 11 (18%) lower-middle-income, 2 (3%) low-income. [V] — [OECD Compendium, Table 1.1 text](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)
- **Exclusions.** The taxonomy "does not include land and property taxes", since these are "usually levied in a uniform manner without distinguishing property owners that are affected by public interventions". Likewise "joint ventures, public-private partnerships, or tax increment finance are not included … due to difficulties in delineating their role in capturing value increments." [V] — [OECD Compendium, ch. 1](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)
- **The five definitions**, quoted [V] — [OECD Compendium, ch. 1](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf):
  - **Infrastructure levy:** "a tax or fee levied on landowners possessing land that has gained in value due to infrastructure investment initiated by the government". The government identifies the catchment. The amount "should be based on the amount of land value benefit obtained", paid once or over time. Other names: betterment contributions, betterment levies, special assessments.
  - **Developer obligations:** "a cash or in-kind payment designed to defray the costs of new or additional public infrastructure and services private development requires", triggered by the developer's application. They are negotiated or set by formula, and include impact fees, negotiated exactions, development charges and inclusionary zoning.
  - **Charges for development rights:** "cash or in-kind contributions payable in exchange for development rights or additional development potential above a set baseline". They require predefined baseline and maximum densities. Related terms: sale of development rights, sale of air rights, transfer of building rights.
  - **Land readjustment:** "pooling fragmented land parcels for joint development, with owners transferring a portion of their land for public use to capture value increments and cover development costs"; also called land pooling.
  - **Strategic land management:** governments "buying, developing, selling and leasing land to advance public needs and recoup value increments". "If governments acquire land at predevelopment prices, they can fully capture increases in land value". Leasing captures increments "through higher rents".
- **Equivalences across names.** Colombia's betterment levies are "similar in scope to 'special assessments' in the United States". Israel's and Poland's "infrastructure levies" are in fact developer obligations. [V] — [OECD Compendium](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)

Lower-income countries rely more on strategic land management and land readjustment; higher-income countries use developer obligations "much more frequently". [V] — same source

- **Legal basis.** Over 80% of countries using infrastructure levies and land readjustment have a legal basis for them. About one third of countries have no legal basis for charges for development rights or strategic land management. **Valuation and obstacles**
- **Valuation.** Market-based land valuation is used in 69% of all countries, 74% of high-income, 77% of upper-middle-income and 46% of lower-middle and low-income countries. Lower-income countries resort more to non-market approaches because cadastres are poor. [V] — same source, Fig. 1.8

"Charges are too high" is cited by 31% vs 19%, and an inadequate legal framework by 18% vs 45%. "By far the most common" obstacle outside the list was political will. [V; percentages read from Fig. 1.9 in the order printed, 58/31/35/6/19 high-income and 63/18/68/48/45 middle and low] — same source

**How charges are set**
- **Infrastructure levy.** It is applied mostly to transport, then utilities and public space. "Only 23% of countries estimate the amount of total fees based on actual land value increments"; the rest base it on cost. Fees are assigned to plots mostly "according to a score … calculated using known characteristics" (area, zoning, density, taxable value, distance). France and the UK use land area alone. [V] — same source, Fig. 1.13
- **Developer obligations.** Over 90% of countries using them apply them to new development; over 60% apply them to higher-density applications. 79% define the impact area as within the development's boundary. They are assigned more often by established rule than by negotiation. [V] — same source, Fig. 1.14
- **Land readjustment.** Over 80% of countries using it use it for urban expansion. Plots are "most commonly" reallocated proportionally to the value of the original plots. Indonesia, Italy and Türkiye use area alone; Chile and China use value and area. Israel and Hong Kong, China use "vertical" readjustment, allocating units in buildings. Consent varies: all owners, a share (typically two thirds; Colombia and Korea a simple majority of 51%), or none. Portugal expropriates if full consent fails. [V] — same source, Fig. 1.16

**Country figures relevant to instrument design** [V] — [OECD Compendium, country profiles](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)
- **Colombia.** The infrastructure levy (contribución de valorización, legal basis since 1921) "usually amounts to the estimated total cost of public works". It is sometimes proportional to the increment. Up to 30% of administrative costs may be added, though usually less than 10% is charged. The levy is allocated by a formula on distance, location, size, quality and value. Participación en plusvalías recovers "between 30% and 50% of the land value increase resulting from changes in land-use regulations or building indexes". It has faced problems because increases are hard to estimate, and the largest cities have strengthened developer obligations instead.
- **Germany.** Urban development contracts (städtebauliche Verträge) can require up to 30% affordable units, as in Frankfurt. Erschließungsbeiträge "can amount to 90% of public costs". For road renewal, 25%–75% of costs are passed to benefiting owners depending on road type. In Umlegung, owners "typically" give up to 25% in greenfield, more in brownfield; owners are compelled to participate.
- **India.** The betterment levy "can vary from 30% to 50% of the increase in value of land, depending on the state Acts", and among the states with provisions it "has only been implemented in Gujarat", where a uniform rate per m² recovers the scheme's cost. Land readjustment reserves "23% to 50% of the readjusted plots" for public improvements. Excess floor area is charged at "usually between 30% to 70%" of market value. "Public entities conduct analyses to define the contribution ratio that maintains the proportionality of landowners' share before and after pooling."
- **Korea.** Land is redistributed "after deducting a share for public improvements and another one for future sales or leases". "100% of the cost of public improvements related to land readjustment projects is recovered through the sale or lease of those plots". Privately led projects need consent of owners representing 1/2 of owners and 2/3 of land area.
- **Spain.** Reparcelación urbanística rests on Land Act 7/2015 arts. 13.2.c and 14.c, with details in 17 regional laws, and is used "frequent[ly]". "The burden imposed on landowners cannot be larger than the benefits of the project." Owners receive a plot of proportional value as close as possible to the original. The infrastructure levy (contribución especial) is used rarely.
- **United Kingdom.** "Developer obligations effectively capture about 30% of development value on greenfield sites, notably for large builders … developers … can shift the costs to landowners."
- **Hong Kong, China.** All land is leased. Leases run 75–99 years historically and 50 years for new leases. The land premium is usually set by auction, plus annual rent of 3% of rateable value. Lease revenue was 19%–27% of the yearly budget in 2016–2019. Lease modification charges for changes of use or density are "calculated according to the estimated land value gains". In 2018-19 they were 27.2% of lease revenue and 5.4% of the budget. Lease renewal charges have "not been successful".

#### Inferences
- The taxonomy suits a module's top-level type: five instruments, plus TIF and recurrent property or land taxes as two further types the Compendium excludes but the request asks for. Its two axes are who triggers the charge (government action for the levy and readjustment; developer initiative for obligations and rights charges) and what base it is levied on (cost, value increment, area or a score). These are natural fields of a declared instrument. [M]
- The Compendium's data are categorical, expert-reported frequencies, not revenues or capture rates. A module cannot calibrate capture rates from it; it can only supply legal parameters such as statutory shares and consent thresholds. [M]
- **A conflict to keep visible.** The general section says Korea requires a simple majority (51%), while the Korea profile says 1/2 of owners and 2/3 of area for private projects. Probably different project types. [M]

### 2. The Mechanics of Land Readjustment: Contribution Ratio, Reserve Land, Replotting, Value Preservation, Settlement Money and Feasibility
#### Takeaway
Land readjustment is a pair of accounting identities.
- **Area.** Public land before + private land before = public land after + private land returned + reserve land.
- **Value.** Each owner's returned plot, plus or minus settlement money, must be worth at least the original plot, and in proportion to it. The contribution ratio d = (P + R)/A must be matched by a unit-value uplift y with (1 − d)·y ≥ 1. The project is feasible when reserve-land sales, subsidies and contributions cover costs.

Germany caps the area deduction in law (30% greenfield, 10% elsewhere) and settles the excess advantage in money. Gujarat levies half the increment, computed in practice from low administered values. Korea's Act set 50% returned, 30% infrastructure and 20% cost-recovery land.

#### Cited Findings

**Definitions and formulas (from the Japanese literature, reported as literature)** [V] — [De Souza, JICA-RI ch. 1](https://www.jica.go.jp/jica-ri/publication/booksandreports/l75nbg00000ltoak-att/LAND_READJUSTMENT_Chapter1_web.pdf)
- **Contribution ratio (減歩率, genbu ritsu):** "The percentage difference in private property area before and after replotting". It serves "(i) to adjust the demand for land required for proper urbanization (public infrastructure), and (ii) to create supply to – partially or fully – finance project costs."
- **Reserve land (保留地, horyūchi):** "plots of land to be sold to finance the land readjustment projects". Their quantity is set by an economic criterion (financing new public facilities) and an equity criterion (equitably balancing the value increase). Small owners may contribute money instead of land.
- **Statutory value cap (LRL art. 96.2):** "The equivalent value of the land contribution shall not exceed the percentage stipulated on the increase of the total value of private land after the project". Revenue from reserve land in public projects may be used only for the project.
- **Settlement money:** "The equity is money to be collected and paid to clear imbalance for the replot value, if any." Land evaluation is used "to judge the contribution for reserve land, calculate compensation for loss in damage, calculate the replotting area, and calculate equity collection and payment". Japanese practice uses a street-value method: a unit value per street, adjusted by street, accessibility and plot coefficients.
- **The area identity and contribution ratio.** With M = public area before, N = public area after, A = private area before, E = private area returned, R = reserve land:
  - M + A = N + E + R
  - P = N − M (net increase in public area)
  - E = A − P − R
  - d = (P + R)/A = P/A + R/A
- **The proportional ratio (value preservation):**
  - Pr = V′/V = (E·e)/(A·a) = (1 − d)·y, where a and e are the average unit values before and after (private land excluding reserve land) and y = e/a.
  - "Equal balance" requires Pr = 1. For example, d = 33.3% requires y = 1.5.
  - *Caveat:* the source's prose defines y as "previous land price divided by posterior land price", which contradicts its own arithmetic; y = e/a is meant.
- **Worked example (Misato Chuo, 114.8 ha):**
  - Public area 14.0% → 32.5%.
  - A = 987,667 m², P = 213,201 m², R = 160,137 m², E = 614,329 m².
  - d = 21.6% + 16.2% = 37.8%.
  - a = ¥151,000/m², e = ¥295,000/m², so y = 1.954 and Pr = (1 − 0.378)·1.954 = 1.215.
  - Feasibility: expenditure ¥61,561m. Revenue was 57% reserve-land sales (¥35,092m) and 41% national, prefectural and municipal subsidies (¥25,349m), plus other revenue of ¥1,120m. The station's construction cost was excluded.
  - I recomputed the identities from the table and they hold. [V]
- **Hold-outs.** The chapter frames LR against the hold-out problem: owners demanding more than their true reservation value in voluntary assembly (citing Miceli and Sirmans 2007). [V for the framing]

**Germany: Umlegung under the Baugesetzbuch** [V] — [§58 BauGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bbaug/__58.html); [§57 BauGB](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bbaug/__57.html)
- **Area-based distribution (§58).** The area contribution (Flächenbeitrag) offsets the advantage the readjustment brings. It may be "nur bis zu 30 vom Hundert der eingeworfenen Fläche" in areas first developed and "nur bis zu 10 vom Hundert" elsewhere. An advantage beyond what the capped area covers must be compensated in money. The authority may take money instead of area in whole or part. Money values are taken at the date of the Umlegungsbeschluss.
- **Value-based distribution (§57).** The mass is distributed "in dem Verhältnis, in dem die zu berücksichtigenden Eigentümer an der Umlegung beteiligt sind". "Jedem Eigentümer soll ein Grundstück mindestens mit dem Verkehrswert zugeteilt werden" of the former plot, valued at the date of the Umlegungsbeschluss. "Unterschiede zwischen den so ermittelten Verkehrswerten sind in Geld auszugleichen."
- **Practice.** Typical greenfield contribution is up to 25%, more in brownfield. Participation is compulsory and expropriation rare. [V] — [OECD Compendium, Germany](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)

**Spain: reparcelación**

**India: Gujarat's town planning schemes (TPS)**

  - "the increment in land value is worked out which is the addition of the development costs to the original land values times two.   - "But the negative impact of this is low quality infrastructure and the authority not being able to tap into the land value gains."

[V] — [OECD Compendium, Colombia](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)

#### Inferences
- **A general value-based LR model** for a module (my synthesis of §57 BauGB, LRL art. 96 and the Gujarat F form) [M]:
  - Let parcel i have entry value V_i (before, at the valuation date) and allocated value V′_i (after, at the same date's market conditions, with the project).
  - Entitlement: V*_i = V_i · (ΣV′ / ΣV), proportional shares of the distributable value.
  - Settlement s_i = V′_i − V*_i (positive: the owner pays; negative: the owner is paid).
  - Value preservation requires V*_i ≥ V_i, equivalently ΣV′ ≥ ΣV, which aggregates to (1 − d)·y ≥ 1 on private land.
  - The public's capture is the value of P + R. Reserve land is the part convertible to cash: R·e_R.
- **Feasibility:** costs C ≤ R·e_R·(1 − selling costs) + subsidies + cash contributions + betterment levied. With project duration T (10–20 years in Gujarat), revenues and costs must be discounted. [M]
- **An area-based model (§58)** is a special case: d_i ≤ d̄ (30%/10%), with the excess advantage max(0, ΔV_i − value of the area taken) collected in money. [M]
- **Gujarat's practice** shows that the statutory share (50%) and the effective capture can diverge by an order of magnitude when the valuation basis is administered rather than market. A module must keep "statutory rate", "valuation basis" and "effective capture" as separate quantities. [M, from the V findings]

### 3. The Computation of Tax Increment Financing Revenue and Its Documented Risks
#### Takeaway
TIF freezes a district's base assessed value B. It earmarks the tax on the increment I (all overlapping jurisdictions' combined rate) to the district authority for a term, usually 15–30 years, often to service bonds. It creates no new tax; it reallocates. The "but-for" test is the legal claim that the increment would not exist without the district. It is close to pro forma.

The best-known evidence (Illinois) finds that municipalities adopting TIF do not grow faster overall. Commercial TIF districts cannibalise commercial growth elsewhere in the same city, and overlapping jurisdictions (school districts) bear most of the diverted revenue.

#### Cited Findings

**Mechanics** [V] — [Dye and Merriman 2006](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/tax-increment-financing-lla060102.pdf)
- **Value accounting.** B = base-year assessed value in the district, N = base-year value outside it, I = increment in the district, G = growth outside.
  - "Before-TIF value = before TIF local government tax base = B + N"
  - "after-TIF value = B + N + I + G"
  - "after-TIF tax base available to local governments = B + N + G"
  - "TIF district authority's tax base = I"
- **Revenue.** The district "gets revenues from the increment times the combined tax rate for all local governments together". The Illinois-like example rates are municipal 0.15%, school 0.60%, others 0.25%, combined 1.00%. "For each 15 cents of its own would-be tax revenues the municipality puts on the line, the school district and other local governments contribute another 85 cents."
- **Designation and history.** Designation usually requires a finding of "blighted" or "underdeveloped" and that development would not occur "but for" the subsidy. "It is only a bit of an overstatement to characterize the 'blight' and 'but for' findings as merely pro forma exercises, since specialized consultants can produce the needed evidence in almost all cases." TIF is authorised in 48 states, dates to California in 1952, and expenditures are often debt-financed.
- **Base and term (FHWA).** The base revenue keeps flowing to the general fund; revenue above the benchmark, "or a designated portion of it", goes to a special account. Revenues increase "NOT because of higher tax rates". After termination all revenue returns. FHWA's page states district life inconsistently as 15–20, 15–30 and 25–30 years. [V] — [FHWA, "Essential Nexus, Rough Proportionality, and But-For Tests"](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ipd/value_capture/resources/value_capture_resources/essential_nexus/tax_increment.aspx)
- **TIF is not value capture as such.** "TIFs can similarly direct a portion of funds captured by the property tax toward specific public purposes, but they are not in themselves an additional means of land value capture." [V] — [Germán and Bernstein 2018](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/land-value-capture-policy-brief.pdf)
- **Windfalls.** FHWA calls TIF "revenue segregation". It estimates landowners may keep 60–80% of publicly created land value, and that TIFs, "unlike special assessments, exactions, or impact fees", impose no additional burden on benefiting properties. [V] — [FHWA](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/ipd/value_capture/resources/value_capture_resources/essential_nexus/tax_increment.aspx)

**Risks and evidence**
- **Self-financing.** A 1998 PPIC study of 38 California districts found "only four TIF districts … to generate enough new revenue to be self-financing". [V, as quoted by FHWA]
- **Courts.** Illinois and Missouri appellate cases (Burr Ridge 2003, Malec v. Belleville 2011, Castel Properties v. Marion 1994, JG St. Louis West v. Des Peres 2001) turned on unsupported blight findings and undocumented but-for findings, with deference to boards in Missouri. [V via fetch summary] — same source
- **Illinois evidence** [V] — [Dye and Merriman 2006](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/tax-increment-financing-lla060102.pdf):
  - **First study** (Dye and Merriman 2000, 235 Chicago-area municipalities, two-stage selection correction with the inverse Mills ratio): "property values in TIF-adopting municipalities grew at the same rate as or even less rapidly than in nonadopting municipalities"; the offset "seemed to come from smaller growth in non-TIF area".
  - **Second study** (2003): 246 six-county municipalities, 1,242 Illinois municipalities and 247 TIF districts.
    - With controls, six-county: "no positive impact of TIF adoption on the growth in citywide property values. Any growth in the TIF district is offset by declines elsewhere."
    - All-county sample: "a significantly negative impact of TIF adoption on growth in overall available (non-TIF) property values".
    - "Commercial and industrial TIF districts both show a significantly negative impact on growth in commercial assessed values outside the district."
  - **Raw means, six-county** (100 adopters / 146 non-adopters): pre-adoption 1980–84 gross EAV growth 4.66 vs 4.41% per year; post-adoption 1995–98 5.20 vs 6.46; adopters' net-of-TIF growth (G/N) 5.06.
  - **Raw means, 102-county** (205 / 1,037): 3.31 vs 1.86 pre; 6.27 vs 7.60 post; adopters' available EAV growth 5.19.
  - **The 247 districts:** average base about $11 million; district EAV growth averaging 24% a year against 3% in the host municipalities; most growth in the first years, declining about 1 point a year. The TIF–city growth relation is U-shaped, turning positive at host growth of about 6%.
- **Reverse causality.** "Anticipated growth in property values could lead to TIF adoption if municipalities attempt to capture revenues from overlapping governments." [V] — same source
- **Wisconsin.** Kashian, Skidmore and Merriman examine whether TIF-district growth comes at the expense of the rest of the city. [S — not read] — [search result](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/1319_KSM20Final.pdf)

#### Inferences
- **Revenue identity for a module** [M]:
  - R_t = s · τ_t^combined · max(0, AV_t^district − B), where s is the designated share (≤ 1) and τ the sum of all overlapping rates, unless some jurisdictions are exempted ("pass-through").
  - PV = Σ_{t=1..T} R_t/(1+r)^t.
  - Bond capacity ≈ PV / debt-service coverage. [M]
- **Diversion identity.** Let β ∈ [0, 1] be the share of I that is truly "but-for". Then the diversion from overlapping jurisdiction j in year t is D_{j,t} = τ_j · (1 − β) · I_t, and the true net new revenue is τ · β · I_t. Displacement within the city (lower G) is a second loss, −τ · ΔG. A module should require β and ΔG as declared assumptions or estimates, never default β = 1. [M]
- Capture of "uplift" by TIF is bounded by the effective property-tax rate on value over the term. With a 1% combined rate on capital value and r = 5% over 23 years, PV ≈ 13.5% of a permanent increment; so TIF captures at most a small share of the value uplift. This is consistent with FHWA's 60–80% retained. [M, arithmetic mine]

#### Gaps
- Merriman's later Lincoln report on improving TIF, the GAO and other state audits, and studies of TIF's school-finance effects were not read.
- Comparative evidence on capture rates of TIF versus special assessments was not found.

### 4. The Mechanics of Levies, Developer Obligations, Development Rights and Leasing
#### Takeaway
- **Betterment levies and special assessments** apportion a total (usually the project's cost, sometimes a share of value gain) across a benefit zone by a formula.
- **Impact fees, development charges and planning obligations** are per-unit charges at permission.
- **Charges for development rights** price floor area above a basic FAR, by formula (Brazil's "virtual land" method) or by auction (São Paulo's CEPACs, traded securities with zone conversion factors).
- **Leasing** captures through an auctioned premium, annual rent and modification premiums (Hong Kong, China), and in rail-plus-property through granting rights at "before-rail" prices and selling at "after-rail" prices.

#### Cited Findings

**Betterment levies and special assessments**
- **Definition.** "Owners of select properties pay the municipality a fee, which defrays the cost of a public improvement or service from which the owner specifically benefits." [V] — [Germán and Bernstein 2018](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/land-value-capture-policy-brief.pdf)
- **Colombia's valorización:** total ≈ cost of works plus up to 30% administration (usually under 10%), apportioned by distance, location, size, quality and value. [V] — [OECD Compendium](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)
- **Revenue weight** [V] — [Smolka 2013, ch. 3](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/implementing-value-capture-in-latin-america-full_1.pdf):
  - Betterment levies typically account "for much less than 1 percent of own local revenues": Mexico ≤ 0.42% of municipal revenues; Brazil in 2011 0.25% of fiscal revenues and 6.8% of property-related tributes; Rosario 0.30%.
  - Outliers: Medellín in 1968, 45% of all local public expenditures; Cali in the early 1980s, 30%; Bogotá in 1993, 24% of local revenues; Bogotá recently, about US$1 billion of works.
  - Cuenca (Ecuador): almost US$200 per capita over 10 years against Bogotá's US$150. 90% of households paid within four years; 95% of projects collected 60% from contributions; 3% non-compliant.
  - "More than 50 percent of Medellín's main road grid was paid for with betterment levies."
  - In Brazil, 2,890 municipalities (52.5%) collected some betterment revenue; in only 667 of 5,505 did it exceed 10% of property tributes.
- **Buenos Aires** (Law 23.514 of 1987): for a 40 km subway line, a 5% addition to all property taxes plus a 2.4% surcharge within 400 m of stations. The fund received about US$750 million in 2012. [V] — same source
- **Germany:** road renewal 25–75% to benefiting owners; Erschließungsbeiträge up to 90% of public costs. [V] — [OECD Compendium](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)
- **US apportionment** methods (front-foot, area, zone of benefit, assessed value) and the legal "special benefit" requirement are standard. [M — not verified in a source read here]

**Colombia's participación en plusvalía (a charge on regulatory uplift)**
- **Rate.** Councils set a rate that "podrá oscilar entre el treinta (30%) y el cincuenta (50%) del mayor valor por metro cuadrado". Bogotá phased it in from 30% (2004) to 50% (2006 onward). [V] — [MinVivienda guía](https://www.minvivienda.gov.co/sites/default/files/documentos/guia-para-la-participacion-en-plusvalia.pdf); [Smolka 2013](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/implementing-value-capture-in-latin-america-full_1.pdf)
- **Triggering acts (art. 74, Ley 388):** conversion of rural to urban land, changes of zoning or use, and higher density. **Exigibility:** only at a building-licence application, change of use or transfer, with discounts of 5% and 10% in specified cases. [V] — [MinVivienda guía](https://www.minvivienda.gov.co/sites/default/files/documentos/guia-para-la-participacion-en-plusvalia.pdf)
- **Relation to valorización.** Art. 87 contemplates plusvalía from public works only where valorización was not used to finance them, an explicit rule against double charging. [S] — [search of academic article on plusvalía vs valorización](https://repository.urosario.edu.co/bitstreams/2a89d543-0b7c-4fcd-b38c-4cf85be54569/download)

**Developer obligations: impact fees, development charges, s.106/CIL**
- **San Francisco** impact-fee revenue: US$96.1m (FY2013-14), US$90.2m (FY2014-15), US$236.9m (FY2015-16). [V] — [Germán and Bernstein 2018](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/land-value-capture-policy-brief.pdf)
- **Florida** collected over US$2 billion in FY2005–06, more than 5% of revenues in 48 counties and 28% of local revenues in large counties such as Orange (Burge 2010). [S] — [MHCLG 2018-19 study page](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/section-106-planning-obligations-and-the-community-infrastructure-levy-in-england-2018-to-2019-report-of-study)
- **Legal limits (US).** The essential nexus and rough proportionality tests (Nollan 1987, Dolan 1994, extended to monetary exactions by Koontz 2013) limit exactions to the impact of the development. [M — FHWA's page did not define them]

**Charges for development rights: OODC and CEPACs (Brazil)**
- **Legal basis.** Federal Law 10,257/2001 (Statute of the City) codified the onerous grant of the right to build (Outorga Onerosa do Direito de Construir, OODC) above a basic FAR, and CEPACs. [V] — [Germán and Bernstein 2018](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/land-value-capture-policy-brief.pdf)

  - **Faria Lima** (2004–2010): seven auctions at US$550–2,000 per CEPAC; 682,669 offered, 638,074 bought; US$723 million.

### 5. The Estimation of Value Uplift
#### Takeaway
Uplift is estimated by hedonic regression of prices on distance or station zones, by before-and-after comparison, and by quasi-experimental designs (difference-in-differences, with selection correction). Meta-analyses find modest average residential premia near rail (a few percent; about 2.6% per 250 m nearer) and larger commercial premia, with very wide dispersion. Specification matters: omitting highway access biases rail effects upward, and non-parametric "zone" comparisons overstate them.

#### Cited Findings

**Debrezion, Pels and Rietveld (working paper)** [V] — [TI 2004-023](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/04023.pdf)
- **Effect per distance.** "The mean impact of a station on property value (rent) for every 250 metres close to the station is 2.61%", ranging from −12.84% to +38.70% (n = 73).
- **Within ¼ mile (Table 5).**

  | Group | Mean | n |
  |---|---|---|
  | Overall | 8.9% | 70 |
  | Residential | 6.2% | 49 |
  | Commercial | 15.2% | 21 |
  | Commuter rail | 19.4% | 15 |
  | Heavy rail/metro | 5.3% | 28 |
  | Light rail | 7.6% | 23 |
  | BRT | 2.5% | 4 |

- **Meta-regression (within ¼ mile, unweighted).** Commuter rail +0.1845 (t = 2.633) relative to light rail; commercial +0.1064 (t = 1.798); R² = 0.13, 73 observations.
- **Specification effects.** "Models with highway accessibility on average report 4% lower railway station proximity effects", insignificant in the ¼-mile specification. Semi-log specifications may give "on average, in a 12% increase". Non-parametric studies "attribute the differences in property value solely to station proximity, so that result may be overstated". No temporal or spatial effect was found.
- **Conclusion.** "When highway accessibility is not explicitly addressed, railway impacts on property values tend to be overestimated."
- **Published version.** The 2007 *JREFE* version (35(2): 161–180) is summarised as a station-zone premium of about 4.2% for the average residence and 16.4% for the average commercial property. [S] — [RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:kap:jrefec:v:35:y:2007:i:2:p:161-180)
- *Caveat:* the working paper and the published abstract differ in wording on short-distance effects. I read only the working paper.

**Other meta-analyses and case evidence**
- **Mohammad, Graham, Melo and Anderson (2013),** *TR-A* 50: 158–170: 23 studies, 102 observations. Purchase price and rent changes are statistically similar, and there is little effect of property characteristics. Moderators: land use, rail type, system maturity, distance, location. [S, abstract only] — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transa/v50y2013icp158-170.html). Claims that commuter rail exceeds light rail by about 24–25 points and that Europe and East Asia exceed North America by about 9 points come from an AI-written wiki. [unverified — do not use]
- **Rio de Janeiro (Barra da Tijuca):** tunnels, an expressway and Lúcio Costa's plan were "associated with an increase in land value of 1,900 percent from 1972 to 1975", against about 435% in other high-value areas of the city. A crude before-after comparison with a comparison group. [V] — [Smolka 2013](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/implementing-value-capture-in-latin-america-full_1.pdf)
- **Selection.** Dye and Merriman correct for selection into treatment (TIF adoption) by a first-stage adoption model and the inverse Mills ratio. They warn that growth inside a targeted district says nothing about causation. [V] — [Dye and Merriman 2006](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/tax-increment-financing-lla060102.pdf)

**Valuing regulatory uplift and practical estimation**
- **Theory versus practice.** In theory the value of rights to FAR 3 versus a baseline of 1 is "the difference between the residual values of their respective highest and best uses". In practice "no two buildings in the area are the same and changes in some plots affect the highest and best use for nearby plots"; higher density is not always more profitable. [V] — [Smolka 2013](https://www.lincolninst.edu/sites/default/files/pubfiles/implementing-value-capture-in-latin-america-full_1.pdf)
- **Consensual increments.** In development-based LVC "the increment in land value is calculated using a method agreed by stakeholder consensus". With taxes or fees "the accuracy of the estimated incremental value is often challenged". [V] — [Suzuki et al. 2015](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/468551468165570019/pdf/936860PUB00ISB0TransportDevLV0final.pdf)
- **Valuation infrastructure.** Its lack is the main barrier to moving from area-based to value-based land readjustment. [S] — [search summary of Yomralioglu](https://web.itu.edu.tr/tahsin/PAPERBOX/B06.pdf)

#### Inferences
- **Uplift must be counterfactual** [M]:
  - ΔV = V(with) − V(without) at the same date.
  - A before-after comparison adds the market trend: ΔV_BA = ΔV + trend.
  - Difference-in-differences subtracts a comparison area's change, but announcement effects (anticipation) shift the "before". Displacement from untreated areas (as in TIF) contaminates the control group.
  - A module should store the estimator, the comparison set, the dates (announcement, construction, opening) and the uncertainty with each uplift estimate.
- **Meta-analytic magnitudes for screening only** [M]:
  - Residential: about 2–3% per 250 m nearer, or about 6% within ¼ mile.
  - Commercial: about 15%.
  - Commuter rail higher.
  - The dispersion (−13% to +39%) means a project-specific estimate is needed.

#### Gaps
- Mohammad et al.'s coefficients and later meta-analyses (e.g., the 2022 *TR-A* meta-analysis applying a transit-planning lens) were not read.
- No source on the share of uplift that is anticipation versus realised.
- No primary studies on the uplift from BRT or roads used for levies.

### 6. Empirical Capture Rates and Incidence by Instrument
#### Takeaway
Capture rates are rarely measured against a counterfactual uplift; most figures are statutory shares or cost-recovery ratios.
- **Statutory shares:** 30–50% for Colombia's plusvalía and India's betterment levies; 50% of the increment in Gujarat, but administered values push effective capture far lower; Spain's 5–15% of edificabilidad; Germany's area cap of 30%.
- **Estimated or reported:** about 30% of development value captured by developer obligations on English greenfield sites; FHWA's view that owners keep 60–80% of publicly created value; Korea's 100% recovery of public-improvement cost by reserve land.
- **Incidence:** impact fees appear to be borne by landowners (land prices fall about $1 per $1 of fee) while house prices rise. TIF's incidence falls on overlapping jurisdictions and on the untreated parts of the city.
- **Failures documented:** speculation (Korea); under-recovery and delay (Gujarat; over 10 years for most schemes); unsold or under-priced rights (Curitiba); TIF cannibalisation (Illinois). I found no primary quantitative evidence on displacement caused by these instruments.

#### Inferences
- Three different ratios are reported under the name "capture rate", and a module must keep them apart [M]:
  - κ = captured / counterfactual uplift (true capture);
  - ρ = revenue / project cost (cost recovery);
  - σ = statutory share × (valuation basis / market value) (effective statutory capture).
- **Gujarat** shows σ much lower than the statutory 50%. **Korea** shows ρ = 1 with κ unknown. **CEPAC auctions** price the rights at market (high κ on the regulatory uplift of the rights sold, but none on existing floor space).
- **Incidence differs from the statutory payer.** Fees levied on developers fall largely on landowners (land prices) and, in Dade County, partly on buyers. TIF falls on overlapping jurisdictions. A module should hold an incidence split as a declared or estimated parameter. [M]

- No systematic cross-instrument study of κ against counterfactual uplift was found. Not read: Medda (2012), Mathur, Crook–Henneberry–Whitehead.
- No primary quantitative evidence on displacement or gentrification caused by LVC instruments specifically.
- The England £7bn figure was not checked against the MHCLG report itself.

## 5. A Synthesis across the Parts

The four practices look unrelated: a study tour, a debt ceiling, a resilience scorecard and a land pool. Read together, they share one architecture and one set of failures. Each applies a **declared scheme** (a results framework, a ratio's definition and threshold, an ordinal scale and its aggregation rule, a statute's share and consent threshold) to **records of uneven standing**, and each reports results that are routinely read as more than they are. The sections below follow the distinctions of §0.1 and then the places where the Parts meet.

### 5.1 Each Field Applies a Declared, Versioned Scheme to Evidence-Bearing Records

In learning programmes the scheme is a results chain with closed codebooks at almost every link: three capacity areas with six, eight and six indicators in the CDRF's 2009 version and five, eight and six in WBI's 2011 version, a closed typology of intermediate outcomes, ten instruments and four classes of activity, with indicators that always carry a threshold and a time window ("Six weeks after the exchange, 80% of participants will have taken at least two concrete steps…") (Part I §1) [V] ([CDRF 2009](https://documents.worldbank.org/curated/en/482971468188374127/pdf/80632-WP-Capacity-Development-and-Results-Framework-Box-377295B-PUBLIC.pdf); [AoKE 2015](https://www.apccolombia.gov.co/sites/default/files/2022-06/WB%20%282015%29%20The%20art%20of%20knowledge%20exchange.pdf)). In municipal finance the scheme is a tuple of ratio definitions bound to a chart of accounts, thresholds, a combination rule and a legal consequence. Mexico's regulation even names the chart-of-accounts items its third ratio uses (Part II §5) [V] ([Reglamento del Sistema de Alertas](https://www.nl.gob.mx/sites/default/files/migrate-files/12.-_reglamento_del_sistema_de_alertas.pdf)). In scorecards it is a tree of statements, ordered levels with anchors, special values (D\*, NA, NU) and an aggregation rule at each node, including declared refusals to aggregate (Part III §1) [V]. In value capture it is an instrument defined by mechanism (who triggers the charge, what base it is levied on, what share and what consent the statute fixes), as the OECD–Lincoln taxonomy defines it (Part IV §1) [V].

Two consequences follow, both verified in the Parts. First, **schemes change, and a number is meaningless without the version it was computed under.** Colombia's stock threshold is 100% of current revenue since Ley 2155 de 2021 [V] and was 80% before [S]; Brazil's CAPAG changed in 2017, 2022 and 2023 [S]; Italy's parameters are set for each three-year period [V] (Part II §5); and the outcome typology split coalitions from networks in 2011 and folded them back in 2015 (Part I §1) [V]. Second, **the scheme is always someone's**: a World Bank guide, a statute, an agency's committee, a framework's secretariat. The infrastructure's practice of saying "no standard … is any jurisdiction's until a scheme states it" fits all four fields without change [M].

### 5.3 Before-and-After Comparisons Overstate, and Attribution Needs a Counterfactual and Named Rivals

Each Part contains a result that was read causally from a comparison that cannot bear it, and a result obtained with a design that can.

- **Learning programmes.** URBACT's evaluation credits the programme with "an almost nine-fold increase" in integrated plans on before-and-after self-report with no comparison group [V] ([URBACT III impact evaluation](https://urbact.eu/sites/default/files/2023-01/uiii-impact-evaluation.pdf)). Against it, Lot, Goyal and Hoppe's staggered difference-in-differences on more than 700 OECD cities found "no statistically significant relationship between C40 membership and CO2 emissions" [V] ([Lot, Goyal and Hoppe 2025](https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/do-transnational-municipal-networks-accelerate-the-net-zero-trans/)). Neither reading settles what learning did: the first has no counterfactual, and the second tests an outcome so far downstream that the intermediate link it did find, raised ambition, is skipped (Part I §3) [M for the reading].
- **Value capture.** The "but-for" finding that licenses tax increment financing is, in Dye and Merriman's words, close to "merely pro forma exercises", and their selection-corrected Illinois studies find that TIF-adopting municipalities grew no faster, with growth in the district "offset by declines elsewhere" [V] ([Dye and Merriman 2006](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/2024/04/tax-increment-financing-lla060102.pdf)). Barra da Tijuca's 1,900% rise in land value in 1972–1975, against about 435% in the city's other high-value areas, is a crude before-and-after with a comparison group [V], and rail premia are overstated when highway access is omitted or when zones are compared without controls [V] ([Debrezion et al.](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/04023.pdf)) (Part IV §3, §5).
- **Municipal finance.** The analogue is in projection, not attribution: projections of historical trends "may ignore or hide potential balloon payments", so debt capacity must be tested over the whole repayment period [V] ([handbook p. 356](https://www.uclg-localfinance.org/sites/default/files/Municipal%20Finances%20A%20Handbook%20for%20Local%20Governments.pdf)) (Part II §3).

The parallel between the first two is exact, and it is this survey's inference [M]. The TIF district's **but-for share β**, the fraction of the increment that would not exist without the district, plays the role that Mayne's "accounting for other influencing factors" plays in a contribution claim: both measure the share of an observed change that the intervention caused, and both are dangerous when silently set to 1. A module in either field must store, with every causal or uplift claim, its counterfactual estimator, its comparison set, its dates (announcement, start, opening, end of engagement) and an interval, and must refuse to default β, or the absence of rivals, to the claimant's favour.

### 5.4 Ordinal Rules Survive Recoding, While Averages and Weighted Sums Carry a Hidden Interval Assumption

Part III's central result is computed and exact: PEFA's averaging table M2 is **a function of the number of dimensions and the sum of the codes D = 1, C = 2, B = 3, A = 4 and of nothing else**, with rounding that is not uniform (down below C, with one exception, and up from C) [V, computed] ([PEFA Framework 2016](https://web.archive.org/web/20210423214147/https://www.pefa.org/sites/pefa/files/resources/downloads/PEFA%202016_latest%20version_with%20links%20%282%29.pdf)). It therefore assumes equally spaced levels, while the weakest-link rule M1 needs only their order (Part III §1, §3) [M for the meaningfulness argument]. The OECD/JRC Handbook says the same of composite indices in general: in linear and geometric aggregation "weights express trade-offs between indicators", not importance [V] ([OECD/JRC Handbook](https://www.oecd.org/sdd/42495745.pdf)).

The same test applies to the schemes of Part II, which the finance literature does not examine in these terms. Table 5.2 sorts the combination rules found across the Parts by the scale they need. The classification of the Part II rules is this survey's inference [M]; the rules themselves carry the markers of their Parts.

First, **one checker of meaningfulness serves all four modules**: it tags each declared rule as order-only or interval-dependent, recomputes interval-dependent comparisons under strictly increasing recodings, and reports a counterexample where one exists (PEFA's {D, A} against {C, C} under D = −10, Part III §1) [M]. Second, the agencies already know their scorecards are not ratings: Moody's matrix suggested baa3 for Cape Town and the committee assigned baa2, and the matrix is "not … a matrix for automatically assigning or changing these assessments" [V] ([Moody's credit opinion](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)). A shadow rating computed from an interval-dependent scorecard is doubly provisional, and must say so (Part II §4).

### 5.5 Who Judges Decides What a Result Is Worth

Self-assessment runs through all four fields. The MFSA "relies on municipal staff themselves" [V]; UNDRR's Scorecard is scored by the city, which may weight the Essentials as it wishes, and the Scorecard declares its scores "not comparable across different cities" [V] ([UNDRR, preliminary level](https://preparecenter.org/wp-content/sites/default/files/unisdr_disaster_resilience_scorecard_for_cities_preliminary.pdf)); URBACT's implementation figures come from cities' answers to its surveys [V]; and the highest rating in a self-assessment of 27 city-to-city partnerships went to peer learning [S] (Part I §3; Part II §1; Part III §2). Self-assessment has real virtues, which the sources name: the MFSA "puts city officials in the driver's seat", and in the Scorecard "the conversation is often as important as the score" [V]. But **satisfaction is not a result**: in the IEG's evaluation over 90% of respondents found their training interesting, and only about half of the training produced substantial change in the workplace [V] ([IEG 2008](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/6312)) (Part I §3).

The methods that raise a result's standing all bring in someone other than the claimant. Outcome harvesting asks substantiators "positioned outside the change agent organization" to grade both the outcome and the contribution as fully agree, partially agree or disagree [V] ([Outcome Harvesting brief](https://cnxus.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/wilsongrau_en_Outome20Harvesting20Brief_revised20Nov202013.pdf)); MSC records the criteria of each selection and verifies the stories selected [V]; PEFA names its reviewers, the Secretariat among them [V]; the LGAF's panels give "a collective and motivated ranking" [V]; and the agencies' committees override their own scorecards [V]. Yet **none of the scorecards read measures agreement among assessors statistically**: disagreement is resolved by consensus, review and moderation, and the independent ratings that an agreement statistic needs are not kept (Part III §4) [M, from the schemes read]. Value capture has its own version of the same choice. In development-based capture "the increment in land value is calculated using a method agreed by stakeholder consensus", while tax-based estimates are "often challenged" [V] ([Suzuki et al. 2015](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/468551468165570019/pdf/936860PUB00ISB0TransportDevLV0final.pdf)), and appeals are most frequent against the infrastructure levy and land readjustment [V for the ranking] (Part IV §1, §5).

The common requirement is a record of **who made each claim or score, how independent they are of the party credited, whether the value is an independent rating or a consensus, and the grade of each evidence item by its source type**. The CDRF's weaker and stronger evidence methods (Part I §1) [V] and PEFA's per-dimension evidence with its D\* flag (Part III §4) [V] are ready-made grading schemes.

### 5.6 Time Enters as Follow-up Windows, Horizons, Validity Intervals and Durations

Time is handled carelessly in each field in a different way.

- **Follow-up windows.** AoKE sets surveys at six weeks, checks at one, two and three months and at three, six and nine months, and a follow-up at six to nine months [V]; URBACT's follow-up came three years after closure [V]. "Time to action" is never measured as a duration with censoring, only as a threshold ("within three months") or a snapshot (Part I §4) [M for the absence, from the sources read].
- **Horizons.** The MFSA projects five years ahead [V]; Fitch measures its financial profile in a stressed five-year "rating case" [V]; and the debt-capacity test must run over the whole life of the loan [V] (Part II §1, §3, §4). Ratios are averaged over windows that differ by scheme: four quarters in Mexico [V], a weighted three years in CAPAG [S], seven years in Poland [M] (Part II §5).
- **Validity intervals** of schemes, as in §5.1.
- **Durations and valuation dates.** Most finished Gujarat schemes took more than ten years [V for the counts]; TIF districts last 15–30 years [V, stated inconsistently]; German readjustment values every plot at the date of the *Umlegungsbeschluss* [V]; CEPACs are sold in tranches, and Água Espraiada's average price rose from US$172 in 2004 to US$636 in 2012 [V] (Part IV §2, §3, §4).

A module therefore makes **dates first-class**: every quantity carries its date or window, every follow-up records who did not answer, every scheme its validity interval, and every comparison of cash flows a declared discount rate and price level. Time to action becomes a censored duration with abandonment as a competing risk (Part I §4) [M].

### 5.7 The Parts Meet: Captured Value Enters the Accounts, and Lessons Travel Only with Their Support Factors

The four practices are not only parallel; they feed one another. The links below are this survey's inferences [M] from findings the Parts verify.

- **Captured value enters the accounts, mostly as capital revenue.** S&P's operating revenues "exclude capital revenues, such as capital subsidies and proceeds from asset sales" [V] ([S&P 2019](https://www.maalot.co.il/Publications/MT20200715130530.PDF)) (Part II §2). Reserve-land sales in land readjustment, auctions of development rights and lease premiums are therefore capital revenue and do not raise the operating balance on which coverage-based debt capacity rests, while recurrent forms (betterment levies paid over time, annual lease rent, TIF increments) may. The scale can be large: lease revenue was 19%–27% of the yearly budget of Hong Kong, China in 2016–2019 [V] ([OECD Compendium](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2022/06/global-compendium-of-land-value-capture-policies_64f3c257/4f9559ee-en.pdf)) (Part IV §1). A value-capture module should hand its cash flows to the finance module classified as operating or capital, recurrent or one-off.
- **TIF reallocates other governments' revenue.** In Dye and Merriman's illustration, "for each 15 cents of its own would-be tax revenues the municipality puts on the line, the school district and other local governments contribute another 85 cents" [V] (Part IV §3). The overlapping jurisdictions' fiscal capacity and debt dynamics (Part II §6–§7) are changed by a decision they did not take, and a TIF bond's capacity is the present value of the increment divided by a coverage floor, the same solver as Part II §3.
- **Fiscal capacity rests on property bases that value capture changes.** The representative tax system applies standard rates to measured bases (Part II §7) [S for the method]; uplift raises the base, and equalisation that fills a share of the gap in capacity can return part of a jurisdiction's capture to the system.
- **The finance assessment and the scorecards share PEFA.** The MFSA's financial-management step checks six areas drawn from PEFA [V] (Part II §1), and PEFA's own rules are Part III §1's subject. The LGAF, PEFA's sibling for land, assesses the land governance on which value-based readjustment depends: market-based valuation is used in 69% of the Compendium's countries but in 46% of lower-middle- and low-income ones [V], and the lack of valuation infrastructure is the main barrier to value-based readjustment [S] (Part III §2; Part IV §1, §5).
- **A lesson travels only with its support factors.** Rose's lesson drawing asks "whether and how the conditions which might make them work in a similar way can be created in importer jurisdictions" [V] ([Stone 2001](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/pais/research/csgr/research/workingpapers/2001/wp6901.pdf)), and inter-city adoption is rarely wholesale: 38% of 69 cities adopted an idea only partially and 20% stopped at pilots [S] (Part I §3). For a programme that teaches value capture, the Compendium's obstacles are the support factors to examine: owners' resistance (58% of high-income countries, 63% of middle- and low-income), administrative capacity (35% against 68%) and the legal framework (18% against 45%) [V] (Part IV §1).
- **The self-assessments are learning instruments.** The MFSA and the Scorecard are exercises whose results include improved consensus and new actions, the intermediate outcomes of Part I's typology, and the CDRF counts "financial viability and probity" among the indicators of organisational effectiveness [V] (Part I §1). UNDRR's third Essential, "Strengthen financial capability for resilience" [V], points the scorecard back to the accounts of Part II.

### 5.8 Findings the Four Parts Leave Unverified

- **Learning programmes.** No experimental or quasi-experimental study compares study tours, peer exchange, workshops and twinning on implementation outcomes; no source reports distributions of time to action, implementation beyond three years or the decay of change; no published data standard for knowledge-exchange results was found; and no validated scale exists for "improved consensus" or "enhanced connectivity" in city settings (Part I §3–§4, Gaps).
- **Municipal finance.** The agencies' sub-factor thresholds, S&P's Guidance cut-offs, Fitch's outcome matrix, the current CAPAG bands, Mexico's current municipal ranges, the MFSA template and the 2019 handbook's detailed methodology were not read; no primary source states the closed-form debt capacity or a subnational stochastic debt analysis on a revenue denominator (Part II, Gaps).
- **Scorecards.** No inter-rater study of PEFA, UNDRR, CRI or LGAF scores was found; the CRI's aggregation from questions to goals is not stated; the Scorecard's tools and later versions were not read; the theorems of ordinal aggregation must be read before they are cited (Part III, Gaps).
- **Value capture.** No systematic study measures capture against counterfactual uplift across instruments; no primary quantitative evidence on displacement or gentrification caused by these instruments was found; Chinese Taipei's statute, Hong and Needham, Sorensen and Mittal were not read (Part IV, Gaps).
- **Across the Parts.** The links of §5.7 are inferences. No source was found that models value-capture revenue in a municipal debt-capacity test, that scores a learning programme's outcomes with an ordinal scheme whose meaningfulness is checked, or that applies contribution analysis to a TIF district's but-for claim.


## 6. What This Means for the Infrastructure

This section restructures the closing sections of the four notes into what a module should hold for each Part: its **entities**, its **computations**, the **declared schemes** it should carry as data, the **pitfalls** it should guard against, and the **fixtures** that published worked examples offer. Everything here is design inference [M] unless a parameter or figure carries the marker of the Part it comes from. §6.5 gathers what the four modules share and where they might rest in the infrastructure; placing a new module or domain is a change that waits for the user's agreement (CLAUDE.md §6.5).

### 6.1 Part I: Knowledge-Exchange and Learning Programmes

#### 6.1.1 Entities

- **Programme:** sponsor, period, type (training; peer exchange; network of cities; twinning partnership).
- **Development goal,** owned by a named local champion.
- **Institutional challenge:** typed by a capacity area (one of three), scored by the indicators of a declared version (CDRF 2009 or WBI 2011), with the constraint's priority on WBI 2011's scale of 1–3 [V] and the stakeholders' agreement on the diagnosis.
- **Change objective:** free text with an action verb [V], linked to one or more challenges and checked against AoKE's four criteria (relevant, timely, consistent, a match with social norms) [V].
- **Agent of change:** a person or group, with role, organisation, authority to act and tenure. Tenure matters because "peer learning is primarily about transfers between people" and staff turnover undermines it [S].
- **Engagement:** an instance of one of AoKE's ten instruments [V], made of activities in its four classes [V]; dates, place or virtual, provider and seeker parties, participants, fully loaded cost by source (in-kind support and participants' time included), and stage (identification, design, implementation, follow-up).
- **Intended outcome:** an element of a declared typology (CDRF09, WBI11 or AoKE15), with a type of progress, an indicator, a threshold, a time window and a planned evidence method, as AoKE's examples always carry [V].
- **Action plan:** individual or group, with actions; each action has an owner, timing, a cost estimate, a funding source and dated status events (planned, approved, funded, started, completed, abandoned). The plan carries its completeness on URBACT's elements (actions, timing, responsibility, cost, funding, monitoring, risk) [V for the elements].
- **Outcome record:** in outcome harvesting's form (who changed what, when and where; significance; contribution) [V] or MSC's (story, domain, reporting period, selection level, selection reasons) [V], with its source, its independence from the programme and its evidence grade.
- **Contribution claim:** a link in the theory of change from engagement to outcome to capacity indicator, with plausibility and its circle of influence (control, direct, indirect) [S], fidelity, the outcome observed, rival factors each judged negligible, partial or dominant, the results of discriminating tests (hoop, smoking gun, doubly decisive) [S], substantiation records, and the confidence the intended use requires.
- **Follow-up wave:** a scheduled date per programme (for example the end, six weeks, three, six and nine months, three years), with response or non-response for each participant.
- **Transfer claim:** a lesson drawn from a visited place, stored as a claim of external validity with its support factors and the differences of context that the "inappropriate transfer" failure names [M for the failure types].

#### 6.1.2 Computations

- **Time to action as a censored duration.** From an engagement's end to the first action event of each type (plan approved, budget allocated, process started), right-censored at the last wave answered, abandonment a competing risk; Kaplan–Meier and Aalen–Johansen curves by instrument or programme, and hazard models on the IEG's design covariates (diagnosis done, needs assessed, participants selected, follow-up given, resources available) [V for the covariates' source]. Programme figures such as "75% started within three months" and "80% before closure" then become points on a survival curve.
- **Implementation of a plan:** the share of actions started and completed by date, weighted by count or by estimated cost; funding secured as a share of estimated cost; completeness at baseline.
- **Standing of a claim:** the weakest link along the path from engagement to capacity indicator. A claim is credible only when Mayne's four conditions hold [S for the conditions], with stronger evidence on the key links, rivals examined and substantiation at least partial; otherwise it is plausible or unsupported. The required confidence is a declared parameter of the use.
- **Outcome-harvest analytics:** counts of substantiated outcomes by social actor, by type of change, by intended or unintended, by sign; rates of substantiators' agreement.
- **Cost per substantiated outcome,** by instrument and outcome class, on fully loaded costs; ROI only where benefits are monetised and isolated, with Phillips's formulas, BCR = benefits ÷ costs and ROI = net benefits ÷ costs [V], and his conservative adjustments declared.
- **Crosswalks** between codebook versions (CDRF09, WBI11, AoKE15) as declared, possibly lossy mappings, checked as functors of kinds, with what is lost or lacking named.

#### 6.1.3 Declared Schemes

- CDRF 2009's three capacity factors and Table 2.1's indicators (6/8/6), with Annex 4's possible measures [V]; WBI 2011's three institutional capacity areas and Table 1's characteristics (5/8/6) [V].
- The three outcome typologies: CDRF 2009's six learning outcomes and Table 2.4's generic objectives; WBI 2011's six intermediate capacity outcomes with their sub-dimensions; AoKE 2015's five intermediate outcomes with their types of progress [V].
- AoKE's three institutional challenges, ten instruments and four classes of activity [V].
- Kirkpatrick's four levels and Phillips's five, as secondary tags on individual-level evidence [V]; the CDRF's warning that Kirkpatrick's Level 2 is "not relevant to facilitating consensus and teamwork, formulating policies and strategies, implementing strategies/plans, or fostering networks" [V].
- The evidence grades: the CDRF's weaker and stronger evidence methods (Table A5.1) [V]; outcome harvesting's substantiation on the outcome and on the contribution (fully, partially, disagree) [V]; MSC's recorded selection criteria and verification [V]; AoKE's Template 2, "Yes / Somewhat / No" with "How do you know?" [V].
- Follow-up schedules (AoKE's Table 9) [V] and URBACT's elements of plan quality [V].
- AoKE's Table 6 of instrument strengths, held as a prior with its provenance as expert judgement, never as an estimate [V that it is judgement].
- A checklist for transfer claims after Rose's lesson drawing [V via Stone] and Dolowitz and Marsh's types of failure (uninformed, incomplete, inappropriate) [M].

#### 6.1.4 Pitfalls

1. **Satisfaction taken as a result.** Over 90% satisfaction coexisted with substantial workplace change in about half of training [V]; Level 1 reactions are never aggregated into outcomes.
2. **Survivorship and non-response in follow-up.** URBACT's figures are "of the cities that answered" [V]; non-response is stored and reported, never dropped.
3. **Attribution without a counterfactual or rivals.** URBACT's "nine-fold increase" and C40's null on emissions show that both inflated and deflated readings occur [V for both results].
4. **The programme as its own substantiator.** Outcome harvesting requires substantiators outside the change agent's organisation [V]; independence is stored explicitly.
5. **Silent merges across codebook versions,** where coalitions and networks are split in one version and merged in another [V]; no counts are pooled without a declared crosswalk.
6. **Expert tables treated as evidence** (AoKE's Table 6) [V that it is judgement].
7. **Downstream outcomes skipping the chain.** Testing learning against emissions or GDP without intermediate outcomes gives uninformative nulls.
8. **The unit of learning.** Learning happens in people while results are claimed for cities; outcomes are linked to the persons who carry them, and their departures recorded [S for turnover].
9. **Transfer claims without support factors** [V for Rose's question].
10. **Undeclared time windows.** "Within three months" is comparable only with indicators on the same window and schedule.

#### 6.1.5 Fixtures

- URBACT's 2019 IAP study as a check of plan completeness: 84% set out actions, 66% timing, 65% responsibility, 32% costs, 57% funding, 57% monitoring, 37% a risk assessment, 44% at least five of six further quality elements [V] ([URBACT IAP study 2019](https://urbact.eu/sites/default/files/2023-07/iapstudy2019.pdf)).
- AoKE's example indicators, each with its threshold and window, as fixtures of the indicator grammar [V].

### 6.2 Part II: Municipal and Subnational Finance

#### 6.2.1 Entities

- **Chart of accounts,** mapping any local government's budget lines onto a small canonical structure:
  - current revenue by line (own taxes by base, user charges, asset income, shared taxes, unconditional and conditional grants), each flagged own or transfer, recurrent or not, unrestricted or earmarked, so that Mexico's *ingresos de libre disposición*, Brazil's net current revenue and Colombia's unrestricted current revenue are computed from flags, not stored;
  - current expenditure (payroll with social contributions wherever budgeted, as Colombia requires [V]; operation and maintenance; transfers paid; interest apart);
  - capital (asset sales, capital grants, capital expenditure, loan proceeds, principal repaid as scheduled, prepaid or refinanced, since Mexico excludes the last two from debt service [V]);
  - basis flags for every figure (cash, modified cash, modified accrual, accrual; budgeted or executed; fiscal year; currency).
- **Balance sheet:** cash restricted and unrestricted, liquid assets, payables by age (suppliers, contractors, arrears), short- and long-term debt by instrument with full schedules, leases, PPP liabilities, guarantees, the debt of controlled entities, and pension liabilities.
- **Loan:** principal, rate, tenor, grace period, bullet, currency and schedule.
- **Projection:** a declared driver for each component (base growth, rate, collection rate, transfer formula, wage index, price index) and a set of scenarios, in the MFSA's rows A–H [V for the rows].
- **Scheme:** ratio definitions bound to account flags, thresholds, a scoring map, a combination rule, caps and overrides, a consequence, a version with its validity interval, and a citation.
- **Judgement input:** institutional framework, governance, extraordinary support, Fitch's six risk factors; each a declared value with who declared it.
- **Peer group:** the stratum or set against which per-capita ratios, capacity and effort are compared (France compares each municipality's eleven ratios with the average of its size stratum [V]).

#### 6.2.2 Computations

- **Derived aggregates, all computed:** gross operating savings C = A − B, net savings E = C − D, investment financing G = F − E, and the closing balance H = (A + G) − (B + D + F) [V for the definitions], with H read as residual cash and a negative H as unfinanced investment [M].
- **Ratios as named expressions:** numerator, denominator, basis, averaging window (four quarters in Mexico [V]; a weighted three years in CAPAG [S]; seven years in Poland [M]) and source, with the MFSA's, the IMF's, S&P's, Moody's, Fitch's and the statutory variants kept side by side.
- **Debt capacity:** the largest new loan whose service keeps projected coverage above a declared floor in every year of its life and that respects every statutory cap, L_max = min(L_max^DSCR, L_max^stock, L_max^interest, L_max^flow) [M], with grace periods and bullets explicit. It returns the binding constraint, the binding year and the coverage path, and tests the position after the borrowing, as Colombia counts the new loan's interest [V] and Spain the projected operation [S].
- **Scorecards and statutory classifications** evaluated as data, the output of a shadow rating labelled "shadow" and showing which parts were declared and how it would change under each declared alternative.
- **Debt dynamics on a revenue denominator,** d_t = d_{t−1}(1 + i_t)/(1 + γ_t) − pb_t + sfa_t, with the debt-stabilising primary balance pb* = d(i − γ)/(1 + γ) [M]; stochastic paths with fan charts and the probability of crossing any declared scheme's cap or coverage floor [M], with transfer-rule shifts and contingent liabilities among the shocks.
- **Fiscal capacity and effort** by a representative tax system or a regression on a declared peer panel, never from one city's accounts [M].

#### 6.2.3 Declared Schemes

Each scheme is stored with its version and validity interval.

- The MFSA's dashboard benchmarks (gross savings > 0.3 of current revenue, net surplus > 0.2, cash ≥ 90 days, debt ≤ 10 years of surplus, debt service < 10%, capital investment > 40%, and the rest) [V], marked as rules of thumb without a stated source where the handbook gives none [M].
- France's eleven ratios against size strata and the West and Central African indicators [V].
- Moody's RLG scorecard: factors 20/20/30/30, the weights within the financial factor, governance as the weakest of three, scores 1–9, extraordinary support bands [V], with the caveat that the 2018 republication was not read [M].
- S&P: the institutional framework (25/50/25, six categories), the individual credit profile as the equal-weighted average of five factors, the anchor matrix of Table 1 and the caps of Table 2 [V].
- Fitch: six risk factors, the four-metric financial-profile thresholds, Type A and Type B, the five-year rating case [V].
- Mexico: three indicators, art. 14's combination rule and art. 24's consequences [V]; the net-financing ceilings of 15%, 5% and 0 [S]; the published range limits [S].
- Colombia: 40% and 60% of operating savings, 100% of current revenue [V].
- Brazil: Senate Resolution 40/2001 [S]; CAPAG's 2017 bands and final-grade rule [S]; later versions noted but unread.
- Spain: net savings and 75% and 110% of current revenue [S]. Italy: eight parameters, half breached [V].
- Japan's Act No. 94 of 2007, as literature: four ratios with early-warning and rebuilding standards [S].
- India's net borrowing ceilings, and the IMF note's further schemes [S].

#### 6.2.4 Pitfalls

1. **One name, different ratios:** "DSCR" and "operating balance" (Table 5.1) [V].
2. **Mixing bases:** cash city budgets against accrual GFS and ESA aggregates; depreciation present or absent; budget against execution.
3. **Hidden debt:** guarantees, debt of utilities and state-owned enterprises, PPPs, supplier arrears and pension shortfalls, which S&P and Mexico fold in by rule [V] and a naive ratio does not.
4. **Balloon payments and grace periods:** a ratio on this year's service understates future service [V].
5. **Transfers:** own-source share counts unconditional grants as own in some schemes and not in others [V]; transfer-rule changes dominate the risk of transfer-dependent cities.
6. **Collection:** levy-based against cash-based revenue; current-year against arrears collection rates [M].
7. **Thresholds are versioned law.** Colombia's current 100% is verified and its earlier 80% secondary (the notes' closing section marked the change [V]; §0.4 item 7); CAPAG changed in 2017, 2022 and 2023 [S]; Italy's parameters run for three years [V]; and the handbook's description of Brazil's caps conflicts with the Senate resolution [V vs S].
8. **Agency scorecards are not ratings:** committees override them (Cape Town, baa3 suggested, baa2 assigned) [V].
9. **Denominators:** per-capita ratios need one population source and date; local GDP rarely exists, as the IMF and the MFSA both say [V].
10. **Derived formulas presented as authority:** the debt-capacity closed form, the lender's floor of 1.2–1.5 and the revenue-denominator debt dynamics are derived [M], and the module says so.

#### 6.2.5 Fixtures

- **Moody's Cape Town arithmetic** [V] ([Moody's credit opinion](https://www.capetown.gov.za/_documents/resource.capetown.gov.za/documentcentre/Documents/Financial%20documents/Moody's%20Credit%20opinion%20for%20Cape%20Town.pdf)): the financial factor 3(12.5%) + 5(12.5%) + 1(25%) + 1(25%) + 1(25%) = 1.75, contributing 0.53; the idiosyncratic assessment 0.20 + 1.00 + 0.53 + 0.30 = 2.03, rounded to 2; with the sovereign at Baa2 the matrix suggests baa3, against baa2 assigned.
- **Fitch's Canton of Zurich** [V] ([Fitch 2026](https://www.zh.ch/content/dam/zhweb/bilder-dokumente/themen/steuern-finanzen/kantonsfinanzen/rating/ratingbericht_fitch_2026.pdf)): economic liability burden 13.3% (aaa), payback 19.8x (bb), synthetic coverage 0.7x (b), fiscal debt burden 34.9% (aaa), giving a financial profile of 'aa' and, with a Stronger risk profile, an SCP of 'aaa' after peer comparison.
- **S&P's anchor:** institutional framework 3 with an individual credit profile of 2.2 falls in 'aa−'/'a+' [V].
- **Mexico's art. 14** as a complete rule table over the levels of three indicators, and the default rule of art. 15 [V].
- **Italy's eight thresholds** and the breach count [V]; **CAPAG's final-grade rule** of 2017 [S].

### 6.3 Part III: Scorecards on Ordinal Scales

#### 6.3.1 Entities

- **Scheme,** declared in four layers:
  - statements (criteria or dimensions), each with an identifier, its text, its parent and its scale;
  - levels, an ordered set of symbols (A > B > C > D; 5 > … > 0; 3 > … > 0), each with its anchor; where the source is cumulative, as in PEFA and UNDRR's count anchors, each level holds its list of requirements and the level is derived as the highest whose requirements all hold [V for the rules]; a count or rate behind a banded level is kept with its thresholds;
  - special values, each with a declared rule and a required justification: NA deleted before aggregation, NU excluded with a reason, D\* aggregated as D with its flag kept in the trace, UNDRR's "not relevant" excluded from denominators [V for the rules];
  - aggregation nodes, one per parent, each naming its rule, and declared refusals where the scheme forbids aggregation (PEFA across indicators; the CRI's single score) [V].
- **Assessment:** the unit, the date, the assessors, the oversight and the reviewers (PEFA's Box 1.1) [V].
- **Score:** who scored it and when; the evidence items, each by digest, with the passage or figure and its date; each requirement of the chosen level and of the next higher level, marked met, unmet or unknown; why the next level was not reached; data-quality notes; for a re-assessment, the previous score, its evidence and the comparability issues [V for PEFA's requirements].
- **Independent rating,** sealed before moderation; **moderation act,** with its reasons (the LGAF's "motivated ranking") [V]; **calibration round** on shared anchor cases.

#### 6.3.2 Computations

- **Rules tagged by the scale they require** (Table 5.2): order only (minimum with "+", maximum, order statistics and the median with a declared tie rule, profiles of counts and cumulative counts, threshold and conjunctive rules, leximin, Pareto dominance with incomparability kept, weighted median, Sugeno integral, lookup tables on levels) or interval-dependent (sums, means, percentages of the maximum, weighted linear indices, PEFA's M2).
- **A verdict of meaningfulness** by recomputing a rule's comparisons under strictly increasing recodings of the levels, reporting a counterexample where one exists ({D, A} against {C, C} under D = −10) [M].
- **A test of declared lookup tables:** whether a table is a function of the code sum under some equally spaced coding; PEFA's Table 1 is, with the step maps of Part III §1 [V, computed].
- **PEFA's M2 encoded verbatim,** never computed from the mean, with guard tests: 10, 20 and 35 rows, symmetry, monotonicity, values between the minimum and the maximum [V, computed].
- **Non-compensatory outranking** (the OECD/JRC rule, eⱼₖ summing the weights of indicators on which j beats k plus half the ties, then a Kemeny ranking) [V for the method].
- **Krippendorff's α** with the ordinal difference function, per criterion and over the scheme, before moderation [S], the conventional marks 0.667 and 0.800 shown as declared thresholds.
- **Change over time within one unit,** the reading UNDRR, the CRI and PEFA support [V].

#### 6.3.3 Declared Schemes

- **PEFA 2016:** 31 indicators in 94 dimensions under seven pillars; A–D with C the basic level; the method of each indicator (4 single, 9 M1, 18 M2); Table 1 verbatim; NA, NU and D\*; the standard quantifiers (All ≥ 90%, Most ≥ 75%, Majority ≥ 50%, Some ≥ 25%, A Few between 10% and 25%); a partial assessment below 21 indicators [V].
- **UNDRR's Scorecard (2017):** the Ten Essentials; 47 preliminary questions scored 0–3 and 117 detailed criteria scored 0–5 [V], with the caveat on the count (§0.4 item 11); the rules on weights, relevance and comparability [V].
- **The City Resilience Index:** four dimensions, 12 goals, 52 indicators, 156 qualitative and 156 quantitative questions scored 1–5, no single score [V].
- **The LGAF:** 21 indicators and 80 dimensions on A–D pre-coded statements, decided by panels [V]; the revised 116 dimensions [S].
- **The OECD/JRC non-compensatory rule** [V].

#### 6.3.4 Pitfalls

1. **Averages and percentages on ordinal anchors** (UNDRR's Essential averages and final percentage) [M for the meaningfulness argument].
2. **M2 computed from the mean,** which reproduces the table only where the rounding happens to agree [V, computed].
3. **Denominators that vary with relevance:** two cities' percentages rest on different criteria [M].
4. **Ranking units on a scheme that forbids it:** the Scorecard is "not comparable across different cities" [V]; a tool refuses, or demands a common declared weighting and criterion set and states that it departs from the scheme.
5. **Aggregating across PEFA's indicators,** which the Framework forbids [V].
6. **Dropping the D\* flag,** which the rule discards but the trace must keep [V for the rule].
7. **Consensus overwriting independent ratings,** so that reliability can never be audited [M].
8. **Banding that discards the quantity** behind count and frequency anchors [M].
9. **Weights read as importance in a compensatory rule** [V, OECD/JRC].
10. **Errors in the source text,** such as the missing "not" [V]; encodings checked against the official tools.
11. **A model's draft score taken as the record's:** it is a proposal of grade C, kept apart.

#### 6.3.5 Fixtures

- PEFA's Table 1 verbatim, its row counts and the step maps of Part III §1 [V; V, computed].
- The recoding counterexample for M2 [M].

### 6.4 Part IV: Land Value Capture and Land Readjustment

#### 6.4.1 Entities

- **Declared project:** catchment, schedule, cost, and the plan's change of rights (added floor area, change of use, rural to urban).
- **Parcel:** area, entry value V_i and allocated value V′_i at a stated valuation date, owner, and consent.
- **Uplift estimate:** V_i(with, t) and V_i(without, t) per parcel, split into infrastructure and regulatory uplift, with the estimator (hedonic, difference-in-differences, before-and-after), the comparison set, the dates (announcement, construction, opening) and an interval.
- **Instrument,** declared by mechanism after the OECD–Lincoln taxonomy (infrastructure levy, developer obligations, charges for development rights, land readjustment, strategic land management) [V], with TIF and recurrent property taxes as two further types the taxonomy excludes [V for the exclusion]; for each, its trigger, its base (cost, value increment, area or a score), statutory share, caps, consent threshold, valuation basis with its ratio to market, legal basis, administering body, payment schedule and incidence split.
- **Land readjustment scheme:** the areas M, A, N, E and R; reserve land and its sale value net of selling costs; contributions in land or money; settlements; duration.
- **TIF district:** base B, assessed values AV_t, designated share s, overlapping rates τ_j, term T, discount rate r, but-for share β and displacement ΔG.
- **Stock of development rights:** authorised, issued, used in licences and in circulation, with zone conversion factors f_z between 0.5 and 2 m² per certificate [V].
- **Lease:** premium, annual rent, term, modification premium and renewal.

#### 6.4.2 Computations

- **The identities of land readjustment:** M + A = N + E + R, P = N − M and d = (P + R)/A, and the proportional ratio Pr = (1 − d)·y with y = e/a [V, from the literature]; entitlements V*_i = V_i·ΣV′/ΣV and settlements s_i = V′_i − V*_i [M]; the area-based variant with a capped deduction d_i ≤ d̄ and the excess advantage settled in money [V for the statute; M for the formula].
- **Feasibility** as the present value over the scheme's duration of reserve-land sales, subsidies, levies and costs [M].
- **TIF:** revenue R_t = s·Στ_j·max(0, AV_t − B), its present value, bond capacity as that value over a coverage floor, diversion from each overlapping jurisdiction D_{j,t} = τ_j(1 − β)I_t, and displacement −τΔG elsewhere in the city [M]; the four value-accounting identities of Dye and Merriman [V].
- **Levies and rights:** a levy's total L = min(cost·(1 + a), λ·ΣΔV_i), apportioned by declared scores [V for the parameters; M for the formula]; the virtual-land charge C = (ΔA/FAR_basic)·p_land·k [V for the method; M for the formula]; CEPAC stock identities [V].
- **The three capture ratios** κ, ρ and σ, reported apart [M], and **incidence by party** (original owners, developers, buyers or tenants, overlapping jurisdictions, owners outside the catchment).
- **No double charging:** Σ over instruments of capture on parcel i ≤ ΔV_i [M], as Colombia's art. 87 bars a plusvalía on works financed by valorización [S].
- **Uplift estimation** by hedonic regression with spatial dependence, by difference-in-differences with selection correction, and by meta-analytic priors for screening only (about 2.6% per 250 m nearer a station, 6.2% residential and 15.2% commercial within a quarter mile, with a range of −12.84% to +38.70%) [V].

#### 6.4.3 Declared Schemes

- **Germany:** the area contribution capped at 30% in areas first developed and 10% elsewhere, the excess in money; distribution by value with every owner allotted at least the market value of the former plot, differences settled in money; values at the date of the *Umlegungsbeschluss* [V].
- **Spain:** the cession of 5–15% (up to 20%) of the weighted average edificabilidad; "the burden imposed on landowners cannot be larger than the benefits of the project" [V].
- **Gujarat:** half the increment; appropriation up to 15% for roads, 5% for parks, 5% for social infrastructure, 15% for sale and 10% for weaker sections, at most 50% in total, 35–40% in practice [V].
- **The Republic of Korea's 1966 Act:** 50% returned, 30% for infrastructure, 20% for cost recovery [V]; consent for private projects of half the owners and two thirds of the area [V], against the Compendium's simple majority (§0.4 item 14).
- **Colombia:** the plusvalía at 30–50% of the uplift per m² with its triggering acts and exigibility [V]; the valorización at the cost of works plus up to 30% for administration [V].
- **India's betterment levies** at 30–50% of the increase [V]; **Brazil's** onerous grant by the virtual-land method or by percentage (Campo Grande 70% of the increment; Salvador 50%) and CEPACs with zone factors [V]; **Hong Kong, China's** annual rent at 3% of rateable value [V].
- **Chinese Taipei's** 45% cap, held as unverified until the statute is read [M].
- **Consent thresholds** from unanimity through two thirds and a simple majority to none [V]; **TIF terms** of 15–30 years [V, stated inconsistently].

#### 6.4.4 Pitfalls

1. **Before-and-after mistaken for uplift:** it adds the market trend and anticipation to the project's effect; a declared counterfactual is required.
2. **β = 1 in TIF:** Dye and Merriman find no net gain citywide and commercial cannibalisation [V].
3. **A statutory rate mistaken for capture:** Gujarat's charges of Rs 150–300 per m² against development costs of Rs 700–1,000 per m² while land values rose at least tenfold [V].
4. **Cost-based levies presented as value capture:** only 23% of countries base levy totals on value increments [V].
5. **Ignoring incidence:** fees on developers are capitalised into land and house prices (Dade County [S]; developers in the United Kingdom "can shift the costs to landowners" [V]).
6. **Uniform meta-analytic premia** applied to a project despite the dispersion and the specification bias [V].
7. **Discounting and duration:** schemes of 10–20 years change feasibility [V for the durations].
8. **Unsold or under-priced rights:** Curitiba's first auction cleared at the minimum and raised US$14.2 million against US$30 million expected [V]; the auction's demand is modelled, not a sale of every certificate assumed.
9. **Name collisions across jurisdictions:** Israel's and Poland's "infrastructure levies" are developer obligations [V]; instruments are declared by mechanism.
10. **Double charging of one uplift** by stacked instruments [M].
11. **Capital revenue counted as operating revenue** in the municipal accounts (§5.7) [M].
12. **Place names and cases:** Chinese Taipei and Hong Kong, China as the naming policy writes them; no Japanese city as a case.

### 6.5 Commitments Shared by the Four Modules, and Their Candidate Placement

#### 6.5.1 Commitments Shared by the Four Modules

1. **Schemes are data,** with an author, a version, a validity interval and a citation: codebooks of outcomes, ratio definitions with thresholds and combination rules, scorecards' levels and rules, statutes' shares and consent thresholds (§5.1).
2. **Quantities are stored by exact definition and basis,** never by family name, and two quantities with one name are never pooled (§5.2).
3. **Every causal or uplift claim states its counterfactual,** comparison set, dates and interval; β and the absence of rivals are never defaulted (§5.3).
4. **One checker of meaningfulness** classifies every declared combination rule as order-only or interval-dependent and reports counterexamples (§5.4).
5. **Every claim and score records who made it,** their independence from the party credited, whether it is an independent rating or a consensus, and the grade of its evidence; judgements enter as declared values with their author, and a model's proposal is of grade C (§5.5).
6. **Dates are first-class,** non-response and censoring are stored, and cash flows are compared at a declared discount rate and price level (§5.6).
7. **Cash flows cross between modules classified** as operating or capital, recurrent or one-off (§5.7).

#### 6.5.2 Candidate Placement in the Infrastructure

These are proposals [M]; each new module or domain waits for the user's agreement.

- **Ordinal schemes and their meaningfulness:** kybernesis's kernel already holds "scales and meaningfulness", and ADR 0131 names "ordinal scorecards in kybernesis" as a gap; the general rules of ordinal aggregation (order statistics, Sugeno integrals, outranking, the recoding test) name no object in nature and belong in a foundation, the scorecards themselves in the domain or work that uses them.
- **Evidence, substantiation and moderation:** tekmerion's grading by declared schemes and its `warrant`; archeion's sealed journal for scores, substantiation records and moderation acts; nomos's agreement of coders in `measurement`, held to the krippendorff package, to be checked for the ordinal difference function.
- **Time to action:** peira's `survival` (Kaplan–Meier, Aalen–Johansen, Cox).
- **Theories of change and transfer:** the policy domain's `change` (a chain no better shown than its weakest link) and `transfer`, and kybernesis's `transfer` with its support factors.
- **Crosswalks and scheme revisions:** mappings checked as functors of kinds, as kybernesis's `ontology` admits revisions, on kategoria.
- **Municipal accounts, ratios and debt capacity:** a module of the economics domain beside `ledger`, or of a domain of public finance; debt dynamics and fan charts on tyche's `time_series` and `uncertainty`; discounting on nomos's `appraisal`.
- **Uplift and incidence:** the urban-science domain's `demand` (hedonic prices with spatial dependence), tyche's `spatial`, peira's `quasi` (difference-in-differences, staggered adoption, synthetic control) and the allocation domain's `incidence` (capitalisation into rents).
- **Land readjustment and development rights:** the identities name land and plots and belong to a domain; the change of rights touches architecture's `rules` (the envelope a lot's rules allow). Which domain holds value capture is an open decision.


## 7. What Was Built

The survey was followed by three decisions, each building one or two of §6's modules. What each leaves unbuilt is said in its record.

- **Scorecards and knowledge programmes** ([ADR 0144](../../../development/decisions/0144-knowledge-programmes-and-scorecards.md)). Two modules came of §6.1 and §6.3:
  - kybernesis's `scorecards`: aggregation rules meaningful on an ordinal scale (the weakest link with its "+", a declared conversion table, the lower median, thresholds, profiles; an interval mean only where declared, and marked), refuted by the admissible transforms of the scale; Krippendorff's ordinal α; weaknesses as needs;
  - the policy domain's `learning`: needs reached, action plans at a date with the pace of completion by Kaplan and Meier, results on a declared ladder at a sufficient grade, contribution claims substantiated only with every rival addressed, formats compared by substantiated contribution per cost.
- **Municipal finance** ([ADR 0145](../../../development/decisions/0145-the-finances-of-local-governments.md)). From §6.2, the economics domain's `fiscal` reads accounts through a declared chart and ratios stated by their terms (never "the DSCR" by name). It also holds threshold schemes as data, the years ahead with annuity and bullet loans, debt capacity (B* of Part II, bounded by a ceiling on the stock), debt dynamics with a moving-block fan, and the representative tax system. The data library holds Eurostat's local government sector for 28 countries.
- **Land value capture and land readjustment** ([ADR 0146](../../../development/decisions/0146-land-value-capture-and-readjustment.md)). From §6.4, the economics domain's `capture` provisionally answers §6.5's open decision on which domain holds value capture. It holds:
  - the levy, rights by formula and at auction, tax increment with the but-for share and its diversion by jurisdiction, and an obligation with declared incidence, all as streams of payments;
  - ρ, κ and σ kept apart (§5's three "capture rates");
  - double charges named by parcel;
  - readjustment's identities;
  - replotting by assignment, with settlements summing to zero.

  Reserve-land sales are kept out of the operating surplus from which `fiscal` computes debt capacity, as §5.7 requires.

All three are composed by the API and drawn together in the Urban Studio's plug-in *Programmes and public finance*. No jurisdiction's scheme, threshold, statutory rate or cap is in the code. Every one is a declaration.
