## 0. Preface

### 0.1 Purpose

The survey serves a work of knowledge management, evolution and governance over heterogeneous multilateral practice. Parties of every kind take part: governments, international organisations, cities, firms, civil society, communities and artificial systems. The work must support:

- governance;
- the benefits and values each party holds and builds jointly;
- the relational structure among parties;
- project progress;
- archive objects;
- evidence;
- event trajectories.

Chapter A covers diplomacy and multilateral practice. Chapter B covers SDG 17, partnerships and effective development co-operation, and opens with the user's monograph (Huang 2026, DOI 10.17613/s9xqh-xm397), whose concepts the work adopts.

### 0.2 What the Two Chapters Agree On

- **Relations are typed and multiple.** An undertaking is a typed dependency structure R = (V, {E_k}, D, Γ) (B.2.3): evidential reliance, communication, authorisation, review, challenge, membership, contribution. Absence of observation is kept apart from absence of relation (B.2.3; A.11.4).
- **Values are vectors with their interpretations, never a forced scalar.** No aggregate stands without an authorised rule (B.2.4, Proposition 12.1). Mutual benefit is read party by party, and value created is kept apart from value claimed (A.4; B.4.2, B.7). Most measures of partnership are ordinal: SDG 17.16.1 and 17.14.1 sum ordinal judgements (B.3.7), and scorecards forbid comparison across bodies.
- **Decision rules are simple games.** A priori power is reported beside the evidence of influence, never instead of it (A.3.1–A.3.2). A decision is a record with its authority, evidence, those affected and routes of challenge (B.10). Closure can be provisional (B.2.3, Definition 9.7).
- **Events are reports.** Machine-coded event data counts reports rather than events, overweights some sources and under-codes opposition. Each origin is counted once (A.6.2), and repeated model outputs are never independent corroboration (B.2.4).
- **A history is a longitudinal evaluative trajectory** (E, Q, K, O, U): the undertaking, its evaluative dimensions, the constraints, the observations and absences, the disputes, with how each changed and why. Indicators are compared over time only across a defensible bridge between versions (B.2.3, Definition 12.4).
- **Project progress has a native form in IATI.** Status, transactions, results and roles; the GPEDC's indicators can be computed from it (B.5–B.6; A.9).

### 0.3 What the Infrastructure Lacks

- **nomos:** Banzhaf and Penrose power, indices for large weighted and compound games, the core and the nucleolus, the Nash and Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solutions, Adjusted Winner.
- **tyche:** dynamic ideal points from votes, relational event models.
- **The data library:** readers for IATI 2.03, UN voting, the UN Treaty Collection, COW IGO and PLOVER.
- **A domain:** chapter A proposes `diplomacy` (institutions, rules, negotiation, relations, events, mandates, reviews, partnerships); chapter B proposes modules of partnerships (parties, commitments, activities, values, coherence, effectiveness). The name and place are the user's.

### A.1 Scope and Method

This part surveys what the study and practice of diplomacy offer a work that manages, evolves and governs knowledge of heterogeneous multilateral practice. It is ordered by need. Each section gives concepts, then methods, data and standards, and what the infrastructure should build ("For the system"). Section 11 maps the needs onto the foundations and proposes a diplomacy domain; Section 12 gives two tables. One principle runs throughout, from the infrastructure's own record: a statement about a party is a claim with a source, and a model's word is a proposal, never the record's.

### A.2 Multilateralism and Its Theory

#### A.2.1 Multilateralism as an Institutional Form

Keohane defined multilateralism nominally as "the practice of coordinating national policies in groups of three or more states, through ad hoc arrangements or by means of institutions" (Keohane 1990, p. 731) [S]. Ruggie (1992) [V] answered that number is not the point: multilateralism is a qualitative form marked by **generalised principles of conduct** (rules for classes of parties and actions, not dyads), **indivisibility** (joint goods and commitments, as in collective security) and **diffuse reciprocity** (a rough balance expected over time and across issues, not item by item) [M on wording].

*For the system.* Each property is checkable on a record. Generalisation is a property of a norm's scope; indivisibility of a commitment's structure; diffuse reciprocity of a ledger. A ledger of contributions and benefits, kept with provenance, lets the work report over which window and which issues a partnership balances, and whether it balances at all.

#### A.2.2 Regimes and Rational Design

Krasner's regime is a set of "principles, norms, rules, and decision-making procedures around which actors' expectations converge in a given area" (Krasner 1982) [S; quotation M]: four layers of normative content. Koremenos, Lipson and Snidal (2001) [V] treat states as designers. Their design dimensions are membership, scope, centralisation, control and flexibility; their explanatory variables are distribution, enforcement, the number of actors and uncertainty (about behaviour, the state of the world, preferences). They state conjectures, such as that uncertainty about the state of the world favours flexibility.

*For the system.* Every institution or partnership should be coded on the five dimensions and four problems, each value with its source and coder. The conjectures become hypotheses on the work's own cases: regressions in tyche, or set relations with consistency and coverage in zetesis's qualitative comparative analysis.

#### A.2.3 Regime Complexes, Minilateralism and Coalitions

Raustiala and Victor (2004) [V] named the *regime complex*: partially overlapping, non-hierarchical institutions governing one issue area, which invite forum shopping. Keohane and Victor (2011) [V] found climate governed by a loosely coupled set of specific regimes, possibly preferable to any feasible comprehensive one. Naím (2009) [V] proposed *minilateralism*: the smallest number of countries needed for the largest impact, a "magic number" varying by issue.

*For the system.* "Party is a member of institution" is a formal context in morphe's sense (its decision 0018); its concept lattice lists the maximal clubs, and overlap, nesting and parallelism are read off it. Naím's magic number is the fewest parties covering a declared share of a quantity; with a submodular coverage function, calx's greedy maximisation with its bound (its decision 0019) answers it and says how far the chosen club is from the best.

#### A.2.4 Complex Interdependence and Networked Governance

Keohane and Nye (1977) [S] characterised *complex interdependence* by multiple channels between societies (interstate, transgovernmental, transnational), no hierarchy among issues and a minor role for force; they distinguished sensitivity from vulnerability [M]. Slaughter (2004) [V] located global governance in *government networks* of regulators, judges and legislators. Abbott, Genschel, Snidal and Zangl (2015) [V] described *orchestration*: an international organisation enlists intermediaries to reach targets it cannot regulate directly.

*For the system.* Relations must be typed by channel (head of government, ministry, regulator, city, firm, community) and by issue: a multilayer network, not one graph. Orchestration is a ternary relation (orchestrator, intermediary, target) that binary edges lose; symploke can hold it as an n-ary relation.

#### A.2.5 Polycentric Governance

Ostrom, Tiebout and Warren (1961) [V] read a metropolitan area's many governments as a *polycentric* system of formally independent centres that take one another into account. Elinor Ostrom (1990) [M] drew design principles of enduring commons institutions (boundaries, fitted rules, collective choice, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution, recognised rights to organise, nesting), and argued in her Nobel lecture (Ostrom 2010) [S] for nested, overlapping units. Jordan, Huitema, van Asselt and Forster (2018) [V] tested polycentricity on climate governance.

*For the system.* Polycentricity needs scales. kinesis's `scales` (its decision 0007) carries quantities on a hierarchy of units with named operators between levels; a polycentric arrangement adds authority to that hierarchy. Ostrom's principles are conditions coded case by case, and their joint sufficiency for endurance is a QCA question.

### A.3 Governance of Multilateral Bodies and Partnerships

#### A.3.1 Decision Rules as Simple Games

A decision rule over parties N is a *simple game*, a monotone family of winning coalitions. Practice offers:

- **consensus**, which in the WTO holds "if no Member, present at the meeting when the decision is taken, formally objects" (footnote 1 to Article IX:1, Marrakesh Agreement) [S]: absence and silence do not block, so consensus is not unanimity;
- **weighted voting** with a quota, as on the IMF's and World Bank's boards [M];
- **double majorities**, as in the EU Council (55 per cent of states, 65 per cent of population) [M];
- **veto**, as for the permanent members under Article 27(3) of the UN Charter [M];
- **consensus with a fallback and a participation rule**: ASEAN decides "as a basic principle" by consultation and consensus; where it cannot be achieved the Summit may decide how a decision is made (ASEAN Charter, Article 20), and "ASEAN Minus X" may apply to economic commitments where there is consensus to use it (Article 21) [S].

The Measurement of International Authority dataset [V] scores international organisations annually on *pooling* (shared decision rules) and *delegation* (authority given to independent bodies), 76 organisations from 1950, updated to 2019.

#### A.3.2 Power Indices and Their Limits

Shapley and Shubik (1954) [V] measured power as the share of orderings in which a member is pivotal. Banzhaf (1965) [S] counted swings, coalitions that win with a member and lose without it. Felsenthal and Machover (1998) [V] separated *I-power* (influence on the outcome: Banzhaf, Penrose) from *P-power* (expected share of a prize: Shapley–Shubik) and catalogued the paradoxes. Leech (2002) [S] applied them to the IMF's weights.

*Caveat.* Indices measure *a priori* power, every coalition equally likely. Influence depends on preferences, agendas and secretariats. The work must report a priori power as such.

*For the system.* nomos computes the exact Shapley value for up to 24 players (its decision 0011), enough for the Shapley–Shubik index of small bodies. Bodies of 193 members need generating functions for weighted games and compound games for double majorities; Banzhaf and Penrose measures are not yet in nomos. Consensus is a game parameterised by attendance.

#### A.3.3 Mandates, Secretariats and Accountability

The Secretary-General's Mandate Implementation Review (UN80 Initiative, A/80/318, 6 August 2025) [V] counted more than 40,000 mandates to UN entities and found fragmented delivery, a widening gap between mandates and resources, and uneven review, leaving each mandate's merit to member states. Principal–agent theory treats a secretariat as an agent whose slack principals limit by screening, monitoring and sanction (Hawkins, Lake, Nielson and Tierney 2006) [M].

*For the system.* A mandate is a record with its source paragraph (read from Akoma Ntoso where marked up, §7.2), issuing body, addressees, duration, review clause, resources and successors. Mandates form a directed graph of renewals and amendments, on which the review's questions (overlap, never reviewed) are queries.

#### A.3.4 Review Mechanisms

Pagani's OECD study [V] describes peer review as the systematic, non-adversarial assessment of one state's performance by others, whose force comes from recommendations, dialogue, public scrutiny and comparison [S]. The Universal Periodic Review, created by Human Rights Council resolution 5/1 (18 June 2007) [V], reviews every member on the basis of the Charter, the Universal Declaration and the instruments it is party to, its first objective the improvement of the situation on the ground; states record recommendations as supported or noted [M]. UPR Info's database holds more than 90,000 recommendations and pledges, by reviewing and recommending state, theme and type of action [S].

*For the system.* A review is a loop (recommendation, response, implementation, evidence, next cycle), each step a dated, sourced record: a kybernesis architecture whose blocks are filled by declared methods.

#### A.3.5 Compliance and Verification

The managerial school (Chayes and Chayes 1993) [S] attributes most non-compliance to ambiguity, incapacity and lags, met by transparency and assistance; Downs, Rocke and Barsoom (1996) [S] replied that high compliance may reflect shallow agreements, a problem of selection and endogeneity. The Paris Agreement's Enhanced Transparency Framework (Article 13) [V] is managerial: first biennial transparency reports by 31 December 2024, then a technical expert review and a facilitative multilateral consideration of progress, "facilitative, non-intrusive, non-punitive".

*For the system.* Compliance is a norm applied to evidence under a standard: the law domain's `norms` and `proof` with tekmerion's charts of evidence. Recording each commitment's depth beside its compliance keeps the Downs critique in view.

### A.4 Negotiation and Value

#### A.4.1 Creating and Claiming Value

Raiffa (1982) [S] founded negotiation analysis as asymmetrically prescriptive: advice to one party, a descriptive model of the others. Lax and Sebenius (1986) [S] showed that creating value and claiming it are inseparable, and named the *negotiator's dilemma*: information that creates value can be used to claim it. Fisher and Ury (1981) [M] introduced the *BATNA*; the *zone of possible agreement* is the set of agreements every party prefers to its BATNA. Sebenius (1983) [V] treated issues and parties as variables: adding an issue valued differently can create a zone where none existed, and adding or removing a party can create or destroy one. Tollison and Willett (1979) [S] gave the theory of *mutually advantageous issue linkage*. Zartman and Berman (1982) [S] described three overlapping phases: diagnosis, the search for a *formula* (a shared definition of the problem and the terms of trade), and details.

#### A.4.2 Two-Level Games

Putnam (1988) [V] modelled a negotiator bargaining internationally while every agreement must be ratified domestically. A party's *win-set* is the set of agreements its constituency would ratify; agreement needs intersecting win-sets, and a small win-set can strengthen a negotiator's hand.

*For the system.* If a ratification rule is a simple game over domestic actors with preferences over agreements, the win-set is computable, and the work can show how a change in a domestic rule moves the zone of agreement.

#### A.4.3 Coalitions, Packages and Axiomatic Bargaining

Coalitions are recorded in practice: the Earth Negotiations Bulletin reports statements "on behalf of" groups, coded across thirty years of climate talks by Castro, Kristof, Kammerer and Cogne (2025) [V] (§6.3). Theory offers minimum winning coalitions (Riker 1962) [M], the core and the nucleolus [M]. A package is a point in a product of issue spaces; linkage pays exactly when parties weigh issues differently. Nash (1950) [V] characterised the solution maximising the product of gains over the disagreement point, from Pareto optimality, symmetry, affine invariance and independence of irrelevant alternatives. Kalai and Smorodinsky (1975) [V] replaced the last axiom by monotonicity. Brams and Togman (1996) [S] applied Brams and Taylor's *Adjusted Winner* procedure to the Camp David accords and found the actual agreement close to its outcome.

*For the system.* nomos's `games` holds equilibria, dominance, the Shapley value and the division of a short estate, but no axiomatic bargaining solutions, core, nucleolus or fair-division procedures; these belong there.

#### A.4.4 Benefit-Sharing in Treaty Practice

The Nagoya Protocol (in force 12 October 2014) [V] makes access to genetic resources subject to the provider's prior informed consent and mutually agreed terms, with an ABS Clearing-House and an internationally recognised certificate of compliance. The WHO Pandemic Agreement, adopted by consensus in May 2025, left its Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing system to an annex that was not concluded by the May 2026 deadline; talks were extended, turning on guaranteed benefits and legal certainty for providers [S, news reports]. Benefit-sharing is a design of three parts: the trigger of an obligation, the share or kind of benefit, and the verification of delivery.

#### A.4.5 The Assessment of Mutual Benefit

"Mutual benefit" is an open term. The Global Partnership for Effective Development Co-operation monitors four principles (country ownership, focus on results, inclusive partnerships, transparency and mutual accountability; its 2023–2026 round drew on 44 partner countries and 93 development partners) [V]: it assesses conduct, not a common measure of gain. Bargaining theory measures gain against a disagreement point, presupposing cardinal utilities parties seldom declare.

*For the system.* Mutual benefit is never one number. Each party's benefit is read under its own construal of the term, with the reading's source (a stated position, a preamble, an analyst's estimate); the work reports whether each party's criterion is met, and separately any common measure the user declares: tekmerion's `construal` (its decision 0008) and kybernesis's dual verdict.

### A.5 Relational Structure

#### A.5.1 Alliances, Memberships, Votes and Sponsorship

ATOP (Leeds, Ritter, Mitchell and Long 2002) [S] codes alliance obligations (defence, offence, neutrality, non-aggression, consultation) from 1815, version 5.1 to 2018. The Correlates of War IGO data, version 3.0 (Pevehouse, Nordstrom, McManus and Jamison 2020) [V], record memberships from 1816 to 2014 in country-year, IGO-year and dyadic forms. The UN Digital Library publishes every recorded General Assembly vote on adopted resolutions from 1946 to resolution 79/328 (5 September 2025), 916,554 entries, for non-commercial use with attribution [V]. Bailey, Strezhnev and Voeten (2017) [S] estimated dynamic ideal points from these votes on one dimension, positions towards the US-led liberal order. Seabra and Mesquita (2022) [S] built a dataset of sponsorship by every member, 2009–2019, an earlier signal than votes.

#### A.5.2 Network Methods

Hafner-Burton, Kahler and Montgomery (2009) [V] recast power in networks as access, brokerage and exit options. The tools in use are the quadratic assignment procedure, exponential random graph models, stochastic actor-oriented models and relational event models, the last used on interactions coded from the Earth Negotiations Bulletin [S].

#### A.5.3 Trust and Reputation

Tomz (2007) [V] built a reputational theory of cooperation from three centuries of sovereign debt, with little evidence of punitive enforcement. Kydd (2005) [M] modelled trust as a belief that the other prefers to reciprocate, updated by costly signals.

*For the system.* tyche's `networks` holds censuses, brokerage, homophily, QAP, MRQAP and conditional uniform tests (its decision 0019), morphe closeness and betweenness (its decision 0012). Missing: dynamic ideal points from ordinal votes and relational event models. Reputation is an estimate, a posterior over a party's type given its record, carrying its model and data (tyche's `bayes`, archeion's provenance).

### A.6 Event Trajectories

#### A.6.1 Machine-Coded Event Data and Their Schemes

GDELT [V] monitors news in over a hundred languages, holding about a quarter of a billion event records from 1979, free for any use with citation and link; it was presented coded in CAMEO (Leetaru and Schrodt 2013) [S]. CAMEO (Gerner, Schrodt, Yilmaz and Abu-Jabr 2002) [S] was built for the study of mediation, with verb codes of two to four digits. ICEWS, built for DARPA, is on Harvard Dataverse and was discontinued on 11 April 2023 [S]. Its intended successor, POLECAT, is coded by active learning, transformer classifiers, question-answering models for actors and recipients, and resolution of actors to Wikipedia articles (Halterman, Schrodt, Beger, Bagozzi and Scarborough 2023) [V], in the PLOVER ontology [V]. PLOVER keeps sixteen active categories (AGREE, CONSULT, SUPPORT, CONCEDE, RETREAT, COOPERATE, AID, DEMAND, DISAPPROVE, REJECT, THREATEN, PROTEST, MOBILIZE, SANCTION, COERCE, ASSAULT), drops CAMEO's comment categories, replaces fine codes by a category-specific *mode* and a general *context*, allows compound actors and optional targets, records the dead, injured and size, and is JSON under CC BY-SA 4.0.

#### A.6.2 Reliability and Critiques

Hammond and Weidmann (2014) [S] found GDELT's agreement with hand-coded conflict data mediocre and its geography biased towards cities. POLECAT's developers reported early false positives [S]. The deeper problem is that event data count *reports*: one statement carried by fifty outlets becomes fifty events unless duplicates are found.

*For the system.* An event should keep the reports it rests on, each with source and passage. tekmerion counts reports sharing an origin once and dilutes a located report's force by the precision of its place and time (its decision 0007): the remedy for duplicated wire stories.

#### A.6.3 Treaty and Negotiation Records

Under Article 102 of the UN Charter every treaty a member concludes is registered and published by the Secretariat, and an unregistered treaty cannot be invoked before any UN organ; General Assembly resolution 97 (I) (14 December 1946) gives effect to it [V]. The UN Treaty Collection holds the Treaty Series and the status of multilateral treaties deposited with the Secretary-General [V]. IISD's Earth Negotiations Bulletin has reported environmental negotiations daily since 1992 [V]. Castro et al. (2025) [V] mined 748 issues on 91 UNFCCC meetings (1995–2023) into 57,375 interventions and 86,763 interactions (agreement, on behalf of, support, opposition), with open code. Their validation is instructive: opposition was found about 1,800 times by machine against about 10,080 by hand, because coders link statements across sentences. The data serve for patterns of cooperation, not the volume of conflict.

#### A.6.4 The Reconstruction of a Negotiation's History

A negotiation's history is rebuilt from successive drafts with bracketed text, records of statements, delegations' reports, archives opened later and interviews. Process tracing (Bennett and Checkel 2015) [V] supplies the logic: hypotheses about mechanisms, the evidence each predicts, and tests of differing strength.

*For the system.* A history is an *account* of events with actors, places and times, each supported by located items with stances, rival accounts kept and weighed: tekmerion's `scenarios` (its decision 0009) and `timelines` (a simple temporal network whose conflicts are named by source; its decision 0005), with zetesis's `tracing`. Draft versions and their differences are archeion's to keep.

### A.7 Archives and Evidence in Diplomacy

#### A.7.1 Records Management and Declassification

ST/SGB/2007/5 [V] charges the UN's Archives and Records Management Section with policy on the use, retention, disposition and access of records; ST/SGB/2007/6 [V] classifies information as unclassified, confidential or strictly confidential and provides for review before transfer to the archives. Its predecessor reviewed strictly confidential records for declassification at twenty years [S]. National diplomatic archives open records by statutory periods [M].

*For the system.* Classification and release are archeion's access rules and retention schedules with holds; each access decision names the rule that decided it.

#### A.7.2 Document Standards

The UN system adopted in March 2017 the Semantic Interoperability Framework for normative and parliamentary documents: Akoma Ntoso localised as AKN4UN for mark-up, and the UN System Document Ontology for entities and identifiers [V]. Akoma Ntoso became the OASIS LegalDocML standard in 2018–2019 [S]. Archival description uses ISAD(G), EAD and Records in Contexts [M]; the Archive Studio already holds forms for Dublin Core, RiC-O and CIDOC CRM (ADR 0120).

#### A.7.3 Provenance and the Verification of Claims

The European External Action Service's reports on foreign information manipulation and interference [V] analyse incidents by a FIMI Exposure Matrix (505 incidents, some 38,000 channels in the third report, 2025) and recommend STIX for sharing incidents broken into their elements (fourth report, 2026). Verifying a claim means tracing it to an origin through channels, the provenance problem of archives.

*For the system.* A claim's chain is a PROV lineage (archeion); its credibility through each channel is tekmerion's cascade; STIX incidents read into symploke as linked data.

#### A.7.4 Evidence in International Dispute Settlement

Article 13 of the WTO's Dispute Settlement Understanding gives panels a broad, discretionary right to seek information, with advisory expert review groups under Appendix 4, and panels may draw adverse inferences from refusals [V, WTO Analytical Index]. The International Court of Justice requires "fully conclusive" evidence for charges of exceptional gravity (2007, reaffirmed 2015), read by commentators as near the criminal standard [S].

*For the system.* Standards of proof are declared standards in the law domain's `proof`; procedures such as Article 13 are rules of its `proceedings`.

### A.8 Knowledge Management in International Organisations

#### A.8.1 Lessons, Communities and Evaluation

The UN peace operations' policy on knowledge management and organisational learning (2020) [S] sets out after-action reviews, lessons-learned studies and end-of-assignment reports. The World Bank resolved in 1996 to become a "knowledge bank"; its evaluators found in 2003 that only a third of operational staff interviewed thought the initiative had changed project design (Gwin 2003) [S]. Wenger (1998) [S] described communities of practice bound by joint enterprise, mutual engagement and a shared repertoire. The UN Evaluation Group's Norms and Standards (2016) [V] set the institutional framework of evaluation functions (enabling environment, policy, responsibility, use and follow-up) and standards of conduct.

*Caveat.* A lesson recorded is not a lesson learned: lessons are filed and unused because they are not attached to the decisions where they apply.

#### A.8.2 Artificial Intelligence, Digital Diplomacy and Science Diplomacy

UNESCO adopted its Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in November 2021 [V]; the UN System Chief Executives Board endorsed ten Principles for the Ethical Use of AI in the UN System in September 2022 [V]; General Assembly resolution 79/325 (26 August 2025) established an Independent International Scientific Panel on AI and a Global Dialogue on AI Governance [V]. Bjola and Holmes (2015) [V] treat digital diplomacy as change management. The Royal Society and AAAS (2010) [V] distinguished science *in* diplomacy, diplomacy *for* science and science *for* diplomacy; a revision was announced in 2025 [S].

*For the system.* A lesson is a historical object with its grade (the history domain's `objects`), transferred to a new setting by its support factors (the policy domain's `transfer`), not as "best practice". A language model may draft, read, extract and code; its output enters as a grade C proposal, found in its document where it quotes, and never changes a verdict. Classified material reaches no external model without an access rule permitting it.

### A.9 Project and Programme Progress

The UN Development Group's Results-Based Management Handbook (2011) [S] harmonised RBM across the UN system around accountability, national ownership and inclusiveness, with a results chain from inputs to impact [M on the chain's terms]. The logical framework was prepared for USAID in 1969–70 by Leon Rosenberg and others [S]; Gasper (2000) [S] criticised its stress on the horizontal logic of indicators over the vertical logic of assumptions. Theories of change entered UN programming with the UNDAF companion guidance of 2017 and the Cooperation Framework guidance of 2019 [S]. The OECD DAC added *coherence*, internal and external, to its evaluation criteria in December 2019 [V]. MOPAN's methodology 3.1 [V] assesses multilateral organisations on twelve key performance indicators (eight of organisational effectiveness, four of results), with 57 micro-indicators and 223 elements from documents, surveys and interviews [S on counts]; MOPAN 4 governs assessments from 2026 [S]. The IATI Standard 2.03 (February 2018) [V] publishes activities, budgets, transactions and results as XML, each result with indicators, baselines, targets, actuals, a flag for whether values aggregate, and references to indicator vocabularies; the Registry links to publishers' files under open licences.

*For the system.* The policy domain holds theories of change in a written form, a chain no better shown than its weakest link, with shared gaps and support factors (ADR 0114); a logframe is its single-path case, and IATI its input. MOPAN-like scores are composites (nomos's `measurement`) whose scores carry their evidence (tekmerion) and whose weights are stated and tested for stability (nomos's `appraisal`).

### A.10 Japanese and Asian Multilateral Practice

Japan's ODA Charter of 1992 became the Development Cooperation Charter in 2015, revised by Cabinet decision on 9 June 2023 [V]: it introduces *co-creation* with partner countries and *offer-type cooperation* (Japan proposes a menu and formulates projects with partners, beside cooperation on request), and restates human security as its guiding idea [V, MOFA White Paper 2023, Diplomatic Bluebook 2024]. The Tokyo International Conference on African Development, held since 1993 [M], is co-organised with the United Nations, UNDP, the World Bank and the African Union Commission; its ninth meeting closed on 22 August 2025 with a declaration under the theme "Co-create innovative solutions with Africa" [V]. Acharya (1997) [S] traced the "ASEAN way" to cooperative security, open regionalism, soft regionalism and flexible consensus; the ASEAN Charter codifies consensus with a Summit fallback and flexible participation (§3.1). APEC's voluntary commitments are a further variant [M].

*For the system.* Who initiates a project (request, offer, co-creation) is a property of a partnership's origin bearing on ownership (§4.5). Co-organisation is a relation with roles, so a forum is not reduced to its host. ASEAN's rules are a parameterised family (a default, a meta-rule for failure, a participation rule) declared as schemes, never hard-coded.

### A.11 A Mapping onto the Infrastructure

#### A.11.1 A Diplomacy Domain (Proposal)

The objects of multilateral practice name things in society, so they belong to a domain (CLAUDE.md §3.7), and the general methods they need to the foundations. A diplomacy domain could hold:

- `institutions`: design on the five dimensions and four problems, Ruggie's properties as checks, memberships as a formal context with regime complexes read off its lattice;
- `rules`: decision rules as simple games (consensus over those present, fallbacks, vetoes, double majorities, participation rules) and their a priori power;
- `negotiation`: parties, issues, reservation values as intervals, packages, win-sets under ratification rules, phases, bargaining outcomes;
- `relations`: typed, multilayer and n-ary relations (obligations, memberships, votes, sponsorships, statements on behalf of others);
- `events`: events in PLOVER's categories, each resting on its reports;
- `mandates` and `reviews`: mandate lifecycles; review cycles with recommendations, responses and evidence;
- `partnerships`: contribution and benefit ledgers, specific and diffuse reciprocity, declared benefit-sharing schemes.

Its place in the order is the user's decision (CLAUDE.md §6.5): a field at height 0 on the foundations, or, more economically, a bridge at height 1 over law (treaties, sources, proceedings) and policy (theories of change, transfer), reusing the law domain's `sources` and the policy domain's `change`.

#### A.11.2 What Goes into the Foundations

New general methods: in nomos, Banzhaf and Penrose measures, power indices of large weighted and compound games, the core and nucleolus (named as next in its decision 0011), Nash, Kalai–Smorodinsky and egalitarian bargaining, Adjusted Winner; in tyche, dynamic ideal points from ordinal votes, relational event models and self-exciting point processes; in symploke, no code, but SKOS vocabularies for PLOVER and CAMEO as data; in the data library, readers for the UN voting CSV, COW IGO, ATOP, IATI XML, Akoma Ntoso, PLOVER JSON and ENB-mined interactions. Everything else in Table 1 exists. The brief credits kybernesis with transfer by a lens with support factors; its record (ADR 0130) lists that as not yet built, and the policy domain's `transfer` serves meanwhile.

#### A.11.3 Ideas the Work Should Take

1. Ruggie's test computed: the scope of norms, the structure of commitments, the window over which reciprocity balances.
2. Rational-design conjectures tested on the work's cases, never assumed.
3. Consensus as a precise rule: no formal objection among those present.
4. A priori power reported beside, never instead of, evidence of influence.
5. Value created and value claimed kept apart; win-sets as part of every party's model.
6. Mutual benefit construed party by party, a common measure only when declared.
7. Events as reports, each origin counted once; models propose, declared rules decide.
8. Review as a facilitative loop; progress read through theories of change fed by IATI; lessons moved by support factors.

Machine-coded events overweight English-language and urban sources and under-code opposition; votes and sponsorships record public positions, not preferences; composite scores hide their weights unless shown. Names of states, economies and disputed areas follow the data library's naming policy (CLAUDE.md §4.1). Classified records stay behind their access rules.

### B.2 The Human–AI Multilateral Phronimos

#### B.2.1 The Record and Its Standing

The monograph is Wanhong Huang, *Future Talent Strategy: The Possibility of the Human–AI Multilateral Phronimos*, Knowledge Commons, issued 2026-11-18, DOI 10.17613/s9xqh-xm397 (a version of 10.17613/q4vzy-9qj02), licensed CC BY-NC-ND 3.0 IGO (Huang 2026) [V]. It is also listed on PhilArchive (record HUAFTS-3) [S]; PhilArchive refused automated reading. The Society's publications page (https://serendip.ngo/publications.html), read this session, did not list it.

The monograph states its own standing with care, and the work should inherit that care: "Its terminology, including *AI-major, post-labor, multilateral, generative–relational system,* and *Human–AI Multilateral Phronimos*, is provisional and paper-local". It says the proposed ideal "has not been demonstrated to improve occupational or organizational outcomes relative to established alternatives", that its 263 cited works were checked "at unequal depths", and that its empirical work recruited no new participants (an exploratory reanalysis of a randomised human–AI chess experiment, 100 participants and 3,000 decisions).

#### B.2.2 The Central Claim

The Executive Orientation gives the claim, which it calls modest: "when capacities for producing answers and artifacts expand, the conditions for identifying worthwhile problems, judging competing ends, understanding heterogeneous parties, and organizing legitimate forms of collective action remain objects of practical inquiry." Whether those conditions call for "a distinct profession, a distributed competence across many occupations, or an institutional arrangement is an open question." Practical wisdom is reconstructed as "context-sensitive, revisable judgment about action" among human and artificial participants who differ in knowledge, resources, authority and mode of operation (abstract, via the DataCite record).

Three domains of human development are distinguished as "an analytical heuristic rather than a universal taxonomy": knowledge inquiry, creation and practical wisdom, mutually dependent (Figure 1.1). Noetarium is named as the author's infrastructure for inquiry, and a proposed Poietarium for creation, "treated here as the author's examples of infrastructural aspirations, rather than as independently validated evidence". The phronimos concerns "how inquiry and creation are interpreted, evaluated, and enacted in circumstances where good practice remains contestable and historically situated." Multilateral Phronimos, the work, is therefore the third member of that triad made infrastructure.

#### B.2.3 Key Concepts

*Multilateral.* Kept from diplomacy because it "directs attention to relations among multiple parties rather than to an isolated decision-maker", and stipulated to mean "organized engagement among heterogeneous participants, perspectives, capacities, and institutional positions whose interaction matters for shared or intersecting practical consequences." It "does not automatically imply symmetrical authority, equal moral status, shared goals, or consensus", and a machine's participation "is not, by itself, a determination that it bears moral rights or legal responsibility" (§1.2). From diplomacy the monograph takes this: "A generalist with diplomatic competence should be able to engage differences without presuming that differences can be eliminated through better translation or a single shared objective" (§3.6.1).

*Generalized language.* "An identifiable repertoire of distinctions, representations, validation procedures, and communicative practices through which a system or community makes matters intelligible and supports action": scientific explanation, regulatory classification, market valuation and engineering specification among them (§1.2).

*The practical-judgment function* (Definition 10.1). "A revisable arrangement of inquiry and intervention that helps participants connect evidential claims, situated purposes, feasible alternatives, differentiated standing, and authorized decisions, while making consequential uncertainties and contestable translations traceable." Who performs it, "a dedicated person, shared team, software-supported procedure, or existing institution", is left open. A work can be one of these.

*The relational coordination function* (Definition 10.4): authorised and reviewable practices that (i) identify consequential dependencies between differently situated contributors and systems, (ii) preserve or reconstruct the warrants on claims crossing them, (iii) make interests and authority boundaries legible, (iv) support challenge, escalation and correction, and (v) "maintain a feasible route for the undertaking to continue without a particular coordinator."

*Typed dependencies* (§10.4.2). An undertaking is written R = (V, {E_k}_{k∈K}, D, Γ): V the participants and systems (people, units, AI models, datasets, review forums, bodies), E_k ⊆ V × V the directed relations of type k (evidential reliance, communication, access to confidential material, authorisation, responsibility for review, rights of challenge), D the decision points and Γ the constraints. For each decision one records its evidential dependencies, its formal authorities, those affected and the venues of contest. "Missing observations must not be interpreted automatically as absent relationships", and a complete communication graph may coexist with an ambiguous authorisation graph.

*Objectives.* Execution, evaluation and formation of objectives are kept apart (§4.10). Legitimate collective objective formation (Definition 8.4) asks whose interests and rights are implicated, who holds authority, what evidence is admissible, "which constraints cannot be overridden by preference aggregation alone", and what review, remedy or withdrawal exists.

*Translation* (Definition 8.3). Warrant-preserving translation makes a claim usable in another context "while appropriately retaining, communicating, or explicitly qualifying" its referent, provenance, assumptions, uncertainty, range, counterexamples, contestable categories and the authority of those entitled to act on it; it "can explicitly mark an unresolved remainder; it need not manufacture equivalence."

*Relational position* (Definition 9.5). "The configuration of effective capabilities, constraints, recognized claims, dependencies, and authorized or de facto opportunities available to a specified unit at a stated time, together with the relevant processes through which those conditions arose", with formal and effective capacity kept apart.

*Emergence of meaning and value* (Definition 9.4). Situated value emergence is "a change in recognized or potentially realizable goods, contributions, capabilities, or evaluative relations", whose worth "remains answerable to relevant epistemic, normative, and institutional criteria." That an artefact exists, that a participant ascribes meaning to it and that it is worthwhile are three propositions.

*Decision-capable engagement* (Definition 9.7). Participants can identify claims and disagreements, make the decision rule intelligible, offer challenge and response, and either implement a justified provisional decision or explain deferral; persistent disagreement is permitted. The requirement is "closure without epistemic finality".

*Longitudinal evaluative trajectory* (Definition 12.4). A documented sequence E_{0:T} = (E_t, Q_t, K_t, O_t, U_t), t = 0..T: the undertaking, the evaluative dimensions and their interpretations, the constraints asserted by competent authorities, "the observations and absences of evidence", and the uncertainty and disputes; it "includes an account of how each component changed and why." No "time-invariant scalar of institutional quality" is posited, and an indicator may be compared over time only across a "defensible bridge between versions".

*Contestable judgment and infrastructure.* A contestable public evaluative judgment discloses its evaluand, warrants, constraints, standing, disagreements, alternatives, authority and routes of review (Definition 12.5). A contestable knowledge and learning infrastructure supports "the authorized creation, retention, retrieval, interpretation, correction and withdrawal of knowledge objects"; "Mere storage volume, model capability or openness of access is insufficient" (Definition 13.7). Purpose-bounded information stewardship governs collection, access, retention, correction, transfer and disposal so that inferences can be reconstructed or challenged (Definition 14.3).

#### B.2.4 Formal Results and Diagnosed Failures

The formal results are deliberately narrow. Feasible-action sets order configurations: [C₁] ⪯ [C₂] iff F_s(C₁) ⊆ F_s(C₂) (Eq. 9.1), a partial order on classes and a preorder on configurations; two configurations can be incomparable, and the order "establishes neither a hierarchy of subjects nor a moral ranking of possible futures." Observed agreement does not identify its grounds when two mechanisms induce the same records (Proposition 10.1). Two alternatives that cross on two dimensions have no unique overall ranking without a justified priority rule or exchange rate (Proposition 12.1). Disclosure alone does not remove structural discretion (Proposition 14.2); keeping a record that serves no authorised reconstruction cannot be justified by accountability alone (Proposition 14.3); delegation without a review path cannot guarantee remedy (Proposition 14.4).

Table 10.2 gives diagnostics for a coordinating function, each a failure to watch: evidential transfer (scope or uncertainty lost across a handoff), authorisation (recommendations mistaken for decisions), participation (consultation without opportunity to contest), AI dependencies ("Repeated outputs treated as independent corroboration"), succession (rationale lost with a person or system) and resource burden. Chapter 14 adds concentration of interpretive authority, conflicts of interest, unequal access and capture, with "grounds for rejecting a professional arrangement".

#### B.2.5 Consequences for the Work's Design

Five follow. The work is a candidate realisation of the practical-judgment function, and must be judged as one, against alternatives and with its own grounds for rejection. Relations are typed and multiple, with absence of evidence kept distinct from absence of relation. Values are vectors with their interpretations, never a forced scalar. Trajectories record the evaluative frame, the constraints and the absences as well as observations. Every record is held for a stated purpose, can be corrected and withdrawn, and lets the work's own coordination be continued without it.

### B.3 SDG 17 as a Measurement System

#### B.3.1 Structure, Tiers and Custodians

Goal 17, "Strengthen the means of implementation and revitalize the Global Partnership for Sustainable Development", has nineteen targets in five groups: finance (17.1–17.5), technology (17.6–17.8), capacity-building (17.9), trade (17.10–17.12) and systemic issues, subdivided into policy and institutional coherence (17.13–17.15), multi-stakeholder partnerships (17.16–17.17) and data, monitoring and accountability (17.18–17.19) (UN DESA, Goal 17 page) [V]. The tier classification of 30 March 2026 counts 165 Tier I, 61 Tier II and 8 multi-tier indicators across the framework; Tier I means an established methodology and regular data for at least half of countries and population in every region, Tier II an established methodology without regular data, and no Tier III indicator remains (UNSD 2026) [V]. In Goal 17 every indicator is Tier I except 17.14.1, 17.15.1 and 17.16.1 (Tier II) and 17.3.1 (Tier I or II by resource). The custodians are given in Table 11.1; the OECD was added as co-custodian of 17.14.1 in January 2026.

#### B.3.2 Means of Implementation

The finance, technology and trade indicators are ratio-scale aggregates from administrative statistics (revenue, ODA, remittances, debt service, connectivity, tariffs, exports). The *Sustainable Development Goals Report 2025* records DAC members' ODA at $212.1 billion in 2024, 0.33% of their GNI and 7.1% lower in real terms than in 2023, and 2.6 billion people still offline in 2024 (UN DESA 2025) [V]. For the work these are context, the material conditions under which partners act, not measures of partnership.

#### B.3.3 Policy and Institutional Coherence

*17.13.1* is a "Macroeconomic Dashboard" (World Bank): a panel of series, deliberately not a scalar. *17.14.1*, mechanisms for policy coherence for sustainable development, is a composite: eight themes (political commitment; long-term vision; integration into policy and finance; whole-of-government coordination; sub-national engagement; stakeholder engagement; impact assessment; monitoring, evaluation and reporting), each three questions scored 0–4, summed to at most 96 and expressed as a percentage. It is self-reported by governments through a UNEP (2020, 2022) and then UNEP/OECD questionnaire (2025, 2027, 2029), and its metadata stress that "there is no one-size-fits-all approach" (UN Statistics Division, metadata 17.14.1, updated 2026-03-27) [V]. *17.15.1*, providers' use of country-owned results frameworks, is the mean of three percentages over new interventions: objectives drawn from country frameworks, results indicators drawn from them, and indicators monitored through government systems (metadata 17.16.1) [V].

#### B.3.4 Multi-Stakeholder Partnerships

*17.16.1* counts countries "reporting progress in multi-stakeholder development effectiveness monitoring frameworks". Such frameworks have voluntarily agreed indicators that "measure the strength of the relationship between development actors", are country-led and involve at least the public sector, private sector and civil society. The GPEDC exercise is the main source, with the ECOSOC Development Cooperation Forum's mutual accountability survey and SEGIB's work on South–South co-operation named as complements. The rule of computation is ordinal: a country counts as progressing when, since its last cycle, more of its framework's indicators show a positive than a negative trend; providers are assessed on use of country frameworks, transparency, annual and medium-term predictability, aid on budget, use of country systems and untied aid; recipients on national strategies, the enabling environment for civil society, aid on budget, mutual accountability, gender budgeting and public financial management. Collection was biennial (2016, 2018) and has been rolling since 2023 (metadata 17.16.1, updated 2024-09-27) [V].

*17.17.1*, the goal's only direct measure of partnerships, counts US dollars committed to public–private partnerships for infrastructure, from the World Bank's Private Participation in Infrastructure database: energy, ICT backbone, transport and water, in low- and middle-income countries only. Its metadata say that health and education partnerships are not captured and that the database "does not collect data on civil society partnerships" (metadata 17.17.1, updated 2024-08-02) [V].

#### B.3.5 Data, Monitoring and Accountability

*17.18.1* is now measured partly by the World Bank's Statistical Performance Indicators (pillars 4 and 5, data sources and data infrastructure), which replaced the Statistical Capacity Index (metadata 17.18.1b) [V]; its other part (17.18.1a) is co-held with Open Data Watch, whose Open Data Inventory it uses [S]. *17.18.2* and *17.18.3* (PARIS21) count countries with statistical legislation complying with the Fundamental Principles and with fully funded statistical plans; *17.19.1* (PARIS21) is resources for statistical capacity; *17.19.2* (UNSD) is the share of countries with a census in ten years and 100% birth and 80% death registration. In 2024, 159 countries reported compliant legislation (132 in 2019), and of 137 implementing a statistical plan only 95 were fully funded (UN DESA 2025) [V].

#### B.3.6 Data Sources and Access

The UN Global SDG Indicators Database is exposed through a JSON API (https://unstats.un.org/sdgapi/, e.g. `/v1/sdg/Goal/List`) [V] and through SDMX under one data structure definition for all indicators, the dataflow DF_SDG_GLH, with M49 area codes and a separate metadata API; DSD version 1.23 was released on 12 December 2025 (IAEG-SDGs SDMX Working Group page) [S]. The metadata repository (https://unstats.un.org/sdgs/metadata/) holds one harmonised file per indicator, the complete set "as of 1 October 2026" [V].

#### B.3.7 Known Gaps

Five gaps matter for the work. The partnership indicators measure the *conditions* of partnership (country-led monitoring, use of country systems) and money in infrastructure, not partnerships themselves: there is no global indicator of a partnership's existence, membership, deliverables, value to its parties or results. Three of the five coherence and partnership indicators (17.13.1–17.17.1) are Tier II, self-reported and episodic. 17.16.1 and 17.14.1 sum or count ordinal judgements, so their arithmetic needs a declared meaningfulness check. 17.17.1 excludes high-income countries, social sectors and civil society. And progress on 17.15.1 has stalled: the OECD–UNDP *Progress Report 2026* reports providers' use of country results frameworks "stagnated at 64% since 2018" and use of country data and systems "particularly low and declining" (OECD and UNDP 2026) [S].

### B.4 Partnerships as Objects of Governance

#### B.4.1 Registries and the SMART Criteria

The UN's SDG Actions Platform is "a global registry of voluntary policies, commitments, multi-stakeholder partnerships and other initiatives", consolidating the registries of Rio+20 (2012), the SIDS Conference (2014) and the Ocean Conferences (2017, 2022); entries are reviewed by the Secretariat against SMART criteria: Specific (concrete deliverables tied to goals and targets, and a defined role for each partner), Measurable, Achievable, Resource-based ("a secured resource base, rather than merely project proposals") and Time-bound (UN DESA, platform pages) [V]. Progress reports are free text posted on the initiative's page, and in November 2026 review and updating of entries was "temporarily paused" [V]. A registry of declared commitments with unstructured progress is thus the present state of practice.

#### B.4.2 Value and the Partnership Delta

The Partnering Initiative (TPI) and UN DESA give the practitioner's vocabulary. Partnerships range from leverage/exchange through combine/integrate to system transformation (Stibbe and Prescott 2020) [S]. *Collaborative advantage* is what lets a partnership do more than single actors; the *partnership delta* ΔP is "the additional impact a partnership delivers compared with single actor approaches"; and "every partnership must create net value for each partner", value being mission value, organisational gain (financial and in-kind) and indirect gains (legitimacy, reputation, knowledge, influence), net of staff time and contributions (TPI, "Value in partnerships") [V]. The DESA–TPI guide lists eleven ways partnerships create value, among them complementary resources, shared risk, shifted norms and scale (Stibbe, Reid and Gilbert 2018) [S]. ΔP is a counterfactual quantity, so it needs a design (peira) and a comparison regime, which practice rarely supplies.

#### B.4.3 Conditions of Effectiveness

Pattberg and Widerberg (2016) [V], from a systematic review, give nine conditions in three groups: actors (optimal partner mix; effective leadership), process (stringent goal-setting; sustained funding; professional process management; monitoring, reporting, evaluation and learning) and context (active meta-governance; favourable political and social context; fit to problem structure). Their earlier sample of about 340 partnerships registered after the 2002 Johannesburg summit found about 38% with low or no measurable output after more than five years, and only 15% with a budget plan and 5% with a disclosed memorandum of understanding. Beisheim and Liese (2014) [S] studied 21 transnational partnerships and 45 projects in areas of limited statehood, with institutional design and process management as conditions of effectiveness. Beisheim and Simon (2018) [S] found most actors in the UN context favouring system-wide metagovernance of partnerships by principles, rules and procedures. Higham et al. (2024) [S] conclude that SDG-era partnerships reproduce many of their predecessors' shortcomings; Bäckstrand et al. (2026) [V] revisit synergies, effectiveness and legitimacy after a decade. The nine conditions are naturally set-theoretic conditions for a case-oriented comparison (zetesis's QCA), with the outcome defined by a declared scheme.

#### B.4.4 Legitimacy and Accountability

Bäckstrand (2006) [S] separates input legitimacy (participation, accountability, transparency) from output legitimacy (effectiveness) for partnership networks; Biermann et al. (2007) [S] asked whether the Johannesburg partnerships kept their promise. Accountability in a partnership has at least three directions: to its members (mutual), to those it affects, and to the public bodies whose mandates it touches. The GPEDC's mutual accountability and the monograph's contestable judgment (Definition 12.5) give operational content: an identifiable way to challenge material reasons and obtain a proportionate response.

#### B.4.5 Modes of Failure

The record supports a typology: *paper partnerships* (registered and inactive), *drift* (activity unrelated to stated goals), *resource fragility*, *capture* (powerful partners setting objectives), *collaborative inertia* (Huxham and Vangen 2005) [M], *accountability gaps* and *succession loss*. Ansell and Gash (2008) [M] name starting conditions, institutional design, facilitative leadership and a cycle of trust and commitment as the conditions whose absence predicts failure. Each type is detectable from a structured record (status, transactions, results, typed relations, dissent), the strongest argument for keeping one.

### B.5 Effective Development Co-operation

#### B.5.1 From Paris to Busan

The Paris Declaration (2005) set five principles (ownership, alignment, harmonisation, managing for results, mutual accountability) with twelve indicators; the Accra Agenda for Action (2008) pressed country ownership, inclusive partnerships and results with open accounting (Wikipedia, "High level forums on aid effectiveness", read this session) [S]. The Busan Partnership (2011) widened the focus "from aid effectiveness to the challenges of effective development" and created the Global Partnership for Effective Development Co-operation (GPEDC), on four shared principles: ownership of development priorities by developing countries, focus on results, inclusive development partnerships, and transparency and mutual accountability [M] (the first confirmed in a secondary record [S]).

#### B.5.2 The GPEDC Monitoring Framework

The 2023–2026 round organises its results under four dimensions: a whole-of-society approach, the state and use of country systems, transparency, and leaving no one behind; it adds the Kampala Principles Assessment on private-sector engagement and indicators on statistical capacity, and it is the official source for SDG indicators 5.c.1, 17.15.1 and 17.16.1 (GPEDC 2023; OECD and UNDP 2026) [S]. Its data are reported by a national coordinator (typically finance, planning or foreign affairs) who consults providers, civil society, the private sector and trade unions; no adjustment is made to submitted data (metadata 17.16.1) [V]. Gisselquist (2024) [S] argues that whether the principles improve development outcomes cannot be settled without clearer definitions and measurement. The framework is a model for the work: indicators of a *relationship*, collected by the parties, reviewed jointly and followed by dialogue (22 partner governments had convened such follow-up by May 2026 [S]).

#### B.5.3 South–South and Triangular Co-operation

South–South co-operation rests on principles that differ from the DAC's, mutual benefit, non-conditionality, national ownership and horizontality among them (the Nairobi outcome of 2009) [M]. Its measurement is now part of SDG 17: the UN Statistical Commission adopted in March 2022 a voluntary framework to measure South–South co-operation, built by countries of the South and covering financial and in-kind flows, with UNCTAD co-custodian of 17.3.1 for these flows (UNCTAD project pages) [S]. TOSSD, Total Official Support for Sustainable Development, measures officially supported resources, and private finance mobilised by them, beyond ODA: Pillar I flows to recipient countries, Pillar II support to international public goods and global challenges; the International Forum on TOSSD is the other custodian of 17.3.1 (TOSSD reporting instructions) [S]. SEGIB's work on South–South co-operation is named in the 17.16.1 metadata as a complementary framework [V]; its Ibero-American reports are a long-running regional record [M].

#### B.5.4 Knowledge Sharing Between Countries

Knowledge sharing is partly a target (17.6 includes "knowledge-sharing on mutually agreed terms") and partly a practice with its own repositories: UNOSSC's series *Good Practices in South–South and Triangular Cooperation for Sustainable Development* (volumes of 107 to about 200 practices, the fourth in 2022) is carried into the South–South Galaxy platform, where solution owners upload practices (South–South Galaxy pages) [S]. A good practice so recorded is a claim that something worked *there*; whether it will work *here* is Cartwright and Hardie's question (2012) [M], which the policy domain's `transfer` already asks. The literature on policy transfer and lesson-drawing (Dolowitz and Marsh 2000; Rose 1991) [M] adds that what moves is often a label, not a mechanism.

### B.6 Data Standards for Co-operation

#### B.6.1 IATI

The IATI Standard (version 2.03) describes an *activity* with an identifier, a reporting organisation, participating organisations, status, dates, locations, sectors, tags, policy markers, budgets, planned disbursements, transactions, documents, related activities and results (IATI Activity Standard 2.03) [V]. Four codelists make it a partnership record. Organisation roles: Funding, Accountable, Extending, Implementing. Activity status: Pipeline/identification, Implementation, Finalisation, Closed, Cancelled, Suspended. Related-activity types: Parent, Child, Sibling, Co-funded, Third Party. Transaction types: thirteen, from incoming funds, commitments, disbursements and expenditure to pledges, loans and equity. The `result` element carries indicators with a `measure` (Unit, Percentage, Nominal, Ordinal, Qualitative), an `ascending` flag, a baseline and periods with targets and actuals, each disaggregable by dimension and location and with comments and documents; tags may use the SDG goal and target vocabularies (IATI codelists) [V]. IATI therefore already declares the scale type of each result indicator, which the work should honour.

#### B.6.2 CRS and TOSSD

The OECD Creditor Reporting System records activities of DAC members and others with purpose codes (several per activity since reporting on 2017 flows), channel codes, policy markers and Rio markers, and an SDG focus field introduced partly to serve TOSSD, after a 2017 workshop judged SDG-linked activities impossible to identify from purpose codes alone (OECD DAC statistical papers) [S]. TOSSD shares CRS classifications and adds the SDG field at activity level [S].

#### B.6.3 AidData

AidData's Global Chinese Development Finance Dataset, version 3.0 (2023), covers more than 20,000 projects in 165 low- and middle-income countries, 2000–2021, financed by 791 official Chinese lenders and donors, compiled by the TUFF method from open sources, with a geocoded companion of 9,405 projects (AidData 2023) [S]. It shows how a record of underreported co-operation can be built from scattered sources with provenance, which is the work's situation for non-DAC and non-state parties.

#### B.6.4 Linking Progress Across Standards

Progress is reported by status codes and transactions (IATI), annual flows (CRS, TOSSD), results against baselines and targets (IATI results; country frameworks under 17.15.1) and narrative (the SDG Actions Platform). The records link through IATI identifiers, related-activity links, organisation identifiers (org-id.guide) [M], SDG tags and CRS purpose codes; in linked data they map to W3C PROV activities and the W3C Organization Ontology [M], which symploke can hold with their provenance.

### B.7 Values and Benefits

#### B.7.1 Mutual Benefit, Shared Value and Public Value

*Mutual benefit* is net value to each party (Section 4.2), a vector over parties and kinds. *Shared value* (Porter and Kramer 2011) [M] is value to a firm created by addressing a social need, criticised for presuming alignment where interests conflict. *Public value* (Moore 1995; Bozeman 2007; Bryson, Crosby and Bloomberg 2014) [M] is value judged by a public through legitimate procedures, a matter of objective formation, not measurement alone. The monograph's Definition 9.4 and Proposition 12.1 discipline all three.

#### B.7.2 Access and Benefit-Sharing as a Multilateral Case

The Nagoya Protocol (adopted 29 October 2010, in force 12 October 2014) requires that benefits from the utilisation of genetic resources be shared fairly and equitably with the providing Party on mutually agreed terms (MAT), that access rest on prior informed consent (PIC), and that utilisation be monitored at checkpoints along the value chain; benefits may be monetary (royalties) or non-monetary (research results), and an ABS Clearing-House publishes requirements and authorities (CBD, "About the Nagoya Protocol") [V]. Decision 16/2 of COP 16 (2024) created the Cali Fund for benefits from digital sequence information, launched on 25 February 2025, expecting contributions from commercial users and exempting public research; more than seventy developing countries objected in 2025 to contributions being framed as voluntary donations (Cali Fund records) [S]. Its indicative rates of 1% of profits or 0.1% of revenue [M] are to be settled by the COP. The case gives the work a template: a benefit is a typed transfer with a legal basis (PIC, MAT), a provenance chain (checkpoints, certificates) and a dispute over its very kind (right or donation), which tekmerion's construal can read under each reading.

#### B.7.3 Stakeholder Value Mapping

Value mapping tools ask, for each stakeholder, value captured, value missed or destroyed, and new value opportunities (Bocken et al. 2013) [M]; partnership practice adds power–interest grids and TPI's partnership health checks (Stibbe and Prescott 2020) [S]. These are qualitative instruments whose outputs are ordinal or nominal and should be stored as such.

#### B.7.4 Plural Values

The IPBES *Methodological Assessment of the Diverse Values and Valuation of Nature* (2022; summary for policymakers, DOI 10.5281/zenodo.6522392) [V] offers the most developed typology of plural values: worldviews and life frames (living from, with, in and as nature), broad values, and specific values, instrumental, intrinsic and relational, with more than fifty valuation methods reviewed and the claim that which values count is decided in valuation processes [M for the typology's details]. For partnerships it licenses keeping each party's values in its own generalised language, translated with remainders marked (Definition 8.3), and refusing a single monetary equivalent where no party authorised one.

### B.8 Interlinkages and Coherence

Nilsson, Griggs and Visbeck (2016) [S] score target interactions on a seven-point scale from +3, "indivisible", to −3, "cancelling", with 0 "consistent"; the intermediate labels, reinforcing (+2), enabling (+1), constraining (−1) and counteracting (−2), are [M], developed in ICSU's guide (2017) [M]. Interactions are directional and context-dependent. Pradhan et al. (2017) [S] classified significant positive and negative correlations between indicator series across 227 countries as synergies and trade-offs, finding SDG 1 most synergetic and SDG 12 most often in trade-offs. Le Blanc (2015) [M] read the goals as a network of targets, and Weitz et al. (2018) [M] used cross-impact matrices to rank targets by systemic influence. Policy coherence for sustainable development, in the OECD Recommendation of 2019 (OECD/LEGAL/0381) [S] and in 17.14.1's eight themes, is the institutional side.

For the work, a partnership's targets, its parties' commitments and its activities form a signed, directed and contextual graph; Nilsson's scale is an ordinal labelling of its edges, correlations are evidence for the labels, not the labels themselves, and each label needs a source and a context of validity.

### B.9 Evaluation and Learning

#### B.9.1 Criteria and Joint Evaluation

The OECD DAC revised its criteria in 2019, adding *coherence* to relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, impact and sustainability, explicitly to "better capture linkages, systems thinking, partnership dynamics, and complexity" (OECD DAC 2019) [S]. Joint evaluations by several partners share terms of reference, governance and findings, with known costs in time and in lowest-common-denominator conclusions (OECD DAC 2006) [M].

#### B.9.2 Developmental Evaluation

Developmental evaluation (Patton 2011) [M] accompanies innovation under complexity, feeding judgement continuously rather than rendering a final verdict; it matches the monograph's warrant-sensitive reflective revision (Definition 13.5) and kybernesis's loop.

#### B.9.3 Global and Regional Programmes

The World Bank's Independent Evaluation Group, under the OECD/DAC Network on Development Evaluation, set out principles and standards for evaluating global and regional partnership programmes, with chapters on evaluation governance, participation and inclusion, transparency and disclosure, and criteria (IEG 2007, http://hdl.handle.net/10986/6601) [S]. Global programmes are partnerships whose governance itself must be evaluated: who sits on the board, whose voice counts, how the secretariat is held to account.

#### B.9.4 City-to-City Co-operation as Multilateral Practice

City networks are multilateral practice without sovereigns. UCLG (from 2004), ICLEI (from 1990) and C40 (from 2005) [M] organise peer exchange, commitments and reporting; the Global Covenant of Mayors' Common Reporting Framework, reported through the CDP–ICLEI Track and assessed by CDP and ICLEI, covered more than 12,600 cities and local governments by 2024 (GCoM and CDP guidance) [S]. Voluntary Local Reviews carry SDG reporting below the state, with UN global guiding elements [S]; New York City's, among the first (2018) [M], is a usable case. These networks supply the work with commitment records, standardised indicators and peer-learning events of a kind the national framework lacks.

### B.10 A Mapping to the Needs of the Work

*Governance.* Adopt the monograph's objective formation (Definition 8.4), decision-capable engagement (9.7), relational coordination (10.4) and safeguards (Table 10.3); the Busan principles and mutual accountability as a declared scheme; SMART as a screen for commitments. A decision is a record with its authority, evidence, those affected and routes of challenge (the monograph's Appendix D.3 asks for a minimum decision record). kybernesis runs the loop and keeps standing and measure apart; apodeixis decides what follows under each reading of a mandate; nomos supplies voting rules and power indices; the law domain's docket on archeion's journal is the model of a decision log.

*Values and benefits.* A value ledger: for each party and kind (mission, organisational, indirect; instrumental, intrinsic, relational), the claimed value with its source, scale type, generalised language and standing. No aggregate without an authorised rule (Proposition 12.1); comparison by dominance and declared rules (nomos's `appraisal`); the Shapley value or a bargaining solution as one reading among others; tekmerion's `construal` to read "mutual benefit" under each reading of its terms.

*Relational structure.* The typed multigraph R = (V, {E_k}, D, Γ), with IATI roles, memberships, authorisation, evidential reliance, review and challenge as edge types, and absence of observation distinct from absence of tie: held in symploke (W3C ORG, PROV, checked by SHACL), measured by tyche's `networks` and morphe, its schema verified by kategoria; relational position (Definition 9.5) is a time-indexed view.

*Project progress.* IATI activities, status, transactions and results (baseline, target, actual, measure) as the native form; the policy domain's theories of change from activity to outcome; GPEDC indicators (predictability, use of country systems, alignment) computed from the activity record rather than asked again.

*Archive objects.* Agreements, memoranda, minutes, reports and datasets as archeion holdings with digests, provenance, custody, retention and access rules, packaged as BagIt, codebooks in DDI; purpose-bounded stewardship (Definition 14.3) as the policy.

*Evidence.* tekmerion's charts, warrant, reviews and located evidence; peira for the designs a partnership delta needs; zetesis's QCA over Pattberg and Widerberg's nine conditions; the policy domain's grading and transfer. Repeated model outputs never count as independent corroboration (Table 10.2); tekmerion already counts reports of one origin once.

*Event trajectories.* The longitudinal evaluative trajectory (Definition 12.4) as the schema of a partnership's history; tekmerion's `timelines` for statements of time; archeion's journal for the sequence of record; kinesis where a dynamical model is warranted.

*Knowledge and its evolution.* Practices as knowledge objects with the context of their success (South–South Galaxy, VLRs, evaluations); transfer by kybernesis's planned lens and the policy domain's `transfer`; heuresis's evolution, as in the needs domain, to search for practices and admit them by a judge; reflective revision (Definition 13.5) as the record of how a judgement changed.

*A domain.* The objects named here (partnerships, commitments, providers, activities, benefits) name things in society, so by the infrastructure's placement rule they belong to a domain, beside the diplomacy domain Part 1 proposes or as its module, at height 0 on kybernesis, tekmerion, archeion, nomos, symploke and zetesis, with the policy domain beneath it. Candidate modules: `parties`, `commitments`, `activities`, `values`, `coherence`, `effectiveness`. The name and place are the user's to decide.
