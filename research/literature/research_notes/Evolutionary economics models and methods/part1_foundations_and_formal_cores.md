## 1. Nelson and Winter (1982) and successors: routines, search, imitation, investment and market selection

### Takeaway
The Nelson–Winter (NW) models are discrete-time stochastic Markov processes on a population of firms whose state is (capital stock, technique or productivity, behavioural parameters such as R&D intensities); transitions are (i) satisficing- or R&D-driven search that draws innovations or imitates best practice, (ii) a market that sets price from aggregate output, and (iii) an investment rule that converts profits into capital, so that selection works through differential capital growth. The original programs were never fully documented; the canonical restatements are Andersen's NELWIN reconstructions and the LSD implementations, with later variants replacing fixed rules by learning (e.g. genetic algorithms).

### Cited Findings
- **Bibliographic anchor.** Nelson, R.R. and Winter, S.G. (1982), *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change*, Belknap/Harvard University Press — [SSRN abstract 1496211](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1496211) [S].
- **The models were never fully documented; reconstructions exist.** Andersen et al. (1996), "The Nelson and Winter Models Revisited: Prototypes for Computer-Based Reconstruction of Schumpeterian Competition" (Aalborg/DRUID working paper 96-5) state that NW's simulation models "were never fully documented" and provide reconstructed versions, NELWIN78 (from ch. 13 of the book) and NELWIN77 (from ch. 12) — [IDEAS RePEc aal:abbswp:96-5](https://ideas.repec.org/p/aal/abbswp/96-5.html) [S]. Its framing: "a verbal account of Schumpeterian competition can naturally be transformed into a description of a computational process" — same source [S].
- **Implementation in LSD.** Valente, M. and Andersen, E.S., "A hands-on approach to evolutionary simulation: Nelson–Winter models in the Laboratory for Simulation Development", *Electronic Journal of Evolutionary Modeling and Economic Dynamics* (e-JEMED) no. 1003 — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/jem/ejemed/1003.html) [S].
- **Ch. 9 growth model is the first evolutionary growth model.** A later restatement describes NW (1982, ch. 9) as "the first evolutionary growth model" and NW behaviour as "mutation and imitation rules operating on the firms' operational parameters" — [Silverberg & Verspagen, IIASA WP-94-084 (search summary)](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/id/eprint/4127/1/WP-94-084.pdf) [S].
- **Firms adopt behavioural rules (mark-ups, payback periods, R&D targets) and are profit-motivated without maximising** — [summary of the book, Stafforini catalogue page](https://stafforini.com/works/nelson-1982-evolutionary-theory-economic/) [S].
- **NW ch. 12/13 model of Schumpeterian competition (state, transitions, parameters)** [M; reconstructed from the book and Andersen's NELWIN accounts, not re-read this session]:
  - State of firm *i* at period *t*: capital K_i(t), productivity per unit of capital A_i(t), innovative and imitative R&D per unit capital r_in,i and r_im,i (fixed behavioural parameters, i.e. "routines").
  - Output Q_i = A_i K_i; industry output Q = Σ Q_i; price from an exogenous demand curve P = D(Q) (constant elasticity η; the book's runs use unit or near-unit elasticity).
  - Profit per unit capital π_i = P·A_i − c − r_in,i − r_im,i, with c a constant production cost per unit capital.
  - Search draws: imitation succeeds with probability a_im·r_im,i·K_i (copy the industry best practice Â(t) = max_j A_j(t)); innovation succeeds with probability a_in·r_in,i·K_i, and draws a productivity either around an exogenously rising "latent productivity" (science-based regime: log draw ~ Normal(log Ã(t), σ²), Ã(t) growing at a fixed rate) or around the firm's own current productivity (cumulative regime). New A_i(t+1) = max{A_i(t), imitation draw, innovation draw}.
  - Investment: gross investment per unit capital is a function of the price–cost ratio P·A_i/c, the firm's market share s_i (a Cournot-like "desired mark-up" that rises with share restrains large firms), and financing (profits plus borrowing proportional to profits), floored at zero; K_i(t+1) = (1 − δ)K_i(t) + I_i(t)K_i(t). The exact functional form of the share term should be taken from the book or NELWIN, not from these notes.
  - Main parameters: η, c, δ (depreciation), a_in, a_im, σ, latent growth rate, bank leverage, number of firms, initial conditions.
- **NW ch. 9 growth model** [M]: firms hold techniques (labour and capital input coefficients); a firm searches only when its rate of return falls below a satisficing target; search is local in technique space or imitative; capital accumulates from retained profits; the wage clears a labour market. Runs were set against Solow's (1957) US data 1909–1949 and reproduced the aggregate series qualitatively. The satisficing threshold value in the book should be checked (often cited as a target rate of return on capital).
- **Learning replaces fixed routines.** Yildizoglu (2002), "Competing R&D strategies in an evolutionary industry model", *Computational Economics* 19: 51–65, replaces the fixed R&D rule of NW firms by genetic-algorithm learning and compares learning firms with fixed-rule firms [M].
- **Macro successor ("Keynes meets Schumpeter", K+S).** Dosi, Fagiolo and Roventini (2010), *Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control* 34(9): 1748–1767, doi:10.1016/j.jedc.2010.06.018 [M]: a two-sector capital-good/consumption-good economy with NW-style search in the capital-good sector and a "quasi-replicator" for consumption-good market shares, f_j(t) = f_j(t−1)·[1 + χ·(E_j(t) − Ē(t))/Ē(t)], E_j a competitiveness index (price and unfilled demand), firms below a share threshold exit [M].

### Inferences
- In the infrastructure's terms an NW model is a heuresis-style population search in which the genotype is a vector of behavioural parameters (r_in, r_im, investment parameters) and the phenotype is (A_i, K_i); fitness is not an explicit objective but the capital growth rate produced by the market, i.e. a frequency-dependent fitness (price depends on aggregate output). It is therefore a co-evolutionary search with an endogenous environment, not optimisation.
- The NW search step is a Bernoulli trial with success probability linear in R&D spending times size; this makes the process a Markov chain on (A, K) with a size-dependent mutation rate, which is what produces the Schumpeterian scale effect of large firms in the cumulative regime.
- Because no NW firm reproduces, NW selection at the firm level is "subset selection" in Hodgson and Knudsen's sense (see §4): routines spread only through imitation (horizontal transmission), and entry is the only source of new firms.

### Gaps
- The exact NW investment function, the satisficing threshold of ch. 9 and the parameter tables of ch. 12–13 could not be re-read (book not online; Andersen et al. 1996 and Valente–Andersen PDFs not fetched). An implementer must take them from the book or the NELWIN/LSD code.
- No analytical (closed-form) restatement of the full NW ch. 12 model was found; the literature treats it as a simulation model. Whether a mean-field or Fokker–Planck reduction exists for NW was not found in this session.

## 2. Metcalfe's replicator dynamics, Fisher's principle and the Price equation in economics

### Takeaway
Metcalfe's evolutionary dynamics is the replicator equation applied to market or capacity shares, with fitness a firm's growth rate of capacity, linear in its unit-cost (or efficiency) advantage over the share-weighted average; Fisher's principle then states that the share-weighted mean unit cost falls at a rate equal to the propensity to accumulate times the variance of unit costs. The Price equation, Δz̄ = Cov(w, z)/w̄ + E(w Δz)/w̄, is the exact discrete-time identity of which Fisher's principle and the replicator are special cases; it is exact for any population with a defined ancestor–descendant correspondence, and Fisher's principle is exact only when the transmission term vanishes (traits fixed within units) and fitness is linear in the trait.

### Cited Findings
- **Price equation as stated in the economics literature (Hodgson and Knudsen's appendix, after Price 1995 and Frank 1997):** for a set P with amounts ω_i of elements with properties x_i transformed into P′ with ω_i′ and x_i′, ΔX = X′ − X = Σ ω_i′x_i′ − Σ ω_i x_i, and ē·ΔX = Cov(e_i, x_i) + E(e_i Δx_i), with ω_i′ = ω_i e_i/ē, where e_i is the fitness of element i and ē the average fitness — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006, "The Nature and Units of Social Selection", JEE, appendix](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Interpretation of the two terms:** "Selection is present whenever Cov(e_i, x_i) differs significantly from zero. By contrast, a transmission effect is present whenever E(e_i Δx_i) differs significantly from zero. In social populations, a transmission effect can be referred to as an individual-level exploration or innovation effect." — [same, appendix](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Recursive (multilevel) expansion:** "it is straightforward to recursively expand the above expression to include multiple hierarchical layers of selection (use the expectation term for expansion)", and extending the equation to higher moments is noted as a further issue — [same](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Price's definitions (quoted in the same appendix):** a "corresponding set" P′ to P exists if there is a one-to-one correspondence such that each member p_i′ "is composed partly or wholly of the same material as p_i, or has been derived directly from p_i, or contains one or more replicas of p_i…"; "Selection on a set P in relation to a property x is the act or process of producing a corresponding set P′ in a way such that the amounts ω_i′ (or some function of them such as the ratios ω_i′/ω_i) are non-randomly related to the corresponding x_i values" (Price 1995: 392) — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V]. Price, G.R. (1995), "The nature of selection", *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 175: 389–396 (written c. 1971, published posthumously) [M for volume/pages beyond p. 392].
- **Metcalfe (1994), "Competition, Fisher's Principle and Increasing Returns in the Selection Process"**, *Journal of Evolutionary Economics* 4(4): 327–346: frames competition among firms by Fisher's principle, links the rate of change of the population's moments to higher moments, distinguishes kinds of increasing returns and how they shape selection, and relates Fisher's principle to Kaldor–Verdoorn growth — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joevec/v4y1994i4p327-46.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:joevec:v:4:y:1994:i:4:p:327-46) [S].
- **Replicator dynamics of shares as used in the Metcalfe tradition:** ds_i/dt = s_i(f_i − φ), φ = Σ_j s_j f_j (their Eq. 2); relative growth (ds_i/dt)/s_i = f_i − φ (Eq. 3); the same at sector level (Eq. 4); absolute values dx_k/dt = X s_k(f_k − φ) + s_k(X_t − X_{t−1}) (Eq. 5); a capacity-bounded variant dx_i/dt = x_i(f_i − φ)(1 − Σ_{i∈k} x_i / z_k) (Eq. 10). Fitness is identified with productivity, capabilities, market share or price, citing Nelson and Winter (1982), Silverberg and Lehnert (1993) and Mulder et al. (2001); the sector framework is attributed to Metcalfe, Foster and Ramlogan (2006) — [Heinrich, Yang & Dai 2020, arXiv:2005.01882](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.01882) [V].
- **Metcalfe's 1998 formulation** [M]: Metcalfe, J.S. (1998), *Evolutionary Economics and Creative Destruction* (Graz Schumpeter Lectures 1, Routledge). Firms with unit costs h_i and capacity shares s_i; capacity growth g_i = δ(p − h_i) (δ the "propensity to accumulate", p the common price); replicator ds_i/dt = s_i(g_i − g_s) with g_s = Σ s_i g_i; Fisher's principle dh_s/dt = −δ·V_s(h) when unit costs are constant, plus a term for innovation (the share-weighted mean of dh_i/dt) otherwise.
- **Derivation [D] (from the stated replicator and the linear fitness):** with g_i = δ(p − h_i), g_i − g_s = −δ(h_i − h_s), so p cancels and dh_s/dt = Σ (ds_i/dt) h_i + Σ s_i (dh_i/dt) = −δ Σ s_i(h_i − h_s)h_i + E_s(dh/dt) = −δ·Var_s(h) + E_s(dh/dt). The identity is exact for any common p (so it survives frequency dependence through the price), and becomes Fisher's "rate of change of the mean equals (minus a constant times) the variance" exactly when E_s(dh/dt) = 0.
- **Derivation [D] of the discrete Fisher result from Price:** setting x_i = e_i (trait = fitness) with e_i constant across the step gives ē·Δē = Cov(e, e) = Var(e), i.e. Δē = Var(e)/ē; in continuous time with time-invariant f, dφ/dt = Var_s(f). Exact under (a) no transmission term (traits fixed within units over the step) and (b) fitness not changing with frequency or time; otherwise only the "partial change" interpretation of Fisher's theorem holds (Price 1972, "Fisher's 'fundamental theorem' made clear", *Annals of Human Genetics* 36: 129–140 [M]).
- **Andersen and Holm (2014)**, "Directional, stabilizing, and diversifying selection: an analysis of Price's equation", *Journal of Evolutionary Economics* 24(2): 291–316, doi:10.1007/s00191-014-0350-z, use Price's equation for directional, stabilizing and diversifying selection and argue that the full definition of evolutionary processes must include selection that changes the variance without necessarily changing the mean — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00191-014-0350-z); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:joevec:v:24:y:2014:i:2:p:291-316) [S] (full text not fetched).
- **Andersen (2004)**, DRUID working paper on population thinking and Price's equation, presents Price's equation as the tool for splitting evolutionary change into component parts and argues population thinking is basic to evolutionary economics — [search summary of AAU/DRUID record](https://vbn.aau.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/87704/abstractfil.pdf) [S]; published as "Population thinking, Price's equation and the analysis of economic evolution", *Evolutionary and Institutional Economics Review* 1(1): 127–148 [M].
- **Knudsen (2004)**, "General selection theory and economic evolution: The Price equation and the replicator/interactor distinction", *Journal of Economic Methodology* 11(2): 147–173 — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/jecmet/v11y2004i2p147-173.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:jecmet:v:11:y:2004:i:2:p:147-173) [S].
- **Background claims (secondary):** Metcalfe (1998) showed that economic evolution can be written in Price's form; Frank (1998, *Foundations of Social Evolution*, Princeton) defended the equation's value although it is a tautology; Hodgson and Knudsen (2010) argue the replicator/interactor distinction makes it usable in a neo-Darwinian framework, though the equation is "a general equation about the evolutionary consequences of differential growth" — [search summary across the AAU and Springer records](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-13299-0_5) [S].
- **Confounded, augmented and constrained replicator dynamics.** Holm, Andersen and Metcalfe (2016), *Journal of Evolutionary Economics* 26: 803–822 [M], treat selection processes whose measured covariance is confounded or constrained — [Springer DOI record seen in search](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00191-016-0446-8) [S/M: title from search snippet; content not read].
- **Value chains can reverse the replicator effect** (low-fitness firms expanding): Cantner, Savin and Vannuccini (2019), *Industrial and Corporate Change* 28(3): 589– — [UAB repository PDF](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/artpub/2019/213137/indcorcha_a2019v28n3p589pp.pdf) [S].
- **Biology reference for exactness conditions:** Frank, S.A. (2012), "Natural selection. IV. The Price equation", *Journal of Evolutionary Biology* 25(6): 1002–1019, doi:10.1111/j.1420-9101.2012.02498.x [M]; Price, G.R. (1970), "Selection and covariance", *Nature* 227: 520–521, doi:10.1038/227520a0 [M]; Price (1972), "Extension of covariance selection mathematics", *Annals of Human Genetics* 35: 485–490 [M].

### Inferences
- The replicator ODE on the simplex has closed-form solutions for constant fitness, s_i(t) = s_i(0)e^{f_i t} / Σ_j s_j(0)e^{f_j t} [D], conservation Σ s_i = 1 and invariance of the simplex [D], and convergence to the vertex of maximal f [D]; these are ideal unit checks for any replicator implementation in the infrastructure (which already holds replicator-like dynamics).
- The two versions of Fisher's principle differ in what they track: Metcalfe's tracks a cost trait under a linear fitness map (and survives a common price), the biological one tracks fitness itself. An implementation should expose the Price decomposition as the general object and Fisher's principle as a theorem checked when its hypotheses are declared true.
- Andersen and Holm's point is a statement about the second moment; a Price-equation module should therefore compute the analogous identity for the variance (Price applied to (z − z̄)² with the correction for the shift of the mean), not only the mean.

- Metcalfe (1994) and (1998) were not read; the exact notation (h, δ, g_s, V_s) above is [M]. The higher-moment recursion in Metcalfe (1994) (rate of change of the k-th moment in terms of the (k+1)-th) could not be verified.
- Andersen and Holm's exact variance equations were not read (PDF refused).

## 3. Productivity decompositions read as instances of the Price equation

### Takeaway
All standard decompositions (BHC 1992, Griliches–Regev 1995, Foster–Haltiwanger–Krizan 2001, Olley–Pakes 1996, Melitz–Polanec 2015) are exact accounting identities that differ only in the reference level against which entrants and exiters are judged and in how survivors' change is split; for survivors without entry and exit, FHK is exactly the Price equation with its transmission term split into "within" and "cross". Melitz and Polanec's dynamic Olley–Pakes decomposition removes a bias by judging entrants against survivors in period 2 and exiters against survivors in period 1, and gives a closed-form test case with known answers.

### Cited Findings
- **Common starting point:** Φ_t = Σ_i s_it φ_it with s_it ≥ 0 summing to 1; ΔΦ = Φ_2 − Φ_1; φ in logs so ΔΦ is a (log) percentage change; a version in levels is in their appendix — [Melitz & Polanec, NBER WP 18182 rev. Aug 2014, Eq. 1](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Baily–Hulten–Campbell (BHC, 1992):** ΔΦ = Σ_{i∈S} s_i1(φ_i2 − φ_i1) + Σ_{i∈S}(s_i2 − s_i1)φ_i2 + Σ_{i∈E} s_i2 φ_i2 − Σ_{i∈X} s_i1 φ_i1 (Eq. 2), S, E, X survivors, entrants, exiters; the entry contribution is always positive and exit always negative whatever their productivity — [Melitz & Polanec, Eq. 2](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Griliches–Regev (GR, 1995):** reference Φ̄ = (Φ_1 + Φ_2)/2; ΔΦ = Σ_{i∈S} s̄_i(φ_i2 − φ_i1) + Σ_{i∈S}(s_i2 − s_i1)(φ̄_i − Φ̄) + Σ_{i∈E} s_i2(φ_i2 − Φ̄) − Σ_{i∈X} s_i1(φ_i1 − Φ̄) (Eq. 3), with s̄_i, φ̄_i time averages — [Melitz & Polanec, Eq. 3](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Foster–Haltiwanger–Krizan (FHK, 2001):** reference Φ_1; ΔΦ = Σ_{i∈S} s_i1(φ_i2 − φ_i1) + Σ_{i∈S}(s_i2 − s_i1)(φ_i1 − Φ_1) + Σ_{i∈S}(s_i2 − s_i1)(φ_i2 − φ_i1) + Σ_{i∈E} s_i2(φ_i2 − Φ_1) − Σ_{i∈X} s_i1(φ_i1 − Φ_1) (Eq. 4); the third term is the "cross" (covariance of share change and productivity change) — [Melitz & Polanec, Eq. 4](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Any reference works:** since shares sum to one in both periods, ΔΦ = Σ_i [s_i2(φ_i2 − Φ_REF) − s_i1(φ_i1 − Φ_REF)] for any Φ_REF; BHC is Φ_REF = 0 — [Melitz & Polanec, §2](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Olley–Pakes (OP, 1996) static decomposition:** Φ_t = φ̄_t + Σ_i(s_it − s̄_t)(φ_it − φ̄_t) = φ̄_t + cov(s_it, φ_it) (Eq. 5), φ̄_t the unweighted mean, s̄_t = 1/n_t; "cov" here is the sum without the 1/n_t factor — [Melitz & Polanec, Eq. 5](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V]. Olley & Pakes (1996), *Econometrica* 64(6): 1263–1297 [M].
- **Dynamic OP decomposition (DOPD):** with s_Gt = Σ_{i∈G} s_it and Φ_Gt = Σ_{i∈G}(s_it/s_Gt)φ_it, Φ_1 = Φ_S1 + s_X1(Φ_X1 − Φ_S1), Φ_2 = Φ_S2 + s_E2(Φ_E2 − Φ_S2), hence ΔΦ = (Φ_S2 − Φ_S1) + s_E2(Φ_E2 − Φ_S2) + s_X1(Φ_S1 − Φ_X1) = Δφ̄_S + Δcov_S + s_E2(Φ_E2 − Φ_S2) + s_X1(Φ_S1 − Φ_X1) (Eq. 6); the survivors' covariance uses shares renormalised within survivors; entry can be split further into s_E2(φ̄_E2 − φ̄_S2) + s_E2(cov_E2 − cov_S2) — [Melitz & Polanec, Eq. 6 and fn. 3–4](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V]. Published as Melitz & Polanec (2015), *RAND Journal of Economics* 46(2): 362–375 — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:randje:v:46:y:2015:i:2:p:362-375) [S].
- **Counterfactual reading:** the survivors' contribution is the aggregate productivity that would have been observed absent entry and exit; entrants raise productivity iff Φ_E2 > Φ_S2, exiters iff Φ_X1 < Φ_S1 — [Melitz & Polanec, §3](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Closed-form test case with known answers:** all firms identical with productivity growing at rate γ, plus entry and exit of representative firms. Aggregate growth is γ; DOPD assigns zero net contribution to entry and exit; GR assigns s_E γ/2 to entry and s_X γ/2 to exit; FHK assigns s_E γ to entry and zero to exit. With growth, Φ_1 and Φ̄ lie below Φ_S2, so GR and FHK over-measure entry — [Melitz & Polanec, §3](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Slovenian manufacturing 1995–2000** (AJPES firm panel, NACE 15–37; 3,867 firms in 1995, 4,580 in 2000; 2,677 survivors, 1,903 entrants, 1,191 exiters; employment 232→206 thousand; real value added 425→620 bn SIT): labour productivity with employment weights rose 50.33 log points 1995–2000, decomposed as survivors/entry/exit = GR 34.65/4.08/11.60, FHK 34.47/9.90/5.96, DOPD 44.90/−2.25/7.68; TFP with value-added weights rose 37.82, FHK 28.02/10.13/−0.35, DOPD 35.58/2.64/−0.41. Entry's contribution grows with the span to 9–10 points under FHK and 4–5 under GR while staying roughly constant under DOPD; the between-firm (reallocation) component among survivors is 2–3 times larger than in GR and FHK — [Melitz & Polanec, Tables 2–3](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **An adding-up inconsistency in the working paper's Table 3 [D, checked by code here]:** every row should sum to the aggregate change. All labour-productivity rows and all FHK and DOPD rows do (to rounding), but the GR TFP rows for 1999 (22.04 + 4.35 + 2.35 = 28.74 against 33.85) and 2000 (24.42 + 6.11 + 2.97 = 33.50 against 37.82) do not; the GR survivors' entries for those two years appear mistyped in the NBER revision of August 2014 — computed from [Melitz & Polanec, Table 3](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182). The published RAND version was not checked.
- **Measurement caveat recorded by Melitz–Polanec:** their productivity is revenue-based; Foster et al. (2008) find entrants charge lower prices, which depresses measured physical output of entrants — [Melitz & Polanec, §4](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V]. Foster, Haltiwanger & Syverson (2008), "Reallocation, firm turnover, and efficiency: selection on productivity or profitability?", *American Economic Review* 98(1): 394–425 [M].
- **Toy Price check [D, computed here]:** two firms, shares (0.5, 0.5), productivity (1, 2), fixed productivity, share growth factors w = (1.2, 0.8), so w̄ = 1 and new shares (0.6, 0.4); Φ falls from 1.5 to 1.4, and Cov_s(w/w̄, φ) = Σ s_i(w_i/w̄ − 1)(φ_i − Φ) = −0.1 exactly.

### Inferences
- **FHK is the Price equation [D].** For survivors only, write s_i2 = s_i1 w_i/w̄ (w_i the growth factor of the firm's weight, e.g. employment; w̄ the aggregate factor). Then Σ(s_i2 − s_i1)(φ_i1 − Φ_1) = Σ s_i1(w_i/w̄ − 1)φ_i1 = Cov_{s1}(w/w̄, φ_1) (the "between" term is Price's selection covariance), and Σ s_i2 Δφ_i = E_{s1}[(w/w̄)Δφ] (Price's transmission term), which FHK splits as E_{s1}[Δφ] ("within") + E_{s1}[(w/w̄ − 1)Δφ] ("cross"). BHC's survivors' split is the same identity with the cross term absorbed into "between" (end-period productivity).
- **Exit fits Price; entry does not.** Exiters have w_i = 0, so they enter the covariance term and their (undefined) Δφ is multiplied by zero. Entrants have no ancestor in Price's corresponding set, so every decomposition must add an extra entry term whose value depends on the chosen reference; DOPD's choice (survivors in the same period) is the one with the counterfactual meaning above. This is the economic counterpart of the "new entrants" problem in the Price literature.
- **OP's covariance is a level statistic, Price's covariance is a change statistic.** Δcov_S in DOPD is a change in a cross-sectional moment, not a selection term; Price's selection term is a covariance between fitness and trait. An implementation should keep the two objects apart and test that both, applied to the same panel, sum to the same ΔΦ.
- **Multilevel reading.** Sector → firm → plant decompositions are Price's recursive expansion: between-sector reallocation (structural change), between-firm within sector, within-firm learning.

### Gaps
- Original BHC (1992), GR (1995), FHK (2001) papers were not read; their forms are verified only as restated by Melitz–Polanec.
- No published paper deriving FHK formally as Price's equation with an entry term was read this session; Andersen's work and Holm–Andersen–Metcalfe (2016) are the likely places. Not verified.

## 4. Generalized Darwinism, the continuity hypothesis and micro–meso–macro; units, interactors and multilevel selection

### Takeaway
Generalized Darwinism (Hodgson and Knudsen) commits a model to explicit replicators (habits at the individual level, routines at the organisational level), interactors (individuals, firms) satisfying stated conditions, and selection in Price's general sense, with Darwinian principles necessary but not sufficient; the continuity hypothesis (Witt) commits a model only to cultural evolution having emerged from, and being constrained by, natural evolution, with novelty generation central and no obligation to replicator/interactor ontology; micro–meso–macro (Dopfer, Foster, Potts) makes the unit a rule together with its population of carriers (meso), with macro as the structure of meso populations rather than the sum of micro. Price's recursive expansion gives the formal account of multilevel selection common to all three.

### Cited Findings
- **Two kinds of selection under Price's general definition.** *Subset selection*: "one cycle of environmental interaction and elimination of entities in a population, so structured that the environmental interaction causes elimination to be differential"; formally "a contraction mapping" that "will eventually run dry of variation and come to a halt". *Generative selection*: "one cycle of replication, variation and environmental interaction so structured that the replication process causes new variation … and the environmental interaction causes replication to be differential" (after Hull et al. 2001) — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006, §3–4](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Firm shakeouts and bankruptcies are subset selection;** continuity in subset selection is "secured by enduring identity among a subset of entities", in generative selection "by the transfer of information"; a third process is needed to replenish lost variation in subset selection (Foster and Metcalfe 2001) — [same](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Neither kind implies improvement;** lock-in, path dependence and frequency dependence can confine selection to a suboptimal track; both can involve choice and intentionality — [same, abstract and §2](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Replication** requires (1) causation (the source is causally involved in producing the copy), (2) similarity (the copy can replicate and resembles the source in relevant respects), (3) inheritance (the information making the copy similar comes from that source); it is generally imperfect — [same, §4.1.1](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Interactor conditions** (from Hodgson and Knudsen 2004): for each interactor a non-empty set of component replicators with (1) shared dependence (if the interactor perishes its replicators likely perish), (2) shared organisation (replicators hosted by and interacting with the outside through the interactor), (3) relative independence of non-component replicators, (4) property-dependent replication (the interactor's properties determine the expected number of successor replicators in an environment) — [same, §4.1.1](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Social replicators:** "an emerging consensus that habits are fundamental, individual-level replicators whereas routines count as organization level replicators"; very little is well defined about social replicators, interactors and the multiple levels of social selection, posed as a research challenge — [same, §4.1.2 and abstract](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Lamarckism:** their definitions "do not in principle exclude the 'Lamarckian' possibility of the inheritance of acquired characters", and the applicability of the label is appraised critically elsewhere — [same, fn. 7](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V]; that appraisal is Hodgson & Knudsen (2006), "Dismantling Lamarckism: why descriptions of socio-economic evolution as Lamarckian are misleading", *JEE* 16(4): 343–366 [M].
- **Darwinian principles necessary but never sufficient** for complex evolving population systems (Hodgson and Knudsen's defence) — [search summary of the generalized-Darwinism debate](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1069069/63954_299266.pdf) [S]. Book: Hodgson & Knudsen (2010), *Darwin's Conjecture: The Search for General Principles of Social and Economic Evolution*, University of Chicago Press [M].
- **Continuity hypothesis:** cultural evolution is ontologically continuous with natural evolution (coined by Witt, 1999, 2003); "natural evolution … has shaped the ground, and still defines the constraints, for man-made, or cultural, evolution" (Witt 2004); critics of generalized Darwinism "limit their commitment to ontological continuity", and the disagreement is whether Darwinian evolution literally occurs in the social domain — [THEORIA article](https://ojs.ehu.eus/index.php/THEORIA/article/download/429/515/709); [CORE PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/159144486.pdf) [S]. Witt (2008), "What is specific about evolutionary economics?", *JEE* 18(5): 547–575 [M].
- **Micro–meso–macro:** Dopfer, Foster & Potts (2004), "Micro-meso-macro", *JEE* 14(3): 263–279, doi:10.1007/s00191-004-0193-0: the economic system is a population of rules, a structure of rules and a process of rules; a meso unit is a rule plus its population of actualisations; micro is the carriers of rules and the systems they organise; macro is the population structure of systems of meso; summing micro to macro is rejected — [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/joevec/v14y2004i3p263-279.html); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:spr:joevec:v:14:y:2004:i:3:p:263-279) [S]. The meso trajectory in three phases (origination, adoption/diffusion, retention) [M].
- **Multilevel Price decomposition [D]** (by the recursive expansion stated in [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf)): with groups k (weights q_k), members i in k, group mean fitness W_k and group mean trait Z_k, w̄Δz̄ = Cov_k(W_k, Z_k) + E_k[Cov_{i|k}(w_ik, z_ik)] + E_k[E_{i|k}(w_ik Δz_ik)]: between-group selection, within-group selection, within-member transmission. Original: Price (1972), *Annals of Human Genetics* 35: 485–490 [M]; Hamilton (1975) [M].

### Inferences
- **What each framework commits a model to:**
  - *Generalized Darwinism:* explicit types for replicators and interactors with the four interactor conditions checkable on a model's data structures; a declared correspondence map (who descends from whom) so that Price's terms are defined; selection at several declared levels; extra mechanisms (learning, intentional choice) allowed but kept outside the Darwinian core.
  - *Continuity hypothesis:* no replicator requirement; instead a model of novelty generation and of behavioural dispositions with a biological basis (needs, conditioned and cognitive learning); evolution as change of a population's knowledge and preferences, not necessarily by copying.
  - *Micro–meso–macro:* the state is a set of rules each with its carrier population; dynamics is a meso trajectory per rule (origination → diffusion → retention); macro is a structure (coordination among meso units), so aggregation needs an explicit structure, not a sum.
- The three are compatible at the level of the Price identity: any of them, once given a correspondence map and a weight, yields a Price decomposition; they differ in what they call the unit and what counts as inheritance.
- For firms (no offspring) the dominant firm-level process is subset selection; routines can still undergo generative selection through imitation and spinoffs, which is why the unit-of-selection question recurs.

### Gaps
- Knudsen (2004) and Hodgson & Knudsen (2010) were not read; their formal statement of the replicator/interactor Price equation (e.g. how replicator weights within interactors enter) is unverified.
- Dopfer–Foster–Potts full text not read; no formal (equational) version of micro–meso–macro was found; the framework is ontological, and its formalisation is an open item.

## 5. Schumpeterian competition and creative destruction in formal models; NK landscapes for technological search

### Takeaway
The formal Schumpeterian models are of three kinds: analytic population models of technology distributions (Iwai: logistic imitation plus Poisson innovation converging to a "statistical equilibrium of technological disequilibria"), simulation models of industry dynamics (Winter 1984's regimes; Silverberg–Dosi–Orsenigo's self-organising diffusion with learning-by-using and replicator selection), and analytic life-cycle models (Klepper 1996: scale-dependent returns to process R&D generating shakeouts). NK landscapes (Kauffman; Levinthal 1997 in management) give a tunable model of search with interdependent choices whose checkable properties are known (K = 0 single peak; random landscape local-optimum probability 1/(N+1); NP-completeness, and PLS-completeness for K > 1).

### Cited Findings
- **Iwai (1984a)**, "Schumpeterian dynamics: An evolutionary model of innovation and imitation", *JEBO* 5(2): 159–190 (Cowles DP 602, 1981); **Iwai (1984b)**, "Schumpeterian dynamics, part II: Technological progress, firm growth and 'economic selection'", *JEBO* 5(3–4): 321–351 — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jeborg:v:5:y:1984:i:2:p:159-190); [Cowles DP](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cwl/cwldpp/602.html) [S].
- **Iwai's long-run result:** the economy converges not to a uniform-technology equilibrium but to "a statistical equilibrium of technological disequilibria" that keeps a dispersion of productivities in a statistically stable form, so above-normal profits persist; at the macro level the model behaves like a neoclassical growth model, which Iwai uses to criticise growth accounting's split into movement along and shift of an aggregate production function — [Iwai 1998, CIRJE-F-30 abstract](https://www.cirje.e.u-tokyo.ac.jp/research/dp/98/98cf30ab.htm) [V abstract]. Simulations with stochastic innovation arrival typically give long oscillations in average productivity growth — [search summary of a reconsideration paper](https://bunkyo.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/3468/files/BKSJ170008.pdf) [S].
- **Iwai's mechanics** [M]: technologies indexed by unit cost; s(c, t) the share of capacity using techniques with cost ≤ c; imitation makes s follow a logistic law ds/dt = μ s(1 − s) for each cost level (adoption proportional to contact with better-practice capacity); innovation arrives as a Poisson process at rate ν per unit capacity, moving the best-practice frontier; capital accumulation by technique adds the "economic selection" of part II. The closed-form logistic solution s(t) = 1/(1 + ((1 − s_0)/s_0)e^{−μt}) [D] is a natural check. The exact functional form of Iwai's statistical-equilibrium distribution was not verified (Gap).
- **Winter (1984)**, "Schumpeterian competition in alternative technological regimes", *JEBO* 5(3–4): 287–320, doi:10.1016/0167-2681(84)90004-0 [M for DOI] — citation confirmed by [Breschi–Malerba–Orsenigo (2000) and related regime literature](https://didattica.unibocconi.it/mypage/upload/48944_20101220_021501_BRESCHI-MALERBA-ORSENIGO00.PDF) [S]. A regime is a combination of technological opportunity, appropriability, cumulativeness and the knowledge base; the "entrepreneurial" pattern (innovation by entrants, "creative destruction") contrasts with the "routinized" pattern of "creative accumulation" by incumbents consolidating capabilities — [same](https://didattica.unibocconi.it/mypage/upload/48944_20101220_021501_BRESCHI-MALERBA-ORSENIGO00.PDF); [Springer JEE 2021 summary](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00191-021-00735-6) [S]. In Winter's simulations the entrepreneurial regime has innovation drawn largely by entrants and higher turbulence; the routinized regime more concentration [M].
- **Silverberg, Dosi & Orsenigo (1988)**, "Innovation, diversity and diffusion: a self-organisation model", *Economic Journal* 98(393): 1032–1054 [M; not confirmed by search]: firms choose between an old and a new technology vintage; the new one improves with cumulative use (learning-by-using, partly a public spillover); market shares evolve by a replicator in competitiveness (price and delivery delay); diffusion paths are logistic-like and emerge without equilibrium assumptions; diversity of firms' expectations/strategies matters (early adopters bear learning costs that later adopters free-ride on) [M].
- **Klepper (1996)**, "Entry, exit, growth, and innovation over the product life cycle", *American Economic Review* 86(3): 562–583 — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:86:y:1996:i:3:p:562-83); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v86y1996i3p562-83.html) [S]: summarises regularities from birth to maturity of technologically progressive industries and develops a model in which firm size conditions the returns to innovation; as industries age firms concentrate on process innovation, and the number of firms and the rate and diversity of product innovation eventually wither; the regularities are "a composite picture" — [same records and secondary summaries](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v86y1996i3p562-83.html) [S]. The shakeout pattern (number of producers peaks, then falls and stabilises lower) is from Gort & Klepper (1982), *Economic Journal* 92(367): 630–653 [M]; entry and exit rates depend on the stage of the market's cycle (Agarwal & Gort 1996) — [Agarwal & Gort 1996 PDF](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~rajshree/research/2%20Agarwal,%20Gort%20-%201996.pdf) [S].
- **Klepper's model mechanics** [M]: potential entrants with heterogeneous innovative expertise; each firm's process R&D lowers unit cost in proportion to its output (cost spreading), product innovation adds demand; growth limited by convex adjustment costs; entry stops when expected profit of an entrant reaches zero; the model's propositions (shakeout; earlier cohorts larger and longer-lived; larger firms devote relatively more to process R&D; diversity of product innovation declines) are derived analytically under its assumptions.
- **NK model definition:** fitness F(S) = Σ_i f̃_i(S) with f̃_i(S) = f_i(S_i, S_{k_i1}, …, S_{k_iK}), each contribution depending on locus i and K other loci, contributions often drawn at random; K = 0 gives a smooth landscape with an easily found global optimum; increasing K increases ruggedness via frustration; the SK and infinite-range models are NK models with K = N − 1 — [Wikipedia, NK model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NK_model) [S]. (Many presentations, including Kauffman's, normalise F by 1/N [M].)
- **Complexity:** finding global optima in the NK model is NP-complete (Weinberger 1996, SFI WP 96-02-003); for K > 1 finding even local optima is PLS-complete (Kaznatcheev 2019, *Genetics* 212(1): 245–265, doi:10.1534/genetics.119.302000); Weinberger's (1991) analytical estimate of the number of local optima was later shown to be flawed — [Wikipedia, NK model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NK_model) [S].
- **Levinthal (1997)**, "Adaptation on Rugged Landscapes", *Management Science* 43(7): 934–950, doi:10.1287/mnsc.43.7.934, brought the NK model into organisation theory: an organisation searches by changing its own characteristics, with peaks as profit — [Wikipedia, NK model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NK_model) [S]. Content [M]: a population of organisations (N = 10, K varied) adapting by local search (one-attribute changes) and by "long jumps" (reorientation, many attributes at once), with selection replacing failing organisations; with K = 0 all converge to the single peak; with high K organisations stall on distinct local peaks, so heterogeneity persists and depends on initial position (imprinting, path dependence); environmental change (re-drawn contributions) is more lethal and reorientation less effective when attributes are tightly coupled.
- **Successors on technology and strategy landscapes** [M]: Kauffman, Lobo & Macready (2000), "Optimal search on a technology landscape", *JEBO* 43(2): 141–166; Rivkin (2000), "Imitation of complex strategies", *Management Science* 46(6): 824–844; Gavetti & Levinthal (2000), "Looking forward and looking backward", *Administrative Science Quarterly* 45(1): 113–137; Ethiraj & Levinthal (2004), "Modularity and innovation in complex systems", *Management Science* 50(2): 159–173; Fleming & Sorenson (2001), "Technology as a complex adaptive system", *Research Policy* 30(7): 1019–1039 (an empirical NK reading of patents); Frenken (2006), *Innovation, Evolution and Complexity Theory*, Elgar.

### Inferences
- **Checks with known answers for NK [D]:** with i.i.d. continuous random fitness (the uncorrelated case K = N − 1), a given point is a local maximum among its N one-bit neighbours with probability 1/(N + 1), so the expected number of local optima is 2^N/(N + 1); with K = 0 and continuous contributions there is a unique optimum, reached by any adaptive walk in at most N improving steps (expected N/2 from a uniform random start). These are exact and suit Monte Carlo tests of a landscape generator.
- Iwai's logistic and Klepper's propositions are analytical; Winter (1984), SDO (1988) and Levinthal (1997) are simulation findings whose robustness to parameters is a matter of replication.
- In the infrastructure, NK landscapes belong with heuresis's benchmarks (landscapes with known properties), and organisational search on them is an instance of its local search and quality-diversity machinery; the economics sub-domain would add only the economic reading (attributes as routines or policies, fitness as profit) and the population-with-selection wrapper.

### Gaps
- Iwai's papers, Winter (1984), SDO (1988), Klepper (1996) and Levinthal (1997) full texts were not read; their equations are [M]. In particular the functional form of Iwai's statistical-equilibrium distribution and Klepper's exact assumptions and propositions need checking at the source.
- No 2020–2026 review specifically restating these models in a common formalism was found in this session.

## 6. Theorems versus simulation findings; open problems and critiques to 2026

### Takeaway
The exact, checkable results are accounting and dynamical identities (Price's equation and its multilevel expansion; Fisher's principle under stated conditions; the replicator's closed form; all productivity decompositions adding up; logistic diffusion; NK combinatorial facts and complexity) plus analytical propositions of particular models (Iwai, Klepper). Almost everything about firms' behaviour in NW, Winter, SDO, K+S and Levinthal is simulation evidence. The recorded open problems are the unit of selection for firms (which do not reproduce), the meaning of "Lamarckian" inheritance, the identification of selection from data (a covariance is not a cause; revenue productivity confounds prices with efficiency; decompositions depend on the reference level), and the documentation and replication of simulation models.

### Cited Findings
- **Price's equation is a tautology yet useful** (Frank 1998's defence) and is "not a Darwinian construction, but a general equation about the evolutionary consequences of differential growth" — [search summary of Hodgson & Knudsen 2010 and Andersen records](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-319-13299-0_5) [S].
- **Selection is always a causal claim;** the covariance term identifies selection only when it "differs significantly from zero" and the causes of differential elimination must be supplied separately — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006, §3 and appendix](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Subset selection runs dry;** firm populations without replication need a separate source of variation (entry, imitation) — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Decompositions are identities whose components depend on the reference;** the entry/exit bias in GR and FHK reached up to 10 percentage points over five years in Slovenia — [Melitz & Polanec](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Revenue-based productivity confounds selection on efficiency with selection on prices** (entrants' lower prices) — [Melitz & Polanec, §4, citing Foster et al. 2008](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- **Confounded and constrained replicator dynamics** as a measurement problem: Holm, Andersen & Metcalfe (2016) — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00191-016-0446-8) [S/M]; value chains reversing the replicator effect — [Cantner, Savin & Vannuccini 2019](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/artpub/2019/213137/indcorcha_a2019v28n3p589pp.pdf) [S].
- **Undocumented simulation models:** NW's programs were never fully documented — [Andersen et al. 1996](https://ideas.repec.org/p/aal/abbswp/96-5.html) [S]; replications of other ABMs match qualitatively but only partly quantitatively because of unstated assumptions (Radax & Rengs 2009, on Axelrod's demographic prisoner's dilemma) — [MPRA 14419](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/14419/1/MPRA_paper_14419.pdf) [S].
- **Validation of agent-based evolutionary models:** Fagiolo, Moneta & Windrum (2007), "A critical guide to empirical validation of agent-based models in economics", *Computational Economics* 30: 195–226 [M].
- **Lamarckism:** definitions of replication and interaction do not exclude inheritance of acquired characters; the label itself is contested — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006, fn. 7](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- **Reviews and syntheses** [M]: Dosi & Nelson (1994), "An introduction to evolutionary theories in economics", *JEE* 4(3): 153–172; Dosi & Nelson (2010), "Technical change and industrial dynamics as evolutionary processes", *Handbook of the Economics of Innovation* vol. 1: 51–127; Dosi (2023), *The Foundations of Complex Evolving Economies: Part One: Innovation, Organization, and Industrial Dynamics*, Oxford University Press; Metcalfe, Foster & Ramlogan (2006), "Adaptive economic growth", *Cambridge Journal of Economics* 30(1): 7–32.

### Inferences
- An implementation should mark each result by kind: *identity* (holds for any data; a failing check is a bug), *theorem under hypotheses* (holds when declared conditions hold; the module should check the conditions), *model proposition* (holds in a stated model; checked by simulation against the analytic value), *simulation finding* (a distribution over runs; reported with its spread and seeds). This matches the infrastructure's habit of verdicts with scope.
- The identification problem suggests that a Price module should report its terms as descriptive, and that any claim of "selection on productivity" needs a causal design (peira) or a structural model; the decomposition alone cannot say whether shares moved because of productivity or because of demand or prices.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 review addressing these critiques specifically was located in this session (searches returned older literature); whether Dosi (2023) or later work resolves the unit-of-selection problem formally is unverified.
- The "Part Two" of Dosi's *Foundations* (macro) and any 2025–2026 formal restatements were not checked.

## 7. What an implementation would need

### Takeaway
A minimal evolutionary-economics sub-domain needs six formal objects (a population with weights and a correspondence map; the Price decomposition, single- and multilevel; replicator dynamics in continuous and discrete form; the productivity decompositions; parametrised industry models (NW, Iwai, Klepper, SDO) as Markov processes over firm states; NK landscapes as search environments), a set of checks with known answers, and firm-level panels as data. Most search, selection, genealogy, replicator and inequality machinery already exists in the infrastructure's foundations and should be reused, not rewritten.

### Cited Findings
- Exact forms for the decompositions and the closed-form entry/exit test case — [Melitz & Polanec](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18182) [V].
- The Price equation and its recursive multilevel expansion — [Hodgson & Knudsen 2006, appendix](https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/81/1/900698.pdf) [V].
- Replicator equations for shares and the capacity-bounded variant — [Heinrich, Yang & Dai 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2005.01882) [V].
- NK definitions and complexity — [Wikipedia, NK model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NK_model) [S].

### Inferences
- **Minimal formal objects.**
  1. *Population with correspondence:* units i with weight ω_i (output, employment, capacity), trait vector z_i, period-to-period correspondence (ancestor map; null ancestor for entrants; null descendant for exiters); optionally group membership at several levels (sector → firm → plant; interactor → replicators).
  2. *Price decomposition:* selection covariance and transmission expectation, the multilevel expansion, the variance (second-moment) version; entry handled by an explicit, declared reference term.
  3. *Replicator dynamics:* continuous ds_i/dt = s_i(f_i − f̄); discrete s_i′ = s_i f_i/f̄; Metcalfe's linear-fitness form g_i = δ(p − h_i); the quasi-replicator of K+S with exit threshold; the capacity-bounded variant.
  4. *Productivity decompositions:* BHC, GR, FHK, OP, DOPD (logs and levels), each returning components that sum to ΔΦ.
  5. *Industry models as Markov processes:* NW ch. 12 (Bernoulli search with probability ∝ R&D × capital; imitation of best practice; science-based or cumulative innovation draws; demand curve; investment rule; entry), Iwai (logistic imitation, Poisson innovation), Klepper (cohorts, process R&D with cost spreading, adjustment costs, zero-profit entry), SDO (vintages, learning-by-using, replicator in competitiveness); each with declared parameters and seeds, run on the existing agent-based and Markov machinery, with genealogies recorded.
  6. *Search environments:* NK landscapes (and their variants) for organisational search, reusing the search foundation's landscape generators and local search; the economic layer adds firms as searchers subject to market selection.
- **Checks with known answers.**
  - Price identity holds to rounding on random populations; multilevel expansion equals the single-level total; toy case: shares (0.5, 0.5), φ (1, 2), w (1.2, 0.8) gives ΔΦ = Cov = −0.1 [D].
  - Fisher: discrete Δē = Var(e)/ē for x = e fixed; continuous dφ/dt = Var(f) for constant f; Metcalfe's dh_s/dt = −δ Var_s(h) for constant h_i, independent of the common price p [D].
  - Replicator closed form s_i(t) = s_i(0)e^{f_i t}/Σ_j s_j(0)e^{f_j t}; simplex invariance; convergence to argmax f [D].
  - Decompositions: every method sums to ΔΦ; Melitz–Polanec's representative-firm case (growth γ; entry/exit contributions 0 under DOPD, s_Eγ/2 and s_Xγ/2 under GR, s_Eγ and 0 under FHK) [V]; FHK survivors' terms equal Price's covariance plus the split transmission term [D]; the Slovenian table's labour-productivity rows reproduce exactly from group aggregates (the GR TFP 1999–2000 rows do not add up in the NBER revision, a useful demonstration that an adding-up check catches transcription errors) [D].
  - Logistic diffusion closed form s(t) = 1/(1 + ((1 − s_0)/s_0)e^{−μt}) [D].
  - NK: K = 0 unique optimum reached within N improving steps; uncorrelated landscape: local-optimum probability 1/(N + 1), expected count 2^N/(N + 1) [D].
  - Subset selection without entry or imitation: trait variance non-increasing and the population contracting to a single type (Hodgson–Knudsen's contraction) [V statement; numeric check D].
- **Data a model would consume.**
  - Firm- or plant-level panels: identifiers, years, output or value added, employment, capital, intermediate inputs, prices or deflators, entry and exit dates (as in the Slovenian AJPES panel or national business registers); productivity measures (labour productivity, TFP from production-function residuals) with the share weights matching them.
  - For NW/K+S calibration: demand elasticity, cost per unit of capital, R&D intensities, depreciation, leverage, distributions of productivity growth.
  - For Klepper: counts of entrants, exits and incumbents by year and cohort for products (Gort–Klepper-style series), firm sizes and survival by cohort.
  - For Metcalfe-type sector dynamics: sector shares of output and employment over time (input–output and national accounts already in the infrastructure).
  - For NK applications: none (synthetic), or patent citation/subclass co-occurrence data for empirical interdependence (Fleming & Sorenson 2001) [M].

### Gaps
- Exact NW, Iwai, Klepper and SDO equations must be read from the primary sources before implementation; these notes give their structure, not verified formulas.
- No open, documented reference implementation was verified this session beyond the existence of Andersen's NELWIN and the LSD Nelson–Winter models; their availability and licences need checking.
