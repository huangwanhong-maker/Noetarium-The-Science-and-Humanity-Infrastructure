## Deterministic Dynamics, Their Revision Protocols, and Their Stability Results

### Takeaway
Every deterministic dynamic in Sandholm's framework is the mean dynamic of a revision protocol ρ_ij(π, x) (a conditional switch rate), and the five canonical ones (replicator, best response, logit, BNN, Smith) come from five protocols with exactly stated formulas. Two incentive properties, positive correlation (PC) and Nash stationarity (NS), organise the convergence theory: in potential games any PC dynamic ascends the potential; in contractive (stable) games each canonical dynamic has an explicit Lyapunov function; outside these classes cycling, chaos and survival of strictly dominated strategies are generic, and Rock–Paper–Scissors in its standard, good and bad forms supplies closed-form test cases.

### Cited Findings

**The general construction**
- Mean dynamic of a revision protocol (Sandholm 2015, eq. 13.3): ẋ_i = Σ_j x_j ρ_ji(F(x), x) − x_i Σ_j ρ_ij(F(x), x). "Every well-behaved revision protocol implicitly defines a deterministic evolutionary dynamic"; a deterministic evolutionary dynamic is a map from games F to Lipschitz vector fields V on the simplex with V(x) ∈ TX(x) [V] ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf), §13.3–13.4.1).
- Finite-horizon deterministic approximation (Theorem 13.4, Kurtz 1970, J. Appl. Probab. 7:49–58): if V^F is Lipschitz and X^N_0 → x_0, then for all T < ∞ and ε > 0, lim_{N→∞} P(sup_{t∈[0,T]} |X^N_t − x_t| < ε) = 1. Benaïm and Weibull (2003, Econometrica 71:873–903) prove the rate is exponential in N; Roth and Sandholm (2013) extend it to differential inclusions such as the best response dynamic. The theorem "cannot be extended to an infinite horizon result" (a full-support process visits every state infinitely often) [V] ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf), §13.3.4).
- Incentive properties (§13.4.2): Positive correlation [PC]: V^F(x) ≠ 0 ⇒ V^F(x)′F(x) > 0, equivalently a positive covariance between growth rates and payoffs under the uniform distribution on strategies, Cov = (1/n)V′F. Nash stationarity [NS]: V^F(x) = 0 ⇔ x ∈ NE(F). Proposition 13.1: PC implies every Nash equilibrium is a rest point [V] ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf)).

**The five basic protocols and their dynamics (Sandholm 2015, Table 13.1)** [V] ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf)). Notation: F̂_i(x) = F_i(x) − F̄(x) is the excess payoff, F̄ = Σ x_i F_i, M(π) = argmax_{y∈X} y′π.

| Protocol ρ_ij | Mean dynamic | Name |
|---|---|---|
| x_j[π_j − π_i]_+ (pairwise proportional imitation) | ẋ_i = x_i F̂_i(x) | Replicator |
| ρ_i· = M(π) | ẋ ∈ M(F(x)) − x | Best response |
| exp(η^{-1}π_j)/Σ_k exp(η^{-1}π_k) | ẋ_i = exp(η^{-1}F_i)/Σ_k exp(η^{-1}F_k) − x_i | Logit |
| [π_j − Σ_k x_k π_k]_+ (excess payoff) | ẋ_i = [F̂_i]_+ − x_i Σ_j [F̂_j]_+ | BNN (Brown–von Neumann–Nash) |
| [π_j − π_i]_+ (pairwise comparison) | ẋ_i = Σ_j x_j[F_i − F_j]_+ − x_i Σ_j [F_j − F_i]_+ | Smith |

- Other members named in the chapter [V]: the projection dynamic ẋ = ΦF(x) on int(X) ("follows the payoff vectors to the greatest extent possible", eq. 13.12); the Maynard Smith replicator ẋ_i = x_i F̂_i(x)/F̄(x) (imitation of success with repeated sampling, w(π_j) = π_j, positive payoffs; differs from the replicator only by speed in one population, but has different stability properties with several populations); the imitative logit dynamic (w(π_j) = exp(η^{-1}π_j)); the replicator also follows from linear "imitation driven by dissatisfaction" ρ_ij = (K − π_i)x_j and linear "imitation of success" ρ_ij = x_j(π_j − K) ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf), Examples 13.8–13.10, Table 13.2).
- General imitative protocol (eq. 13.6): ρ_ij = x_j r_ij(π, x) with "net monotonicity of conditional imitation rates" π_j ≥ π_i ⇔ [r_kj − r_jk ≥ r_ki − r_ik for all i, j, k]; the dynamic is ẋ_i = x_i Σ_j x_j (r_ji − r_ij) (eq. 13.7); unused strategies stay unused, so every face of the simplex is invariant [V] (same source).
- Excess payoff dynamics in general: ẋ_i = τ_i(F̂(x)) − x_i Σ_j τ_j(F̂(x)) (eq. 13.26); Sandholm (2005a) shows all excess payoff dynamics satisfy PC and NS. Pairwise comparison protocols satisfy sign preservation sgn(ρ_ij) = sgn([π_j − π_i]_+) (eq. 13.29) and satisfy PC and NS (Smith 1984; Sandholm 2010b). Hybrid protocols mixing imitation with consideration of unused strategies also satisfy both: "It is thus not imitation per se, but rather the exclusive use of imitation, that allows non-Nash rest points to exist" [V] (same source, §13.5.3).
- Origins [V] (same source): BNN introduced by Brown and von Neumann (1950) for symmetric zero-sum games, used by Nash (1951) in a discrete-time form to prove existence, reintroduced by Skyrms (1990), Swinkels (1993), Weibull (1996), Hofbauer (2000); the Smith dynamic introduced by Smith (1984, Transportation Science 18:245–252) for drivers in highway networks; the replicator by Taylor and Jonker (1978, Math. Biosci. 40:145–156), equivalent after a change of variables to Lotka–Volterra (Hofbauer 1981).
- Properties of the families (Table 13.3) [V]:

| Family | Example | Continuity | Data requirement | PC | NS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Imitation | Replicator | Yes | Weak | Yes | No |
| Optimisation | Best response | No | Moderate | Yes | Yes |
| Perturbed optimisation | Logit | Yes | Moderate | Approx. | Approx. |
| Excess payoff | BNN | Yes | Strong | Yes | Yes |
| Pairwise comparison | Smith | Yes | Weak | Yes | Yes |

- Perturbed best response dynamics ẋ = M̃^v(F(x)) − x (eq. 13.23) have rest points at perturbed equilibria (fixed points of M̃^v ∘ F); logit is the case v(y) = η Σ y_j log y_j, or i.i.d. double-exponential payoff shocks [V] (same source, §13.5.2.3).

**Potential games** [V] ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf), §13.6)
- F is a (full) potential game if F ≡ ∇f (externality symmetry); congestion games are potential games (Monderer and Shapley 1996, GEB 14:124–143 [V ref]; Beckmann, McGuire, Winsten 1956).
- Theorem 13.5: x is a Nash equilibrium iff it satisfies the KKT conditions for maximising f on X.
- Lemma 13.1 / Theorem 13.6: the potential is a strict global Lyapunov function for any PC dynamic; all ω-limit points are rest points, and Nash equilibria if NS also holds.
- Theorem 13.7: under PC and NS, x is asymptotically stable iff it is a local maximiser of f and isolated in NE(F). For perturbed best response dynamics the perturbed potential f̃ = f − v is ascended and solutions converge to perturbed equilibria.
- Strict equilibria are locally stable under any dynamic with "strong positive correlation" near them (§13.6.6).

**ESS and contractive (stable) games** [V] (same source, §13.7)
- ESS as local invader (Hofbauer, Schuster, Sigmund 1979, eq. 13.34): there is a neighbourhood O of x such that (y − x)′F(y) < 0 for all y ∈ O∖{x}.
- Contractive game (eq. 13.38): (y − x)′(F(y) − F(x)) ≤ 0 for all x, y; strictly contractive if strict for x ≠ y; conservative (null contractive) if always equal. A potential game is contractive iff f is concave. In C¹ games, contractive iff "self-defeating externalities" (DF negative semidefinite on TX). Called "stable games" in Hofbauer and Sandholm (2009, JET 144:1665–1693 [V ref]).
- Theorem 13.9: in a contractive game NE(F) ⊆ GNSS(F) (globally neutrally stable states), so NE(F) = GNSS(F) and is convex; in strictly contractive games the Nash equilibrium is a GESS.
- Lyapunov functions for the five dynamics in contractive games (Table 13.4): replicator H_{x*}(x) = Σ_{i∈supp x*} x*_i log(x*_i/x_i) with Ḣ = −(x* − x)′F(x) (Hofbauer et al. 1979; Zeeman 1980); best response G(x) = max_i F̂_i(x); logit G̃(x) = max_{y∈int X}(y′F̂(x) − η Σ y_i log y_i) + η Σ x_i log x_i; BNN Γ(x) = ½ Σ_i [F̂_i(x)]_+²; Smith Ψ(x) = ½ Σ_i Σ_j x_i [F_j(x) − F_i(x)]_+². For BNN, Γ̇ = ẋ′DF(x)ẋ − (τ(F̂)′1)(F′ẋ) ≤ 0, so NE(F) is globally asymptotically stable; the same holds for any acute and integrable excess payoff protocol (revision potential γ with τ = ∇γ). Whether other imitative dynamics converge globally in contractive games is stated as "an open question".
- Any ESS is locally asymptotically stable under the replicator; a regular ESS is linearly stable (Taylor and Jonker 1978), and Cressman (1997b) shows the linearisations of all imitative dynamics at a hyperbolic restricted equilibrium are positive multiples of one another.

**Closed-form test games** [V] (same source, Examples 13.20, 13.26, 13.27, 13.30)
- Rock–Paper–Scissors F(x) = Ax with A = [[0, −l, w], [w, 0, −l], [−l, w, 0]], w, l > 0; standard RPS w = l, good RPS w > l, bad RPS w < l; unique NE x* = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) in all cases. A + A′ has eigenvalue l − w with multiplicity 2 on TX, so standard RPS is conservative and good RPS strictly contractive (x* a regular ESS).
- Standard RPS: the replicator has closed orbits around x*; the other four dynamics converge to x* from every interior initial condition (for logit the rest point equals x* only by symmetry).
- Bad RPS (l = 2, w = 1 in the figure): replicator solutions approach a heteroclinic cycle on the boundary; the other four approach interior limit cycles whose position depends on the dynamic; the best-response cycle is a "Shapley polygon" (Gaunersdorfer and Hofbauer 1995, GEB 11:279–303 [V ref]; Shapley 1964).
- Mismatching Pennies (Jordan 1993; three players, two strategies, payoff 1 for differing from player p+1): unique NE at (½, ½, ½); almost all replicator solutions converge to a six-sided heteroclinic cycle; almost all best-response solutions converge to a six-sided Shapley polygon; time averages of replicator solutions approach the Shapley polygon.
- Hart and Mas-Colell (2003, AER 93:1830–1836 [V ref]): with "uncoupled" protocols, any hyperbolic rest point in such three-population games with a unique completely mixed equilibrium is unstable.
- Survival of strictly dominated strategies (Berger and Hofbauer 2006; Hofbauer and Sandholm 2011): Smith dynamic in "bad RPS with a feeble twin", A_d with rows (0, −2, 1, 1), (1, 0, −2, −2), (−2, 1, 0, 0), (−2−d, 1−d, −d, −d); for d = 1/10 the attractor is interior, and the dominated Twin is recurrently played by at least 10% of the population when d ≤ 0.31, 5% when d ≤ 0.47, 1% when d ≤ 0.66. General theorem: any continuous dynamic satisfying PC, NS and "innovation" (unused optimal strategies grow away from equilibrium) has games in which strictly dominated strategies survive. Imitative dynamics and best response eliminate strictly dominated strategies on interior solutions (Theorem 13.10), but this is "knife-edge" (the replicator's invariant foliation x_S/x_T = const is structurally unstable).
- Theorems on supermodular games (13.11–13.12) and iterated p-dominance (13.13, with "Young's game" where e₃ is asymptotically stable under the 2-sampling best response dynamic) are in §13.8 (located, not read in detail) [V location only].

**Hawk–Dove** [M] (Maynard Smith 1982; Weibull 1995): with resource value V and fight cost C > V, the unique ESS is the mixed state with Hawk share V/C; it is a strictly contractive (stable) game, so all five dynamics converge to it.

### Inferences
- The revision-protocol representation is the natural code abstraction: a protocol is a function (π, x) ↦ n×n nonnegative matrix; one mean-field integrator, one finite-N Markov jump simulator, and Kurtz's theorem as a cross-check between them serve every dynamic. This maps onto existing pieces: nomos's agents (logit choice), tyche's Markov chains, kinesis's flows and Lyapunov tools.
- Because PC and NS are checkable numerically (sign of V′F; rest points vs. Nash set), they can be asserted as laws of a protocol in the infrastructure's verdict style ("holds / fails at x / undecided").
- Table 13.4 gives one Lyapunov function per dynamic; monotone decrease along numerical trajectories in random contractive games is a strong, cheap regression test.

## Stochastic Dynamics, Stochastic Stability, and Equilibrium Selection

### Takeaway
With full-support (noisy) protocols, a finite population's Markov process has a unique stationary distribution; stochastically stable states are those retaining mass as noise vanishes (small-noise limit; Kandori–Mailath–Rob 1993, Young 1993) or as population grows (large-population limit; Binmore–Samuelson 1997, Blume 2003). Exact, computable results exist where the chain is reversible: two-strategy games (birth–death chains, an explicit product formula) and potential games under logit choice (a Gibbs distribution in the potential). Risk dominance is selected in 2×2 coordination games under best response with mutations, and under every noisy best response rule when payoffs are linear; Ellison's radius and modified coradius generalise this and bound waiting times.

### Cited Findings
- Kandori, M., Mailath, G.J., Rob, R. (1993), "Learning, mutation, and long run equilibria in games", *Econometrica* 61(1):29–56, DOI 10.2307/2951777: finite population, persistent mutations select "long run equilibria"; in 2×2 symmetric games with two strict equilibria the risk-dominant one is selected [S] ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ecm:emetrp:v:61:y:1993:i:1:p:29-56); [Econometric Society](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/browse/1993/01/01/learning-mutation-and-long-run-equilibria-games)).
- Young, H.P. (1993), "The evolution of conventions", *Econometrica* 61(1):57–84, DOI 10.2307/2951778: players learn a coordination game with no common knowledge, sampling from a finite memory of past play and occasionally erring; when mistakes are rare one convention is played almost all the time, identified by a general theorem on perturbed Markov processes [S] ([ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:61f174fe-a28c-4d33-a8c1-7092887a3ac6)). [M]: adaptive play with memory m and sample size s converges to a convention in weakly acyclic games when s ≤ m/(L_G + 2); stochastically stable states minimise "stochastic potential" computed on resistance trees (Freidlin–Wentzell); in 2×2 coordination games with incomplete sampling the risk-dominant convention is selected.
- Ellison, G. (2000), "Basins of attraction, long-run stochastic stability, and the speed of step-by-step evolution", *Review of Economic Studies* 67(1):17–45, DOI 10.1111/1467-937X.00119. Abstract: "long run stochastic stability" is related to the speed of evolution toward and away from a convention; evolution is faster "when it may proceed via a series of small steps between intermediate steady states"; the radius and modified coradius "characterize the long run stochastically stable set" and "bound the speed with which evolutionary change occurs"; risk dominance in 2×2 games is generalised to "½-dominant equilibria in arbitrary games"; applications include two-dimensional local interaction and cycles as stochastically stable sets [V] ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/restud/v67y2000i1p17-45..html)). [M]: the theorem states that if R(Ω) > CR*(Ω) for a union of limit sets Ω, the stochastically stable set lies in Ω and the expected waiting time to reach Ω is O(ε^{−CR*(Ω)}) as ε → 0.
- Example protocols (Sandholm AMS notes, Examples 5.1–5.2) [V] ([Sandholm AMS](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/research/ams.pdf)): best response with mutations BRM(ε): switch to the best response with probability 1 − ε, uniform at random with probability ε (two strategies: optimal with probability 1 − ε/2) (KMR 1993; Young 1993); logit ρ_ij(π) = exp(η^{-1}(π_j − π_{k*}))/Σ_k exp(η^{-1}(π_k − π_{k*})) (Blume 1993, 1997). Under BRM mistakes are independent of their cost; under logit costlier mistakes are exponentially rarer, and "predictions of infinite horizon behavior hinge on the relative probabilities of rare events".
- Birth–death stationary distribution, exact (Theorem 5.3) [V] ([Sandholm AMS](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/research/ams.pdf)): N agents, two-strategy game F, full-support protocol ρ; on X^N = {0, 1/N, ..., 1},
  μ^N_x / μ^N_0 = Π_{j=1}^{Nx} [(N − j + 1)/j] · ρ_01(F((j−1)/N), (j−1)/N) / ρ_10(F(j/N), j/N),
  normalised to sum to 1. It follows from detailed balance μ_x q_x = μ_{x−1/N} p_{x−1/N} for birth–death chains (eq. 16).
- Small-noise vs. large-population limits (Theorem 7.7) [V] (same source): define the relative cost κ̃(d) = lim_{η→0}(−η log σ_η(−d) + η log σ_η(d)), the ordinal potential I(x) = ∫_0^x κ̃(F_Δ(y)) dy with F_Δ = F_1 − F_0, and ΔI(x) = I(x) − max_y I(y). Then lim_{N→∞} lim_{η→0} max_x |(η/N) log μ^{N,η}_x − ΔI(x)| = 0 and the same with the limits reversed: the two orders agree. For BRM, I is the signum potential I_sgn(x) = ∫_0^x sgn(F_Δ(y)) dy = |x − x*| − x* in a coordination game with mixed equilibrium x*; for logit, I is the standard potential ∫_0^x F_Δ(y) dy; for probit, the quadratic potential ∫_0^x ¼⟨F_Δ(y)⟩² dy.
- Risk dominance and stochastic stability (Corollaries 7.11–7.12) [V] (same source): in a two-strategy coordination game with linear payoffs, e_i is weakly stochastically stable under every noisy best response protocol iff strategy i is weakly risk dominant (x* > ½ makes strategy 0 strictly risk dominant), and uniquely stochastically stable if strictly risk dominant. Under BRM the same holds for nonlinear payoffs; under other rules with nonlinear payoffs risk dominance is "no longer a necessary or sufficient condition"; Theorem 7.14 replaces it by a stochastic-dominance condition.
- Practical scale (same source, §5.4): with N = 100, a mutation rate ε = 10⁻⁵ is not small enough for the small-noise prediction, ε = 10⁻⁷ is closer; "in economic contexts ... the large population limit is more likely to be the relevant one" when the two limits disagree [V].
- Reversibility beyond two strategies [V] (same source, §8): "The only many-strategy evolutionary game environments known to generate reversible processes are potential games ... with agents using either the standard or imitative versions of the logit choice rule" (Blume 1993, 1997; Sandholm 2011). Otherwise stationary distributions have no closed form and one uses Freidlin–Wentzell large deviations.
- Gibbs form (Young 2015, Theorem 1 and Corollary 1.1) [V] ([Young 2015 preprint](https://www.econ2.jhu.edu/people/young/SocNormsOCT1.pdf)): for log-linear response to prior-period actions in an n-person game built from a two-person symmetric potential game ρ with weights w_ij and idiosyncratic action values v_i, the potential is ρ*(x) = Σ_{ij} w_ij ρ(x_i, x_j) + Σ_i v_i(x_i), the unique ergodic distribution is μ(x) = e^{βρ*(x)}/Σ_{x′} e^{βρ*(x′)}, and the stochastically stable states are exactly the maximisers of ρ*. Blume (1993), "The statistical mechanics of strategic interaction", *GEB* 5:387–424 [S via reference list; DOI 10.1006/game.1993.1023 M].
- Large-population limit and the mean dynamic (Sandholm 2015, §13.10.1) [V]: states retaining mass as N → ∞ lie in the Birkhoff centre of the mean dynamic (Benaïm and Weibull 2003); Benaïm (1998) excludes unstable rest points under global conditions; locating the selected component needs large deviations (Benaïm, Sandholm, Staudigl 2014).

### Inferences
- The infrastructure's Markov-chain code can compute exact stationary distributions of the finite-N process for small N and two or three strategies, and compare them with (i) Theorem 5.3's product formula, (ii) the Gibbs formula in potential games, (iii) the predicted selection (risk-dominant convention), and (iv) the rate (η/N) log μ → ΔI. ## Learning in Games and Its Fit to Experimental Data

### Takeaway
Fictitious play is the discrete counterpart of the best response dynamic, stochastic fictitious play of perturbed best response dynamics, and Cross-type reinforcement of the replicator; so the population dynamics above double as continuous-time limits of individual learning rules. Empirically, simple reinforcement (Roth–Erev) predicts long-run play in mixed-strategy games better than equilibrium; EWA nests reinforcement and belief learning and generally beats both in-sample and on holdouts; self-tuning EWA matches EWA out of sample with fewer parameters; but simulation studies (Salmon 2001) show standard econometric methods have difficulty telling learning rules apart, which limits how much any fit identifies the rule.

### Cited Findings
- Fictitious play (Brown 1949, 1951) "is essentially a discrete-time version of the best response dynamic", after a reparametrisation of time; stochastic fictitious play (Fudenberg and Kreps 1993, GEB 5:320–367) is a decreasing-step stochastic process whose expected motion is a perturbed best response dynamic, so stochastic approximation (Benaïm 1999) gives global convergence results (Fudenberg and Kreps 1993; Kaniovski and Young 1995; Benaïm and Hirsch 1999; Hofbauer and Sandholm 2002; Benaïm and Faure 2012; Perkins and Leslie 2013); the rule is ε-consistent [V] ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf), §13.10.2).
- Shapley (1964) constructed the first example of cycling under fictitious play [V] (same source, Example 13.26).
- Reinforcement and the replicator: Börgers and Sarin (1997, JET 77:1–14) show that Cross's (1973) reinforcement rule yields the replicator dynamic in the continuous-time limit; Hopkins (2002) links reinforcement learning and the logit/replicator dynamics [V] (same source, §13.10.2 and fn. 50). The convergence results for contractive games are inspired by Hart and Mas-Colell's (2001, JET 98:26–54) consistent strategies [V] (same source).
- Erev and Roth (1998), "Predicting how people play games: reinforcement learning in experimental games with unique, mixed strategy equilibria", *AER* 88(4):848–881: data from published experiments with 100 or more periods of play in games with unique mixed equilibria; models assessed both by best fit and by ex ante prediction with parameters estimated on other experiments; "even a one-parameter reinforcement learning model robustly outperforms the equilibrium predictions"; predictive power improves with forgetting and experimentation, or with probabilistic fictitious play [S] ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v88y1998i4p848-81.html); [author PDF](https://stanford.edu/%7Ealroth/papers/ENO.pdf)). [M]: the basic model updates propensities q_j(t+1) = (1 − φ)q_j(t) + E_k(j, R(x)) with experimentation ε spilling reinforcement to other strategies, and chooses j with probability q_j/Σ_k q_k; Roth and Erev (1995), *GEB* 8:164–212, is the earlier paper on intermediate-term play.
- Camerer, C. and Ho, T.-H. ### Inferences
- A learning module needs one "attraction" interface with EWA as the general case and reinforcement, weighted fictitious play and Cournot best response as parameter settings; the logit choice already present in nomos supplies the choice map. Börgers–Sarin and the fictitious-play/best-response correspondence give convergence checks against the deterministic dynamics.
- Salmon's result suggests that any fitting tool should report how well the record identifies the rule (e.g., a parameter recovery study on simulated data) before reporting which rule fits best, in keeping with the infrastructure's habit of saying what a record does not identify.

## Cultural Evolution of Norms, Conventions and Institutions

### Takeaway
The economic literature models norms and conventions as equilibria of recurrent games selected by stochastic evolutionary dynamics (Young 1993, 1998, 2015), with three qualitative signatures (punctuated equilibrium, compression, local conformity with global diversity) that have been confronted with data (Illinois share contracts; footbinding). Cultural transmission models (Cavalli-Sforza–Feldman; Boyd–Richerson; Bisin–Verdier) give replicator-like logistic dynamics; Bisin–Verdier's purposeful socialization with cultural substitution yields a globally stable heterogeneous population with a closed form. Institutional co-evolution with behaviour is studied by multilevel simulation (Bowles, Choi, Hopfensitz 2003); Aoki's comparative institutional analysis treats institutions as shared beliefs that summarise an equilibrium.

### Cited Findings

**Norms as stochastically stable equilibria (Young)**
- Young (2015), "The evolution of social norms", *Annual Review of Economics* 7:359–387, DOI 10.1146/annurev-economics-080614-115322 [V] ([preprint](https://www.econ2.jhu.edu/people/young/SocNormsOCT1.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/anr/reveco/v7y2015p359-387.html)): "Social norms are patterns of behavior that are self-enforcing at the group level: everyone wants to conform when they expect everyone else to conform"; mechanisms include coordination, fear of sanctions, signalling membership, following others; cases are bargaining, contract terms, retirement, duelling, footbinding, medical treatment and contraception.
- Updating rules in that framework [V] (same source, §4.2): best response to those matched with; uniform error (best response with probability 1 − ε, random otherwise); log-linear response; when matches are rare (duelling), a random sample from the history of the other population's actions with a trembled best response; recency by exponential discounting or a truncated memory.
- Three qualitative features [V] (same source, §4.4): persistence and tipping, "long periods in which people follow a norm ... interrupted by a burst of activity in which one norm is displaced by another (the punctuated equilibrium effect)"; the compression effect (less diverse choices than fundamentals warrant); local conformity / global diversity (non-interacting communities settle on different norms under the same influences).
- Illinois share contracts (Young and Burke 2001) [V] (same source, §7): Illinois Cooperative Extension Service survey (1995) of several thousand farms; over 98% of share contracts were 1/2–1/2, 2/5–3/5 or 1/3–2/3; the north uses 1/2–1/2, the south 1/3–2/3 or 2/5–3/5 (the boundary roughly that of the last glaciation); farms of equal soil quality in different regions operate under different shares; compression predicts an upward bias in tenants' net income on high-quality land, "confirmed by the Illinois data (Burke 2004)". Young notes identification from cross-sections is "extremely difficult" (Manski 1993; Brock and Durlauf 2001). The paper itself: Young, H.P. and Burke, M.A. (2001), "Competition and custom in economic contracts: a case study of Illinois agriculture", *AER* 91(3):559–573 [M].
- Footbinding (Mackie 1996) [V] (same source, §8.1): a norm that persisted about a thousand years "disappeared almost entirely within a single generation"; top-down edicts had failed; reformers organised "natural foot societies" whose families pledged not to bind daughters nor let sons marry bound women, shifting expectations of interacting families simultaneously; Mackie proposes this as a template for female genital cutting.
- Further norm literature listed by Young [S via reference list]: Sugden (1986) *The Economics of Rights, Cooperation and Welfare*; Skyrms (1996) *Evolution of the Social Contract*, (2004) *The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure*; Bowles (2004) *Microeconomics: Behavior, Institutions, and Evolution*, Princeton UP; Belloc and Bowles (2013) on persistence of inferior cultural-institutional conventions, AER P&P 103; Bowles and Choi (2013), "Coevolution of farming and private property during the early Holocene", PNAS 110 (pages printed as 8830–8735 in Young's list, evidently a typo; [M] 8830–8835, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1212149110); Boyd and Richerson (2002), "Group beneficial norms can spread rapidly in a structured population", JTB 215:287–296; Hwang, Naidu, Bowles (2014) on social conflict and unequal conventions ([Young 2015 preprint](https://www.econ2.jhu.edu/people/young/SocNormsOCT1.pdf)).

**Cultural transmission**
- Cavalli-Sforza and Feldman (1981) mechanism, as stated in Bisin and Verdier's survey [V] ([NBER w16512](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16512)): dichotomous trait {a, b}, share q_i; direct vertical socialization with probability d_i, otherwise oblique adoption from a random role model; P_ii = d_i + (1 − d_i)q_i, P_ij = (1 − d_i)(1 − q_i); continuous-time dynamics q̇_i = q_i(1 − q_i)(d_i − d_j) (eq. 3), "a simple version of the replicator dynamics", logistic; with exogenous d_i > d_j the trait i fixes from any q_0 ∈ (0, 1]; with d_i = d_j nothing moves. Boyd and Richerson (1985) allow exogenous frequency-dependent d_i = d(q_i).
- Bisin and Verdier (2001), "The economics of cultural transmission and the dynamics of preferences", *JET* 97(2):298–319, DOI 10.1006/jeth.2000.2678 [S] ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jetheo/v97y2001i2p298-319.html)). Model as stated in the survey [V] ([NBER w16512](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16512)): "imperfect empathy" V^{ij} = u^i(x^j) with x^j = argmax u^j, so V^{ii} ≥ V^{ij}; parents choose d_i to maximise −C(d_i) + P^{ii}V^{ii} + P^{ij}V^{ij}; with quadratic cost C(d) = d²/2, d_i = (1 − q_i)ΔV^i where ΔV^i = V^{ii} − V^{ij} is "cultural intolerance" (eq. 2); then the stationary states are 0, 1 and an interior q*, and "the culturally heterogeneous stationary state is globally stable" on (0, 1). Cultural substitution (d_i continuous, strictly decreasing in q_i, d_i(1, ·) = 0) makes homogeneous states unstable; strong cultural complementarity can drive the population to homogeneity.
- The survey itself: Bisin, A. and Verdier, T. (2010), "The economics of cultural transmission and socialization", NBER Working Paper 16512 (published in the *Handbook of Social Economics*, 2011) [V] ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16512)).
- Montgomery (2010), "Intergenerational cultural transmission as an evolutionary game", *AEJ: Micro* 2:115–136, connects the two literatures [S via Sandholm's reference list].

**Co-evolution of institutions and behaviour**
- Bowles, S., Choi, J.-K., Hopfensitz, A. (2003), "The co-evolution of individual behaviors and social institutions", *Journal of Theoretical Biology* 223:135–147, DOI 10.1016/S0022-5193(03)00060-2: agent-based simulations of a deme-structured population; group institutions spread culturally, individual behaviours are inherited; intergroup conflict can explain altruism toward unrelated group members and institutions such as food sharing, when group-beneficial behaviours impose costs on outsiders and institutions reduce their cost to individuals [S] ([ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2003JThBi.223..135B/abstract)).
- Related [M]: Choi, J.-K. and Bowles, S. (2007), "The coevolution of parochial altruism and war", *Science* 318:636–640, DOI 10.1126/science.1144237; Bowles and Gintis (2011), *A Cooperative Species*, Princeton UP; Gintis (2007), "The evolution of private property", *JEBO* 64:1–16 (property as a convention via a bourgeois strategy, after Maynard Smith's Hawk–Dove–Bourgeois); Boyd and Richerson (1985), *Culture and the Evolutionary Process*, Chicago UP.

**Institutions as equilibria (Aoki) and the commons (Ostrom)**
- Aoki (2001), *Toward a Comparative Institutional Analysis*, MIT Press: a section titled "Aspects of Institutions: Shared Beliefs, Summary Representations of Equilibrium, and Endogenous Rules of the Game"; institutions as shared beliefs about how the game is played, self-enforcing, a "summary representation" of an equilibrium that may or may not match formal law; chapter 7 elaborates this rules-in-equilibrium view [S] ([RIETI slides](https://www.rieti.go.jp/en/events/03010801/pdf/Aoki_p.pdf); [Cambridge JIE 2025 commentary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/B8153640E3B8C0568B71C48E8A42CF01/core-reader)). A 2025 commentary places Aoki within equilibrium theories of institutional change, needing comparative and historical analysis to explain which equilibrium is selected [S] (same Cambridge source).
- Ostrom (1990), *Governing the Commons*, Cambridge UP (design principles for long-enduring common-pool resource institutions), and Ostrom (2005), *Understanding Institutional Diversity*, Princeton UP (the IAD framework and the "grammar of institutions": attributes, deontic, aim, conditions, or else) [M]; not verified in this session.

### Inferences
- Bisin–Verdier with quadratic cost gives a closed form: q̇ = q(1 − q)[(1 − q)ΔV^a − qΔV^b], so q* = ΔV^a/(ΔV^a + ΔV^b), with derivative at q* equal to −q*(1 − q*)(ΔV^a + ΔV^b) < 0 (derived from the survey's equations 2–3; not printed in the extract read).
- Young's Gibbs result makes norm dynamics on networks exactly checkable for small networks: enumerate the 2^n action profiles, compute ρ*, and compare simulated long-run frequencies with e^{βρ*}.
- The three signatures (punctuated equilibrium, compression, local conformity / global diversity) are testable outputs of a simulation on a structured population (regions as weakly linked graphs), and suit the urban and social domains as empirical targets.
- Aoki's "summary representation of an equilibrium" and Ostrom's rule grammar suggest representing an institution as a declared object with two faces: a rule text (kybernesis-style declared data, or the law domain's norms) and the equilibrium beliefs that make it self-enforcing; kategoria could check the mapping between them.

### Gaps
- No formal model of institutional change in Ostrom's or Aoki's sense was read in full; the summaries above are secondary.
- Boyd–Richerson conformist transmission (frequency-dependent bias) formulas were not verified.
- Quantitative evidence on how often observed norms coincide with the stochastically stable state (beyond the cases Young reports) was not found.

## Evolutionary Approaches to Economic Policy

### Takeaway
Neo-Schumpeterian economics recasts policy as acting on variety, selection and retention in an "experimental economy" (Metcalfe 1994, 1995); later strands add directionality (Mazzucato's missions; Schot and Steinmueller's transformative frame), discovery (Hausmann and Rodrik's self-discovery; Rodrik's industrial policy as a process), and recursive revision of provisional frameworks (Sabel and Zeitlin's experimentalist governance). Formal work that treats policy itself as an evolving object exists mainly in political science (learning and diffusion, policy search on a Brownian landscape) and in adaptive management; agent-based macroeconomics uses evolutionary learning chiefly for private agents. Evidence on outcomes is thin: OECD stocktakes (2024–2025) find conventional evaluation unsuited to missions and report countries downsizing or abandoning them; adaptive management is rarely implemented as designed; the newer causal literature on industrial policy is more favourable than the older correlational one.

## What an Implementation Would Need

### Takeaway
A sub-domain (or a foundation module, since the dynamics name no object in nature) needs: revision protocols as first-class functions with mean-field and finite-N Markov realisations; the five canonical dynamics plus imitative, excess-payoff, pairwise-comparison and perturbed-best-response families; PC and NS as checkable laws; potential and contractive game tests with their Lyapunov functions; exact stationary distributions where reversibility holds and resistance-tree computation elsewhere; EWA-family learning; cultural transmission; and a representation of policy as a population of instruments under declared variation, selection and retention operators whose fitness comes from the existing policy domain's evidence and value of information.

### Cited Findings
- The pieces with exact formulas and theorems are those listed above: Table 13.1 protocols and dynamics, Table 13.3 properties, Theorems 13.6–13.7 (potential games), 13.9 and Table 13.4 (contractive games), RPS/Mismatching Pennies/feeble-twin examples ([Sandholm 2015](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/teaching/805/handbook.pdf)) [V]; Theorem 5.3 product formula, Theorem 7.7 limits, Corollaries 7.11–7.12 risk dominance ([Sandholm AMS](https://users.ssc.wisc.edu/~whs/research/ams.pdf)) [V]; Gibbs distribution in potential games ([Young 2015](https://www.econ2.jhu.edu/people/young/SocNormsOCT1.pdf)) [V]; Bisin–Verdier dynamics ([NBER w16512](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16512)) [V].

### Inferences

**Dynamics and protocols worth implementing (in order)**
1. A `protocol` interface ρ(π, x) → nonnegative n×n matrix, with multi-population support; the mean dynamic ẋ_i = Σ_j x_j ρ_ji − x_i Σ_j ρ_ij; a finite-N continuous-time Markov jump process (agents revise at Poisson rate R) built from the same protocol; and a differential-inclusion integrator for best response (or its logit approximation with small η).
2. Protocols: pairwise proportional imitation (replicator), Maynard Smith replicator, imitative logit, best response, logit (η), BNN, Smith, projection, BRM(ε), probit; the general imitative (r_ij), excess-payoff (τ) and pairwise-comparison families as parametrised classes; hybrids.
3. Game classes: normal-form matrices, population games F(x), congestion games (potential), random contractive games (DF negative semidefinite on TX), supermodular games.
4. Stochastic: exact stationary distribution by linear algebra for small state spaces; birth–death formula for two strategies; Gibbs in potential games; resistance-tree stochastic potential via minimum-weight arborescences over recurrent classes; Ellison radius/coradius; waiting-time estimation by simulation.
5. Learning: EWA with its special cases; Roth–Erev with forgetting and experimentation; stochastic fictitious play; regret matching (Hart–Mas-Colell) [M]; fitting with a parameter-recovery report.
6. Cultural transmission: Cavalli-Sforza–Feldman, Boyd–Richerson frequency dependence, Bisin–Verdier endogenous socialization; norm dynamics on networks with log-linear response (reusing nomos's agents and Kirman herding, tyche's networks).

**Checks with known answers**
- Table 13.1 identities: each protocol's mean dynamic equals the closed form (symbolic or numerical, random F and x).
- PC holds for replicator, BR, BNN, Smith (V′F > 0 off rest points, random games); NS fails for the replicator (pure states are rest points that need not be Nash) and holds for BNN and Smith.
- Standard RPS (w = l): x₁x₂x₃ is conserved along the replicator (derived: d/dt Σ log x_i = Σ_i F_i − 3F̄ = 0 since x′Ax = 0 for antisymmetric A and A's column sums vanish when w = l); equivalently H_{x*} is constant, consistent with the closed orbits in the chapter. The other four dynamics converge to (1/3, 1/3, 1/3).
- Good RPS: all five converge to x*, with the Table 13.4 Lyapunov functions decreasing. Bad RPS (l = 2, w = 1): replicator approaches the boundary heteroclinic cycle (min_i x_i → 0 along time), others an interior limit cycle.
- Feeble twin (d = 0.1, Smith): the dominated strategy's share stays above 10% recurrently (the chapter's thresholds 0.31, 0.47, 0.66 for 10%, 5%, 1%).
- Mismatching Pennies: replicator time averages approach the six-sided Shapley polygon of the best response dynamic.
- Potential games: the potential increases along every PC dynamic; asymptotically stable states equal isolated local maximisers (Theorem 13.7).
- Hawk–Dove (C > V): convergence to Hawk share V/C [M].
- Kurtz: finite-N sample paths within ε of the ODE on [0, T] with probability → 1; Benaïm–Weibull's exponential rate as a statistical check.
- Two-strategy coordination: Theorem 5.3's product equals the stationary vector of the generator; (η/N) log μ approaches ΔI; the risk-dominant state is selected (strategy 1 if x* < ½); the N = 100, ε = 10⁻⁵ vs 10⁻⁷ contrast reproduces.
- Logit in potential games: stationary distribution ∝ exp(η^{-1} × potential) on the finite grid (Blume; Young's Theorem 1), checked exactly for small N or small networks.
- Fictitious play: convergence in zero-sum and 2×2 games [M: Robinson 1951; Miyasawa 1961] and the Shapley (1964) cycle.
- Börgers–Sarin: Cross reinforcement with small step approaches the replicator path.
- EWA: δ = 0, ρ = 0 reproduces cumulative reinforcement; δ = 1, ρ = φ reproduces weighted fictitious play (exact equality of attractions up to normalisation) [M formulas].
- Bisin–Verdier: q* = ΔV^a/(ΔV^a + ΔV^b), globally attracting on (0, 1); with exogenous d_i > d_j, fixation of trait i.

**Policy as evolution, formally**
- State: a population (or portfolio) of policy instruments or programme variants k ∈ K with shares (budget weights) x_k, each carrying a theory of change and a posterior over its effect (from the policy domain's `evidence`, `transfer`, `adoption`).
- Fitness: expected net value under the posterior, optionally plus the value of information of continuing to fund it (the policy domain's EVPI/EVSI), so that selection rewards both performance and learning; this is the dual-control idea of active adaptive management in the language of revision protocols.
- Variation: new variants proposed (local discretion in experimentalist governance; Rodrik's discovery; Callander's search over a Brownian landscape with known drift and variance), as mutation operators with declared rates; directionality (missions) as a constraint set on admissible variants.
- Selection: a revision protocol over budget shares (replicator = proportional reallocation to above-average programmes; logit = soft reallocation with temperature as political inertia; Smith = pairwise comparisons by programme managers; BRM = rare random reallocation), so that PC, NS and the stability theorems transfer and say when a portfolio settles, cycles, or keeps a dominated programme alive.
- Retention and revision: the experimentalist cycle (framework, discretion, report and peer review, revision) as a loop in kybernesis's architecture, each turn sealed in archeion's journal; diffusion among jurisdictions as a network process with own vs. social learning (Volden–Ting–Carpenter), where adoption records alone may not identify which.
- Evaluation: OECD's "second-order additionality" as the difference between the portfolio's outcome under a coordinated protocol and under independent protocols for the same instruments, computed by simulation.
